## The story

He installed a water butt in under an hour with a few wonky drill bits. It rained that night and filled it completely.

That is the small end of a very large story. El Niño is simple in mechanism: the winds that normally push warm surface water west across the Pacific weaken, and the warm water slides back east. Millions of square miles, a few degrees warmer, and weather patterns rearrange worldwide.

NOAA gives this one a greater than 90% chance of becoming a very strong event, peaking through autumn and winter — and it lands on an ocean and atmosphere already warmed. The music, he says, is playing at eight or nine, and El Niño turns it past eleven.

The effects fan out unevenly. Drier in parts of Australia, Indonesia, South Africa and South Asia — drought, crop failure, fire. Wetter in parts of South America, East Africa and the southern US. Monsoons disrupted, storms relocated, harvests hit, and food prices moving in countries that never saw the weather.

In Britain: a wetter, stormier autumn, possibly a hard cold snap in January or February, and rain falling on ground baked by a long drought, which runs straight off instead of soaking in.

Nobody watching can do anything about the Pacific, which is exactly where most people stop — the planetary scale makes you feel like a rounding error. He names that feeling and refuses it, while being careful that for communities facing the worst of it, none of this is enough.

He redraws the boundary. You have no say in the event. You have every say in what it does to your ground, your water and your food.

So: a water butt. Then a member who turned half a whiskey barrel into a pond and kept it topped up through the drought, plus a small pond under a hedge that matters enormously to wildlife when everything dries out. Then one with a river on his land, waders on, building a leaky dam that holds back water and sediment and raised the water table while he waits for beavers.

And the same logic at garden scale: trees for shade, hedges against wind, plants covering the soil, deadwood and leaf litter left where they fall, no artificial turf baking like green tarmac, and enough food growing that a price shock isn't a shock.

Which is the whole argument, and it fits in one line: the event isn't yours, but the ground is — and that is where agency actually lives.

## Beliefs that shift

- *A planetary event leaves you powerless.* → A planetary event arrives on ground you control.
- *Preparing for drought is about watering.* → Preparing for drought is about catching and holding what already falls.
- *A tidy garden is a healthy garden.* → A layered, untidy garden holds water, shade and life; a bare one sheds all three.

## What we learn

- **Catch water where it falls.** A butt, a half-barrel pond, even a small pond under a hedge — that one matters enormously to wildlife in a drought.
- **Slow the water down.** One community member waded in and built a leaky dam: it holds back water and sediment and raises the water on his land.
- **Rain after drought is runoff, not relief.** Baked ground sheds it. Living soil and leaf litter take it in.
- **Layers beat lawns.** Trees for shade, hedges against wind, plants covering the soil.
- **Scale isn't the excuse.** Hundreds of acres, a small garden, or a balcony — the principles don't change.
- **Expect prices, not famine.** The likely effect is availability and cost of particular foods, not empty shelves.
