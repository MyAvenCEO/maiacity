## The story

El Niño is simple in mechanism and enormous in effect. The winds that normally push warm surface water west across the Pacific weaken, and the warm water slides back east.

Millions of square miles of ocean, a few degrees warmer, and weather patterns rearrange worldwide.

NOAA gives this one a greater than 90% chance of becoming a very strong event, peaking through autumn and winter.

What makes it different is what it lands on: an ocean and atmosphere already warmed. The music is already playing at eight or nine, and El Niño turns it past eleven.

Drier in parts of Australia, Indonesia, South Africa and South Asia — drought, crop failure, fire. Wetter in parts of South America, East Africa and the southern US. Monsoons disrupted, storms relocated.

In Britain, the Met Office expects a wetter, stormier autumn and early winter, possibly a hard cold snap in January or February. And rain after a long drought mostly runs off baked ground instead of soaking in.

Then the video turns, and stays turned: you can't change the event. You can change what it does to your home, your garden, your street.

So he installed a water butt. Under an hour, a few wonky drill bits — and it filled completely that same night.

## Beliefs that shift

- *A planetary event leaves you powerless.* → A planetary event arrives on ground you control.
- *Preparing for drought is about watering.* → Preparing for drought is about catching and holding what already falls.
- *A tidy garden is a healthy garden.* → A layered, untidy garden holds water, shade and life; a bare one sheds all three.
- *Artificial turf is low-maintenance ground.* → Artificial turf is green tarmac: it bakes in the heat and sheds the rain.
- *Food resilience is a full pantry.* → Food resilience is a season you know and a bed you planted.

## What we learn

- **Catch water where it falls.** A butt, a half-barrel pond, even a small pond under a hedge — that one matters enormously to wildlife in a drought.
- **Slow the water down.** One community member waded in and built a leaky dam: it holds back water and sediment and raises the water on his land.
- **Rain after drought is runoff, not relief.** Baked ground sheds it. Living soil and leaf litter take it in.
- **Layers beat lawns.** Trees for shade, hedges against wind, plants covering the soil.
- **Scale isn't the excuse.** Hundreds of acres, a small garden, or a balcony — the principles don't change.
- **Expect prices, not famine.** The likely effect is availability and cost of particular foods, not empty shelves.

## For maiaCITY

Let's translate this.

- **Every roof catches water.** Tanks and cisterns as standard from day one, never a retrofit.
- **Slow water across the whole site.** Leaky dams, ponds and swales that keep rain on the land instead of piping it away — the same logic as the [permaculture farm](/inspire-me/permaculture-farm-that-pays).
- **No sealed ground.** Ban artificial turf, minimise tarmac, and plant shade trees and hedges instead. Let deadwood and leaf litter stay — that's policy, not neglect.
- **Grow some of it, and eat the season.** Not self-sufficiency. A buffer, and a food culture that isn't shocked when a price moves.
- **Give every resident one visible act.** The community-challenge model: one small resilience project at a time, shared publicly.
