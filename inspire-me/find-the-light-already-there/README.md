## The story

You walk into a location with no clue where to start. Or worse, you put up every light you brought, and the room looks like a set.

That is where most people learning to light get stuck, and it is where this filmmaker starts his video. He has one approach he uses for nearly every scene he has ever lit, and it begins with a word he means differently from everyone else: motivation.

Not how pumped he is. Where the light already comes from, before anything is touched. A window, a lamp, the lights overhead.

A lamp put up anywhere it happens to fit has no reason to be there, and the eye feels it. A lamp that copies the window, from the window's side, at the window's colour, reads as the window. The viewer never asks where it came from.

So the first job is to find the main source, and the second is to put it behind the subject. Backlight gives a face its shape, and shape is depth: a flat frame starts to feel three-dimensional.

He shows it in a house his girlfriend Anna is sitting for. By day the windows behind them are the main source, and the rule is to light spaces, not faces. He sets the wide shot first. With today's cameras the daylight nearly does it alone, so he adds one daylight lamp under a giant dome, behind and to one side, from the direction of the windows. The bigger the source, the softer the light.

Then he takes light away. Black negative fill near their faces cuts the light bouncing round the room onto the shadow side, so the picture stays moody. A little haze gives the window light a body.

For the close-ups nothing new arrives. The same lamp moves in and angles to wrap the window's light across each face, giving an eye light and a soft roll-off, and the black fill comes closer.

Night is harder. The windows are dark, the room is dark, and the only source is a lamp in the corner, a practical, a light you can see in the frame.

The lamp in frame can't light a face on its own, so a film light gelled orange to the lamp's colour sits beside it, out of sight, doing the work the lamp seems to do. Outside, a daylight lamp high on a stand behind the trees plays the moon, so the window is not a black hole. Blue moon against orange lamp makes depth out of colour. A cheap LED in the foreground adds a moonlit window on the left that doesn't exist, and nobody can tell, because the moon was already set up in the shots before.

In the close-ups the orange lamp moves in to wrap his face, and the moon swings round to rim the back of his head in blue. Anna gets the same lamp turned round, a hallway lit by that same established moon, and a light wand held over her head by hand because there was no stand left.

Not one of those lights is the lamp or the moon. Every one of them is believed, because each copies a source the room already showed you.

## From the ground up

- **Light source.** Anything that gives off light: the sun through a window, a lamp, a ceiling light, the moon. Before you bring any lamps, every place already has some.
- **Motivation.** The reason a light is there. A motivated film light pretends to be a real source in the place, so it comes from that source's direction, with its softness and its colour. Lights with no motivation are what make a scene look fake.
- **The main source.** The strongest light the place already has. It decides where the other lights go.
- **Backlight.** Light from behind the subject. It draws a bright edge round the head and shoulders and lifts the person off the background, which is what makes a flat picture feel deep.
- **Soft and hard light.** A big source makes soft light with gentle shadows; a small one makes hard light with sharp shadows. A giant dome over a lamp, or a big window, makes it soft.
- **Negative fill.** A black cloth or board near the shadow side of a face. In a room, light bounces off every wall and fills the shadows in; the black soaks that up and brings the shape and the mood back. It takes light away instead of adding it.
- **Haze.** A thin mist in the air. It makes beams of light visible, so a window throws rays you can see, and the background gains depth.
- **Practical.** A light you can see in the frame, like a table lamp. It shows where light comes from, but it is rarely bright or soft enough to light a face, so a hidden film light does that from the same direction.
- **Colour temperature and gels.** Light has a colour: a lamp bulb is orange (warm), daylight and moonlight read blue (cool). A CTO gel, colour temperature orange, is a sheet over a daylight lamp that turns it the colour of a bulb, so it can pass for the lamp. Warm inside against cool outside gives depth from colour alone.

## The steps

1. Walk into the place with every lamp off and look: where is the light coming from? Windows, a lamp, overhead lights. *(motivation)*
2. Pick the strongest one. That is your main source, and every light you add copies it. *(the main source)*
3. Turn the scene so the main source sits behind your subject. *(backlight)*
4. Set the wide shot first and light the space, not the faces. With daylight, often one big soft lamp from the window's side is enough. *(soft and hard light)*
5. Hang black negative fill on the shadow side of each face to stop light bouncing in. *(negative fill)*
6. Add a little haze so the window light and the background gain volume. *(haze)*
7. At night, find the practical in frame and place a hidden lamp beside it, gelled to the same warm colour, aimed at the face. *(practical, colour temperature and gels)*
8. Give the dark window a reason to glow: a cool lamp outside, high, playing the moon. *(colour temperature and gels)*
9. Move in for the close-ups. Bring the same lights closer and angle them so they wrap round the face, and swing the moon round to rim the back of the head. *(backlight)*
10. Any extra light you need must come from a source you have already shown, the window, the lamp or the moon, even if it doesn't really exist. *(motivation)*

## Beliefs that shift

- *Good lighting means more lights.* → Good lighting means fewer lights with a reason: each one copies a source the place already has.
- *Light the face so you can see it.* → Put the main source behind the face for shape and depth, and take light away from the shadow side with black.
- *A lamp in frame lights the scene.* → The lamp in frame only tells the story; a hidden light of the same colour, from the same side, does the lighting.

## What we learn

- **Look before you light.** With every lamp off, the place tells you where the light belongs. Our gaffer skill makes the same law its first: every source has a reason in the world.
- **Light spaces, not faces.** The wide shot sets up the light; the close-ups only bring the same lamps closer.
- **Subtract before you add.** Negative fill on the shadow cheek does more for a moody face in a small white room than another lamp, and Samuel's 14 m² room bounces light off every wall.
- **The north window is the main source in our room.** By day, the CB60 works best from the window's side, big and soft, backing up the window instead of fighting it.
- **The Edison bulb is our practical.** At night, dim it until it reads, and let the CB60 at a warm 2,700–3,200 K light the face from the bulb's side, the way he paired his lamp with an orange-gelled light.
- **Make depth with colour.** Warm bulb inside, cool moon outside: the same teal and orange the colorist would otherwise have to build in the grade.
- **A source you showed once can light everything after it.** Once the moon is established through one window, it may rim a head or light a hallway in later shots. The same rule holds in our 3D worlds: a world's sun or bulb has to be visible before it can motivate a light the shot needs.
