Note: "SOLO FILMMAKING: How to Turn Any Story Into a Cinematic Film" by Sam Newton, made alongside his
film The Lioness for Canon USA, shot in Kenya. Below are the points the report card is built on, in
the order the video makes them.

---

**The claim.** What makes a film feel cinematic is much more than big cameras and cool visuals: it is
making the audience feel something real. Five tips, then a bonus.

**1. Find a powerful story.** A Kenyan tented-camp company, Ishara, asked him for a brand film. He asked
whether he could make it about a person instead, and found Amara. What he looks for in a subject:
a core life experience with a struggle and its overcoming; passion about their own story; willingness
to be vulnerable; wanting to be part of the creative process; a character who makes him feel something
real. Good stories are everywhere: if you cannot direct a film in your backyard, you will not be able to
in Kenya. He then interviews the subject with a prepared list of questions.

**2. Start with emotion.** Before deciding what the film says, decide how it should feel — by asking how
the character makes you feel. For Amara: strength, inspiration, power. Then find music that carries
that emotion (with eyes closed, can you picture the film?). Then write a voice-over from the interview
answers, shaped to the song: the struggles where the music is low and slow, the growth as it builds,
a big ending.

**3. Plan with intention.** All references, music, the character and the overview go onto one visual
board (he uses Milanote, the video's sponsor), so collaborators can see the feeling and the look of the
film rather than read a document.

**4. Capture shots that feel real.** Put the character where they are comfortable. About half the shots
are planned; the other half are moments caught as they happen. The rule for the unplanned half: one
wide, one medium, one tight, and one "other" — look left and right for what is outside the line of view.
Leave room for spontaneity and for the team's ideas; when things go wrong, keep the energy and pivot.

**5. The edit.** A rough first cut with simple cuts, lined up to the music and a scratch voice-over.
Then the subject records the real voice-over — coached, because how things are said matters. Then sound
design: layer several sounds for every effect; keep it subtle, so the audience feels it rather than
hears it; build a full sound design that works with the music turned off; use sounds captured on set
(singing, children laughing, the subject's own voice). Then the colour grade (a conversion LUT from log,
then a look LUT). Final test: does it evoke the emotion you set out to?

**Bonus.** Two things few filmmakers still do: send thank-you notes to everyone who helped, and host a
premiere — even a few people round a TV at home — so the film has a place to live before it goes online.
