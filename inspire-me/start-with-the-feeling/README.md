## The story

A company in Kenya wanted a brand film. He asked if he could make it about a person instead.

That swap is the whole method. Sam Newton's film The Lioness, made with Canon USA, is not about a tented camp. It is about Amara, and about what she overcame. The camp is where it happens.

Most of us start the other way round. We think of the camera, the location, the shots we want to get, and hope the feeling turns up in the edit. It rarely does. The world is full of beautiful footage that nobody remembers a minute later.

So he starts with a person. He looks for five things: a real struggle and how they came through it, passion for their own story, the courage to be open about it, the wish to shape the film with him, and the simple test that they make him feel something. If they do, he can make an audience feel it too.

Then, before a single word of the film is written, he decides how it should feel. With Amara the answer came from how she carries herself, a quiet confidence: strength, inspiration, power.

The feeling picks the music. He listens with his eyes closed until a song puts him inside that emotion and he can see the film. Only then does he write. The voice-over is built from her answers to his interview questions, laid over the song: her struggles where the music is low and slow, what she learned as it builds, the big ending where it peaks.

The plan is loose on purpose. About half the shots are planned. The other half are caught as they happen, with the people of a village, with the team's own ideas. To make sure the edit has enough, he keeps one habit for every moment: one wide, one medium, one tight, and one "other" — whatever is just outside where he was looking.

Things go wrong on a shoot like that. The answer is energy, and a team that shares it.

In the edit the feeling is tested again and again. A rough cut first, shots lined up to the music. Then Amara records the voice-over herself, coached line by line, because how a sentence is said matters as much as what it says. Then the sound, layered and quiet, until the film works even with the music off and the audience feels it rather than hears it. The singing and the children's laughter recorded on the day stay in, because they make you feel you were there.

The last question before a film is finished is not whether it looks good. It is whether it makes you feel what you set out to make people feel.

And when it does, he gives it a place to live before it goes online: thank-you notes to everyone who helped, and a premiere, even if it is only friends round a TV.

## Beliefs that shift

- *Cinematic means expensive cameras and striking visuals.* → Cinematic means the audience feels something real; the images serve that feeling.
- *A good film is fully shot-listed in advance.* → Plan half, catch the other half as it happens — one wide, one medium, one tight, one other — and leave room for what you could not have planned.
- *Sound is what you add at the end.* → Sound design, layered and subtle, is what puts the audience in the place; it should carry the film even with the music off.

## What we learn

- **Start with a person, not a product.** A brand film about someone's story says more about the brand than a film about the brand.
- **Decide the feeling first.** Ask how the character makes you feel; name two or three emotions and let them choose everything else.
- **Music before words.** Find the song that holds the emotion, then write the voice-over to its rise and fall.
- **Let people speak their own story.** Record the subject's voice-over, and coach the tone, not only the words.
- **Cover every moment four ways.** Wide, medium, tight, and one look outside the frame gives the edit what it needs.
- **Layer sound, keep it quiet.** Several sounds per effect, recorded ambience from the day, and a mix you feel rather than notice.
- **Finish properly.** Thank the people who helped, and show the film to them first.
