## The story

A blue blob has a rocket. A bar beside it shows the lowest price it will take: $20. Below that, it would rather keep the rocket. An orange blob wants one, and its bar shows the most it will pay: $40.

Any price between the two bars leaves both happier. Say they settle at $30. The buyer gets a $40 rocket for $30, and the seller gets $10 more than it needed. Each comes out $10 ahead. Economists call that gain surplus. Value is slippery and personal, but here it becomes a number, simply by comparing each blob's deal with walking away.

Then the rules start running. Every day, sellers set up shop at the price they expect. Buyers come by in a random order and buy if the price suits them. The day ends when every rocket is sold or nobody left will deal. At the end of the day each blob gives in a little, but only a little. Overnight each one adjusts. A blob that traded asks for a better deal tomorrow. A blob that didn't asks for a little less.

With one buyer and one seller, nothing happens. Neither has the upper hand, and the price sits still.

Add a second buyer whose limit is a little lower. Every day one buyer goes home without a rocket and offers more tomorrow. The seller almost always makes a sale, so it asks for more too. The price climbs until it passes the poorer buyer's limit, and then it's one-to-one again. Most of the surplus has gone to the seller. When buyers compete, sellers win.

Add two more sellers, one cheaper to supply and one dearer. Now one seller goes home unsold every day and drops its price. The buyers always get a deal and push lower. The price falls until the dearest seller can't keep up. This time the buyers keep most of the surplus. When sellers compete, buyers win.

Then a third buyer, poorer than the other two. Three buyers and three sellers, yet only two rockets sell each day. The poorest buyer and the dearest seller can never agree on any price. Push the price up and too few buyers want rockets. Push it down and too few sellers make them. The blobs settle where the number willing to buy equals the number willing to sell. Nobody computed it. Each blob only learned from its own days.

With thousands of blobs, the bars line up into two curves: the sellers' limits are the supply curve and the buyers' limits are the demand curve. The price settles where they cross.

Now the argument. The case for leaving the market alone is that it organises itself, and at that crossing it produces the most total surplus there can be. One more trade would cost more than it is worth, because the next buyer won't pay what the next seller needs. Those left out are free to spend their time and money elsewhere. That is what "efficient" means, and it happens on its own: the invisible hand.

The case for stepping in comes in two kinds. First, is the real market like the blobs' market? The good results need many buyers and sellers, who can switch freely, can walk away, and know the prices and the goods. Some real markets are close to that. Others are far from it. Second, is total surplus the right goal? It was counted one blob at a time. A society could measure value across all the blobs at once. In food, work and health care, it might accept less total surplus so that more people get to take part. The video doesn't back any program, but it says there could be value in making sure everyone can eat, earn and see a doctor. That's up to the blobs.

Markets aren't good or bad. They are a tool, and like any tool, they work best when you understand them and choose carefully when to use them.

## Beliefs that shift

- *Someone has to set the price.* → Each blob only learns from its own day: more after a trade, less after none. Together they find the price where buyers and sellers balance.
- *Competition is good for everyone in the same way.* → Competition hands the surplus to the other side: buyers competing make sellers richer, sellers competing make buyers richer.
- *An efficient market looks after everyone.* → It makes the most total value and leaves out whoever can't pay the price. For rockets that's fine. For food it may not be, and it depends on what the society decides to count.

## The experiments

| Blobs | What happens | Who keeps the surplus |
|---|---|---|
| 1 buyer, 1 seller | The price stays where it started | Split, as they began |
| 2 buyers, 1 seller | The price creeps up until the poorer buyer drops out | Mostly the seller |
| 2 buyers, 3 sellers | The price falls until the dearest seller drops out | Mostly the buyers |
| 3 buyers, 3 sellers | Only 2 rockets a day; the price settles where buyers and sellers willing to trade are equal | The most total surplus possible |
| Thousands | The limits become curves; the price is where supply and demand cross | The most total surplus, with the rest left out |

## What we learn

- **This is the look for Sandbox 8.** Samuel wants Sandbox 8 drawn in Primer's style: soft blobs on a small field, rules you can see, no LLM brains, only rule-based agents. Every blob here wears its limit as a bar and its expected price as a mark, so you can see why it walks away. Each citizen and each COOP in Sandbox 8 can wear its needs and its price the same way.
- **Change one thing, ask, then press go.** He adds one blob at a time and asks "What do you expect to happen?" before each run. That is the Sandbox 8 rule: each experiment changes one rule, runs the same seed side by side and says the result in one plain sentence. Asking for a guess first turns a chart into a story.
- **His price rule is a baseline we never had.** Sandbox 7 tried order books, a posted daily price, a flow price set by buying, and a buffer price from stock against need. His blobs have no shared price at all. Each one learns its own: a bit higher after a trade, a bit lower after none. That rule should be the baseline every Sandbox 8 price is compared with, on the same seed.
- **"Left out" means something else when the rocket is water.** A buyer priced out of rockets spends its money elsewhere. A citizen priced out of water dies, and that is how Sandbox 7 lost avens world after world. Proposition 3 is checked per person, never on totals, so total surplus can't be Sandbox 8's score. Deaths, needs met and every decile's living standard come first.
- **Raise the demand curve through ownership, not price caps.** Samuel's rule is fully dynamic prices: no caps and no protected price for basics. The video's second argument says a society may want everyone at the table for food. In theOS the way in is ownership: every citizen's MIND dividends lift their limit, so the poorest blob's bar rises above the price of water instead of the price being pushed down. Sandbox 8 can show that directly, with the bars.
- **Count who gets the surplus.** With many basics COOPs competing, the surplus should flow to the citizens. With one COOP per good, as at genesis, it flows to the COOP. A surplus split chart per good would show which one a world has become.
- **Check the four conditions in every world.** Many buyers and sellers, free switching, walking away and good information are exactly what a Sandbox 8 world needs before its prices mean anything. Genesis, with one COOP per good, breaks the first one, so a world's score should only count once a second COOP sells each good.
