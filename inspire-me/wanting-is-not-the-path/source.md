**The premise.** Wanting something is not the path to getting it, and it can be the reason you
are blocked from it. Every time you want something, your attention goes back to the gap —
to not having it. There are three positions: wishing, wanting, deciding.

**A wish.** Before something exists in your reality it first exists as a possibility in your
mind — *I wish I could start that business, I wish I had the courage.* A wish rarely changes
your circumstances because it rarely changes you. It asks nothing: no belief, no commitment,
no change. You can wish for a different life while staying completely committed to the one you
already have. It amounts to *wouldn't that be nice.* There is possibility in it, and distance.

**Wanting.** A wish that grows stronger and more emotional becomes *I really want this.* Now
there is energy, desire, hunger. Napoleon Hill spent more than twenty years studying people
like Henry Ford, Andrew Carnegie and Thomas Edison for *Think and Grow Rich*, and the first
step in the book is a burning desire. Desire gives you a target and pulls you past your
current circumstances.

**The trap in wanting.** The act of wanting keeps reminding you that you do not have it.
Embedded in *I want* is an awareness of absence. Example given: a client with vision, ambition
and hard work whose attention kept returning to *I'm not making enough yet, I should be
further ahead by now, why isn't this growing faster, look at what everybody else is doing* —
the goal had become evidence of how far behind they felt. Want $500k and you become aware you
earn 40, 60, 80. Want to run a marathon and you become aware how unfit you are. Want to meet
somebody and the desire is what makes you feel lonely. You stand on the side of the gap,
looking across. Wanting turns into the frustration of possibility rather than the experience
of it.

**Decision.** *Decide* comes from a Latin root meaning to cut off. A real decision cuts you off
from every other possibility: no backup plans, no negotiating with the present moment. Most
people never get there because they think they need something first — the how, the strategy,
certainty. It runs the other way: you figure it out because you decided; you find the strategy
because you decided; certainty is built while making it happen. You decide, and the feeling
follows.

**The school story.** At sixteen, asked to leave school — told the grades wouldn't come and
they weren't intelligent enough. They decided to stay, paying no attention to their current
capability, and went from academic failure to straight-A student in six months. The same
decision mechanism was used later for becoming a best-selling author, a global speaker, a
first sold-out event, and the Rewire Reality retreat.

**Doubt.** Doubts still come — *can I do it, what if it doesn't work, what if I lose what I
have.* That is expected: plant the seed of a dream and you plant the seed of a shadow, and the
bigger the tree the bigger the shadow. Commit anyway. Decision does not eliminate doubt; it
changes which voice you are guided by.

**Change one — what you think.** Before deciding, the mind is divided between competing
possibilities: *can I, should I, what if it doesn't work, maybe I'm not ready, maybe I should
wait.* After deciding, the question is no longer whether it will happen but how. Change the
question and you change which thoughts are available: *who could help me, what haven't I
tried, what would need to be true for this to materialise.* Ideas then arrive while walking,
showering, driving. Rick Rubin describes people as antennas — ideas are all around, and the
job is to be receptive enough to receive them.

**Change two — what you notice.** Enormous amounts of information are available at any moment
and the brain filters by relevance: sounds you weren't attending to, clothes against your
skin, objects in your field of vision you never looked at. Decision changes what counts as
relevant. The car example: choose the car and the colour, and suddenly you see that car
everywhere — they were always there, they just meant nothing last week. Applied to a life
decision: a conference mentioned yesterday meant nothing; today it raises *who goes to that,
could I speak there, who runs it.* A name becomes a person you need to meet. A sentence you
had read before jumps out. Often the opportunity is not new — what is new is your ability to
recognise it. This is usually attributed to the reticular activating system, though several
brain systems are involved; the principle is simply that the brain has to prioritise.

**Change three — what you do.** The brain does not like contradiction. Decide *I am a runner*
and then spend the week on the sofa, and something feels wrong — cognitive dissonance. The
mind resolves it one of two ways: lower the decision to match the behaviour, or raise the
behaviour to match the decision. When the decision is non-negotiable, the behaviour has to
start negotiating.

**The lag.** Decision is the beginning of creation: it changes the thoughts you have, the
opportunities you see and the actions you take, and those actions create new conversations,
relationships, results, and eventually a new reality. Results may look identical tomorrow —
the money not there, the business not built, the relationship not existing. Walk in one
direction, turn around, and you have not reached the new destination, but your future has
changed because your direction has. Do not mistake a delay in evidence for a delay in change.

**The closing question.** What is one thing you have been wishing for, wanting — and are now
ready to decide?
