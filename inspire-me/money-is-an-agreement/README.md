## The story

A four-tonne stone at the bottom of the Pacific, still being spent.

In 1903 an American doctor, William Furness, arrived on Yap and found money that never moved. Huge limestone discs sat by the road; when one was traded, only its ownership changed. One stone sank in a storm while being carried by canoe. The whole island simply agreed it still existed, and its owners kept spending it.

Keynes and Milton Friedman disagreed about almost everything. Both read about Yap, and both said the same thing: these people understood money better than we do.

The narrator had studied economics for six years and still didn't understand it. What he found in nine years of reading contradicted what he was taught — and the professor he trusted most confirmed all of it.

The first thing to fall is the barter story. Adam Smith wrote it in 1776 as a logical argument, not as history, and it was repeated as fact for 250 years. When anthropologists went looking, they found nothing. Caroline Humphrey's conclusion: no example of a barter economy has ever been described. Barter turns up after money fails — Russia in the 1990s, Argentina in 2002 — not before it.

What came first was credit. People remembering what they owed one another. The oldest writing yet found is a debt record from Mesopotamia; coins arrived in Lydia much later. Civilisations ran for centuries on recorded promises.

Money was meant to work like a ruler, a stable measure everyone agrees on. Unlike a minute, it is a human agreement, so it can be changed without telling anyone. That is why people tied it to gold and silver for thousands of years.

Paper broke the anchor, and each time it broke the same way. Song dynasty China: merchants' receipts for heavy iron coins became official paper money, war got expensive, the state printed, trust collapsed. London in the 1600s: goldsmiths lent out gold they were only storing, then issued more receipts than they held gold. In 1694 that practice received a charter — the Bank of England, founded on a £1.2 million loan to King William III — and became the model central banks copied.

Bretton Woods in 1944 tied every currency to the dollar and the dollar to gold at $35 an ounce. On a Sunday in August 1971, Nixon ended convertibility. Since then every currency rests on trust alone.

Most money today is not printed by any government. It is typed into existence by commercial banks at the moment a loan is signed. Central banks target 2% inflation a year, which roughly halves the value of savings every 35 years — quietly helping every borrower, and above all the largest borrowers of all, governments.

The ruler is being shortened on purpose. Once you see money as an agreement, the question stops being what money is, and becomes who gets to write the agreement.

## Beliefs that shift

- *Money is valuable in itself.* → Money is an agreement; its value lives entirely in the trust behind it.
- *Barter came first, then money.* → Credit and recorded promises came first; barter is what's left when money collapses.
- *Banks lend out savers' deposits.* → Commercial banks create new money when they make a loan.

## What we learn

- **An agreement can outlive its object.** Yap's sunken stone kept its value because everyone agreed it did.
- **Credit is older than coins.** People recorded what they owed long before anyone minted money.
- **Every anchorless currency tempts its issuer.** Song China, the goldsmiths and 1971 show the same pressure to print.
- **Whoever creates money gains first.** Bank-created money earns interest for the creator from the moment it exists.
- **A small steady rate compounds hard.** 2% a year is invisible in a month and halves savings over a working life.
- **Designing money is designing fairness.** Who issues it and how decides who carries the cost.

## Open questions

- The narrator is not named in the transcript, so the card has no author. Fill it in from the channel before citing.
- "$100 in 1971 buys $8 today" doesn't match standard US CPI figures, which put it closer to $13. Check before repeating the number.
- The claim that interest money "is never created", so debt must grow forever, is contested among economists — interest paid gets spent back into the economy. Worth hearing the other side.
- The 97% figure comes from the Bank of England's 2014 paper on the UK; the share differs by country and year.
