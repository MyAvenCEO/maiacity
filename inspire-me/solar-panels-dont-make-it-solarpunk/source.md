Note: a video by Our Changing Climate, made in collaboration with St. Andrewism, on what it would
take to build a Solarpunk world with tools that exist today. Below are the points the report card
is built on, in the order the film makes them.

---

**The opening.** Mainstream headlines make it feel as if we are on the edge of an apocalypse.
The film asks what it would look like to imagine the opposite: worlds where nature, people and
technology thrive together, rich with "the greens and blues of abundance". Utopias, perhaps, but
ones worth striving for. That is Solarpunk: a growing genre of art and a movement that rejects the
dystopian, technology-heavy future of cyberpunk and imagines people living well with the natural
world. It is described as an exercise in imagination and a call to action. The question for the
video: how can we build a Solarpunk world right now?

**Technology depends on context.** Using an electric tram or putting solar panels on a roof does
not make a community Solarpunk. It is a step in the right direction, but the same panels under an
exploitative state change nothing about its relationship with the planet or people; mining the
periphery for battery materials is not Solarpunk. The narrator quotes a Reddit user: "Solarpunk
without the abolition of capitalism is just greenwashed Cyberpunk", and argues the tools only
work as part of a broader political struggle, naming eco-anarchism and eco-socialism.

**High and low tech.** Solarpunk is neither always low-tech nor always high-tech. The idea is the
appropriate tool for the place: passive cooling in one town, communal living covered in solar
panels in another; mechanised apple pickers, or shovels and hands in the dirt, or both. What
matters is that the solution strengthens the bond between a community and its land, and lessens
the work needed to live a comfortable life.

**Earthships.** In the high desert around Taos, New Mexico, stand houses designed by architect
Michael Reynolds, who in the 1970s set out to build a completely off-grid home that could handle
New Mexico's extreme heat and cold. The principles: passive heating and cooling, recycled and
local materials, recycled water, and self-reliance through greenhouse gardens built into the
house. The foundation walls are old tyres stacked and packed tight with dirt. Their mass works
like a battery: the roof is angled to let in the right amount of sun, the walls soak up its
warmth by day and release it through the night. Some owners claim they need no external heating.
The house is built from other people's rubbish (tyres, glass bottles) and the earth around it.

**The drawbacks.** Tyres eventually break down and release toxic gases. Reynolds and Earthship
builders say plastering the walls protects the people inside; other builders, including Martin
Holladay, say you would have to keep sealing cracks to be sure. Claims of independence from the
water grid through rainwater collection are called dubious in Taos's desert climate. The
conclusion: Earthships will not work everywhere, but their pieces can go into housing anywhere —
passive heating and cooling, gardens inside the house, filtered rainwater in the plumbing, local
and recycled materials.

**Sailing cargo.** On the Hudson River in New York, Sam Merrett (spelled "Marrett" in places in the
transcript) runs a zero-carbon shipping company that carries local goods up and down the river by
sailboat — dependent on weather and seasons, which the narrator frames as a way for buyers to
appreciate the rhythms of the natural world. The film adds that sails and ropes could support
hemp farming, which it says sequesters "thousands of tons of carbon with each crop".

**High-tech sails.** For ocean freight, engineers are retrofitting cargo ships with column-like
rotor sails that may cut fuel use by "possibly as much as 30%", and designing ships with
retracting rigid sails. The picture: rigid-sail ships crossing oceans for a hemp trade between
continents, and small canvas boats carrying goods the last mile to market. Neither is a silver
bullet; each fits its context.

**Slower.** Wouldn't sail freight mean delays? The narrator's answer is that Solarpunk does not
prioritise one-day-delivery convenience, which comes at the cost of the planet and of workers in
gruelling conditions. "So yes, things might be a bit slower. But I would gladly slow down my life
if it meant that my community and my surroundings thrived."

**What is holding us back.** Earthships and sail cargo exist, but they are rare: the narrator
still sees cargo-ship smoke and concrete buildings. Some of the technologies are still in
development. The main reason given is a profit-centred economy: inventions that cannot compete
in the market are pushed to the margins, whatever their climate or community value. Merrett's
company is a novelty because it cannot compete with one-day delivery or industrial shippers like
Maersk; ramming hundreds of tyres full of dirt would empty one person's bank account. The close:
seek out tools that deepen relationships between people and with the earth, instead of the ones
that make the most profit.
