## The story

The walls are made of rubbish. Old tyres, stacked like bricks and packed tight with earth.

They stand in the high desert around Taos, New Mexico, where architect Michael Reynolds started building in the 1970s. He wanted a house that needed no grid at all and could survive New Mexico's heat and its cold. He called them Earthships.

Today the easy picture of a green future is a skyline of glass with solar panels bolted to every roof and an electric tram humming below. The film goes after that picture first. A tram or a roof of panels is a step in the right direction, it says, but it changes nothing if the society underneath still treats the planet and the people who mine its batteries as things to use up. The panel does not decide what kind of place it powers.

So the film asks a different question: what is the right tool for this place?

The Earthship is one answer. The roof is angled to take in just the right amount of winter sun. The heavy tyre walls soak up the day's warmth and hand it back through the night, like a battery made of dirt. Greenhouse gardens grow inside the house, water is caught and reused, and the materials are the ones nobody else wanted: tyres, glass bottles, the ground it stands on. Some owners claim they need no heating at all.

It is not paradise, and the film says so. Tyres break down over time and can release toxic gas. Earthship builders say plaster keeps it out; the builder Martin Holladay says you would have to keep sealing cracks to be sure. Living off rainwater alone sounds less convincing in a desert. The whole house will not work everywhere, but its parts can go almost anywhere: passive heating and cooling, a garden inside the walls, rain in the plumbing, local and recycled materials.

The second answer floats. On the Hudson River, Sam Merrett ships local goods up and down the river by sailboat, with no fossil fuel at all. His deliveries wait on wind and season. For ocean freight the high-tech version is already being tested: column-shaped rotor sails that the film says may cut a cargo ship's fuel use by up to 30%, and rigid sails that fold away. Big ships with rigid sails cross the ocean; small canvas boats carry the goods the last mile. Neither one alone is the fix.

That leaves the obvious objection. Won't everything be slower?

Yes, says the narrator, and that is a price worth paying. One-day delivery is paid for by the planet and by people working punishing hours. "I would gladly slow down my life," he says, "if it meant that my community and my surroundings thrived."

Then the hardest part. If Earthships and sail freight already exist, why are they so rare? The film's answer is money. A tool that cannot beat industrial shipping on price gets pushed to the edge, however good it is for the climate or the town. Packing hundreds of tyres by hand would empty one family's bank account. Sail freight cannot compete with next-day delivery.

What survives the whole argument is simpler than its politics: stop asking whether a tool is green, and ask what it ties you to. A shovel in the dirt and an automated apple picker can both be right. The right one is the one that brings a community closer to its land, and makes a comfortable life take less work.

## Beliefs that shift

- *Put enough solar panels on a city and it becomes sustainable.* → The panel doesn't decide what kind of place it powers. The society it serves does.
- *The future is either high-tech or back to the land.* → Both, chosen per place: rigid sails for the ocean, canvas for the last mile, tyres and earth for a desert wall.
- *Slower always means worse.* → Speed has a price paid by workers and the planet. A life a little slower can be richer if the place around you thrives.

## What we learn

- **Pick tools by place, not by fashion.** Earthship walls suit a sunny desert with cold nights; rotor sails suit ocean freight. The question is always what fits this land.
- **Thermal mass is a battery you never charge.** Heavy walls that store daytime heat and release it at night can replace a heating system in the right climate, with no moving parts.
- **Build with what is already there.** Tyres, bottles and local earth turned waste into walls. Every site has materials nobody is using.
- **Take the parts, not the whole kit.** A full Earthship fails in many climates; passive heating, indoor gardens and rainwater reuse travel almost anywhere.
- **Pair the ocean with the last mile.** Long-distance and local transport can use different tools: high-tech sails at sea, small sailboats to the market.
- **A good tool can lose to a cheap one.** Earthships and sail freight are rare because they cannot win on price, not because they do not work. Changing what counts as value changes which tools survive.

## Open questions

- The film's politics call for abolishing capitalism outright. Our own direction, from [Day 09](/blog/day-09-hearts-and-minds/), is to spread ownership to every person rather than end markets. Worth thinking through where the two meet: both want tools judged by what they do for a place, not only by profit.
- "Up to 30%" fuel savings from rotor sails is an upper bound. Check real voyage data from ships already fitted with them.
- The claim that hemp for sails and rope would sequester "thousands of tons of carbon with each crop" is given without a source. Find real figures per hectare before using it.
- Tyre off-gassing in Earthship walls is disputed between builders. Look for measured indoor air studies before using tyre walls anywhere people sleep. The transcript also spells the sail-freight captain's name two ways (Merrett and Marrett); confirm before quoting.
