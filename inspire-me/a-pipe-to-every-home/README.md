## The story

Look past the hype at what is being built. Data centers that draw as much power as a city. Solar farms to the horizon. Chip factories that cost more than an aircraft carrier. Humanoid robots coming off the line by the thousands. Underneath, it is silicon, steel and sunlight. Put together, it is a new kind of wealth: machines that think, machines that work, and the power to run them.

For all of history, the one thing we never had enough of was human effort. Apollo needed 400,000 people at its peak. Cancer research has taken more than 50 years and billions of hours, and it isn't done. Whatever we wanted had to squeeze through the same narrow opening: human time, hands and brains.

Post-labour economics starts where that opening widens, in two waves. First, more thinking than we can use, from machines that read, write, design and reason around the clock. Then robots that carry that thinking into the physical world. The Empire State Building took 7 million hours of labour. A million robots working nonstop put in 7 million hours every seven hours, and a few million could rebuild Manhattan in months. Energy won't hold them back for long either: every doubling of solar capacity has made panels about 20% cheaper.

So what is the bottleneck now? We are. Consent, democracy, legitimacy, permission. Nothing this big gets built for long without the agreement of the people who live with it, and that agreement is slipping. By one count, local opposition blocked or delayed $130 billion of US data center projects in the first three months of 2026. About half of Americans are more worried than excited about AI, and fewer than one in ten feel the opposite. Seven in ten expect fewer jobs. They are afraid of losing their income and of a world where a few people own everything.

They have reason to be. When a new technology arrives, the people at the top take the gains first. In Britain between 1780 and 1840, output per worker rose 46% while real wages rose 12%. Historians call it Engels' pause.

Today wages are how the wealth reaches people. You work, you get paid, and the paycheck is your stake. But that stake is shrinking. For half a century after the Second World War, 60 to 66 cents of every dollar US businesses made went to workers. By 2026 it was under 53 cents, the lowest on record. Now imagine 10 cents, or 5.

The wealth hasn't gone. There is more of it than ever. What is missing is the claim on it.

Think in stocks and flows. A stock is something owned: a solar farm, a data center, a fleet of robots. A flow is the income it pays, year after year. A wage is a hand pump: water comes out only while you work the handle. An ownership stake is a pipe to the water tower. The machines keep the tower full, and the water flows whether anyone needs your labour or not.

Most homes are barely connected. The richest tenth of US households own 88% of all stocks. The bottom half own less than 1%. So the plumbing has to be rebuilt around one promise: everyone holds a stake. The stakes can come from many places. Personal ones: savings, a home, a pension. Collective ones: worker-owned companies, cooperatives, credit unions. Municipal ones: a city that owns its solar farm and shares what it earns. State ones: Alaska has paid every resident an oil dividend since 1982. National ones: a fund that pays every citizen. A stake can even be given at birth and run like an endowment.

Now run the economy with labour at 10 cents on the dollar. If a few people own the machines, the other 90 cents pool in a few huge accounts, and the machines lose their customers. Connect every home, and the money flows down every pipe, gets spent, and the machines have customers again. The loop closes, at 10 cents or at 5.

And once people can see a pipe running to their own home, the last bottleneck opens. The wealth is coming. The question was never whether there will be enough. It is whether everyone holds a piece of it.

## Beliefs that shift

- *Labour and energy limit what we can build.* → Machines and cheap solar remove both limits. What's left is permission: the consent of the people who have to live with it.
- *Your job is your share of the economy.* → A wage is a hand pump that stops when you stop. An ownership stake is a pipe that keeps flowing from machines that never stop.
- *Automation makes us poorer.* → It makes us richer in total. Whether each person is richer depends on who holds the claims, and that is a design choice.

## What we learn

- **theOS gives everyone a stake every day.** His "on-ramps" are built into the first law: every citizen gets 24 HEARTS a day, and HEARTS buy nothing. They can only be invested into COOPs, and each HEART becomes a MIND: half to the person, 0.4 to the COOP's treasury and 0.1 to its pool. Nobody needs savings to start owning.
- **Law 5 is the pipe to the tower.** A fifth of every sale is paid at once to everyone who holds the seller's MIND. That is his flow, paid in real time rather than once a year.
- **The bigger pipe is the machine you own.** In our post-AI simulation no human labour is paid at all, and the round trip still closes. But dividends are only 7% of a person's income. Their own HEARTS bring 35%, and the work their Aven proposes and delivers brings 58%. The strongest line to the tower is a machine that works for you and pays you in ownership.
- **Pipes drain upward unless the tower melts.** The richest tenth hold 88% today, because ownership compounds. The video connects every home but has no way to stop the pipes from concentrating again. theOS melts every HEART and MIND by 7% a year. Proposition 2 says the resting Gini stays bounded as long as the melt is higher than the best lasting return. In the post-AI simulation, holdings settle at a Gini of 0.33, against 0.54 when wages are paid.
- **His collective and municipal stakes are our COOPs.** "A city that owns its own solar farm" is the Village COOP, which owns the geothermal plant and sells its power and heat. The City COOP holds all the land, with one equal share for every citizen. There is one difference: theOS pays no basic income and no rent payout, so the land share is membership. The flow comes only through MINDs.
- **Let the neighbours own the data center.** His $130 billion of blocked data centers is the consent problem. In a village, the settlement and Village COOPs own the inference hardware and sell inference to avenCEO. The people who live next to the machines hold its MINDs, so its success pays them.
- **Check the loop person by person.** "It works at 10 cents and at 5" is a claim about totals. Proposition 3 checks every person on every day: no one starves, no one must work to survive, and living standards rise in every decile. Sandbox 8 scores a world only on that.

## Open questions

- The narrator isn't named. "Post-labour economics" is the term David Shapiro has popularised, and the framing sounds like his, but check the channel before adding an author.
- Several numbers come without sources: the $130 billion of blocked data centers, the labour share "under 53 cents, the lowest ever recorded", and the 88% and 1% of stocks. Check them before quoting.
- He lists stakes guaranteed at birth and national funds paying every citizen. theOS rules out a basic income. From what day does a newborn get HEARTS, and who invests them until the child can?
- At rest, the Law 5 dividend yields only about 0.6% a year in our simulation, because yearly sales are a small part of holdings. Is that enough for people to see the pipe running to their own home?
