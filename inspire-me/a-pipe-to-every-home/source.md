Forget the hype for a moment and look at what we are actually building. Data centers that draw as much power as a city, solar farms that stretch to the horizon, chip factories that cost more than an aircraft carrier, humanoid robots rolling off the line by the thousands, and hundreds of billions of dollars a year are pouring into all of it. Underneath, it's just matter and energy. Silicon, steel, and sunlight. But put it together and you get a new kind of wealth. Machines that think, machines that work, and the power to run them.

To see why that's such a big deal, look back. For all of history, the one thing we could never get enough of was human effort. Want to go to the moon? At its peak, the Apollo program employed 400,000 people. That's billions of hours of human work. Want to cure cancer? We have put in more than 50 years and billions more hours and we're still not done. Whatever humanity wanted, it had to squeeze through the same narrow opening: human time, human hands, and human brains.

Post-labor economics means just this. Human effort stops being the bottleneck. It arrives in two waves. First comes cognitive hyper-abundance. More thinking than we could ever use up from machines that read, write, design, and reason around the clock. Then comes labor hyper-abundance as robots carry that intelligence out into the physical world.

Think about what that makes possible. The Empire State Building took 7 million hours of human labor. A million robots working around the clock could put in 7 million hours every 7 hours on the clock. Count the labor alone and a few million of them could rebuild all of Manhattan in a matter of months. Energy won't hold us back for long, either. Every time the world doubles its solar capacity, the price of a panel falls by about 20%. And fusion may soon follow.

So, if labor isn't the bottleneck anymore and energy isn't the bottleneck, what is? We are. Consensus, democracy, legitimacy, and permission. Nothing this big gets built for long without the consent of the people who have to live with it. And that consent is already slipping. By one count, local opposition across the United States blocked or delayed $130 billion worth of data center projects in the first 3 months of 2026 alone. AI is already incredibly unpopular. About half of Americans say they are more concerned than excited about it. Fewer than one in 10 say the opposite. And AI hasn't even entered its golden age yet. We haven't even begun to see what the robots will be capable of.

Why? Because people are afraid of being left behind. Seven in 10 Americans expect AI to leave the country with fewer jobs. They fear losing their income, and they fear a world where a handful of people own everything. And they're right to worry. History shows that when a disruptive technology arrives, the people at the top make sure they capture the gains first. Between 1780 and 1840, as the Industrial Revolution took hold in Britain, output per worker rose 46%, real wages rose only 12%. This was called Engels' pause.

That's where post-labor economics comes in. My goal is to show that we can all win and to build a democratic consensus to get there. People don't need another promise that the future will be amazing. They need to see a path to success for themselves. If every voter and every citizen sees a real chance at a better, easier, more fulfilling life, then they will get on board with automation and robots.

So, here is the key insight. Right now, wages are the main way the wealth we create reaches people. It is the bedrock arrangement of civilization. You work, you get paid, and that paycheck is your stake in the economy. It is entirely transactional. And it's assumed to be irreplaceable, which is why conventional economists won't even say the words post-labor economics. But wages have been losing ground for decades. For half a century after the Second World War, between 60 and 66 cents of every dollar American businesses produced went to its workers. By 2026, that number was under 53 cents, the lowest ever recorded. Now imagine 10 cents or five.

Notice what hasn't changed. The wealth is still there. There is more of it than ever before because machines are making everything we need. Wealth is not the problem. The problem is the claim on it, an ownership stake.

Now think in terms of stocks and flows. A stock is something that's owned, a solar farm, a data center, or a fleet of robots. A flow is the income it throws off year after year. A wage is a hand pump. Money comes out only while you work the handle. An ownership stake is a line to the water tower. The machines keep the tower full and it flows whether or not anyone needs your labor.

Today, most homes are barely connected. The richest tenth of American households hold 88% of all the stocks. The bottom half hold less than 1%. So, we need to rebuild the plumbing. We recalibrate the economy around one guarantee, everyone holds a stake. And it doesn't have to come from any one place. Some stakes are personal. Your savings, your home, or your retirement account. Some are collective. Employee-owned companies, cooperatives, and credit unions are good examples. Some are municipal. A city that owns its own solar farm and shares what it earns. Some are held by your state. Alaska has paid its residents a dividend from its oil wealth every year since 1982. And some are federal. A national fund that pays every citizen dividends. A stake can also be guaranteed at birth. It could be managed like an endowment, paying dividends year after year. And we can open more on-ramps for people to build wealth of their own.

Now, run that economy with labor at 10 cents on the dollar. The machines produce more than ever. If only a few people own them, the other 90 cents pool in a few enormous bank accounts. And the machines lose their customers. But, connect every home and the money flows out through every tower, down every pipe. People spend it. The machines have customers and the loop closes. It works at 10 cents and it also works at 5 cents.

And once people can see the pipes that run to their own homes, the last bottleneck opens. Nobody votes against a future they own a piece of. The wealth is coming. We are pouring the concrete for it right now. The question was never whether there will be enough. It's how we make sure everyone has a stake in it.
