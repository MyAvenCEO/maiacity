## The story

A new notebook is deeply optimistic. The woman buying it has plans. Her handwriting will stay the same size. This lasts roughly 11 days.

When you're young, the chapter breaks are handed to you: new timetable, new teachers, new shoes that hurt at the heel. Everyone agrees when one chapter ended.

Then you become an adult and it stops. No final bell. Tuesday becomes Wednesday, and somehow you've used the same dry cleaner for 9 years despite actively disliking them.

Things begin, and then they simply continue — largely because no one remembered to stop them.

Proof is in the drawer everyone has. A charger for something you no longer own. A key — not the key, just a key. You can't identify the lock. You may not live in the same country as the lock. Back it goes, because "what if" has an extremely generous retention policy.

She has moved a pair of pants she doesn't like through three homes. Every clean-out, she tries them on, thinks no, and puts them back. Time makes familiarity feel meaningful.

And the same thing happens with much more expensive items: goals belonging to a woman she barely remembers being, who may have written them while she still had braces — and who is apparently still owed status updates.

So instead of the grand question "what do you want?", a smaller one: **would I choose this again? Not forever. Now.**

Sometimes the answer is a full yes — and choosing something a second time, with the fantasy removed from the premises, turns out to be more moving than getting something new.

## Beliefs that shift

- *Still in my life means still wanted.* → Still in my life usually means never reconsidered.
- *You'll know when something is over.* → Nothing announces it — the absence of any signal is the signal.
- *Leaving needs a reason other people accept.* → Leaving needs one reason: you wouldn't choose it again.
- *Leaving means the years were wasted.* → A decision can be right for its years and wrong for the next one.
- *Your younger self still sets the goals.* → Your younger self wrote them in braces, and her term has expired.

## What we learn

- **We decide once and continue forever.** Everyone talks about making decisions. Nobody talks about continuing them.
- **The hard cases are the ones that are fine.** No incident, no receipts, nothing to point to — so they survive by default.
- **Yes is a real answer.** The second time you choose something you know what it's like in February, and in the rain. Still yes is worth noticing.
- **Knowing sooner wasn't available.** It would have required being who you are now before the experiences that made you this person.
- **Beginnings work because they interrupt.** The empty notebook, the new apartment, the zipped suitcase: they make you look.
- **You can interrupt yourself.** No birthday, breakup, Monday or January required — and no equipment either.

## For maiaCITY

Let's translate this.

- **Sunset clauses by default.** Rules, roles, projects and committees expire and have to be actively re-chosen, never silently renewed.
- **Build the chapter breaks.** A seasonal, city-wide review — the adult version of being handed a new timetable — where people re-pick what they're part of.
- **Make leaving legitimate.** No incident needed. "I wouldn't choose this again" is a complete reason to step out of a role.
- **Audit what's fine.** The things nobody complains about are the ones quietly surviving on inertia. Go looking for them on purpose.
- **Mark re-choosing, not just joining.** Renewal deserves its own ritual, or only the new gets celebrated.

## Open questions

- Which of our current commitments would we not choose again today — and what's stopping us from asking out loud?
- Where does this stop applying? Care, promises and debts should survive a bad Wednesday. Where is the line between re-choosing and flakiness?
- Does a scheduled re-choosing ritual stay honest, or does it become a rubber stamp?
