Note: Daniel Bitton Extra on short-form scripting, ending with a critique from an anonymous
faceless-shorts creator. Below are the points the report card is built on, in order. Two of the
examples he uses as successful hooks are deliberately provocative — one leans on a racial
stereotype, and the critic suggests reaching for an accusation because of the attention it
attaches. Both are described here rather than quoted, and are addressed in the report card's
open questions.

---

**The premise.** One of his uploads got 34 million views; another got 30,000. He attributes the
entire difference to the first couple of seconds, and says he worked the hook out over
thousands of hours and billions of views.

**Three rules for the intro.** First, get straight to the point — a short does not require
attention to start playing, so people scroll, see it, and only stay if you have given them a
reason. Second, everything in the intro must lead to the payoff; he describes every word being
calculated against something arriving in the next thirty seconds. Third, no fluff — anything
that does not contribute makes attention dip, and the algorithm detects boredom immediately.
One bad sentence is enough.

**Sentence one — the hook.** Catch attention instantly with eye-catching visuals or
psychological triggers. His example: a short asking why a well-known creator turned down a
billion dollars, which did over 12 million views. A billion dollars is a huge number and the
refusal immediately raises why. The first sentence's only job is to tell the viewer exactly
what they need to know in the most direct way possible, with fast-paced visuals of the same
subject running underneath. Using a person almost everyone recognises keeps attention past the
intro.

**Controversy as a trigger.** He credits the 34-million video's performance to being extremely
controversial — an opening question built on a racial stereotype, which he frames as giving
people permission to be curious about a subject they were afraid to ask about. He contrasts it
with a video that flopped, whose premise (a question about déjà vu) he judges uninteresting and
whose visuals he judges weak.

**Sentence two — the supporting hook.** Once you have their attention you cannot let them
escape. Subvert expectations, add context, make it more interesting than they assumed.
Over-delivering on expectations is what makes videos go viral; clickbait is the failure of
promising something and not delivering. In the billion-dollar example, the supporting hook
poses the two most likely reasons for the refusal and then tells the viewer they are wrong,
which sends them looking for the real answer. His framing: don't give them what they want right
away, make them fight for it, make them think they are right when they are wrong. He calls it
psychological manipulation and says it works because it makes the viewer feel smart and then
obliges them to prove it.

**When to skip it.** Not every video needs a supporting hook. One of their most popular videos
— about a YouTuber visiting a remote island community — had none, because the concept spoke for
itself and selling the viewer further would have wasted time.

**Sentences three and four — setting the scene.** Set a timeline or a location, develop the
context, and lead slowly toward the payoff. In the billion-dollar example: he went on a podcast
and admitted receiving the offer from a private company, and it covered all 18 of his channels
and all of his companies. There is no reason to explain such details in depth, because viewers
either know or can infer them; over-explaining simple things insults the audience's
intelligence, does not help attention and may kill the video.

**Stakes.** One or two further sentences sell the stakes: accepting would have made him the
world's first YouTube billionaire and the youngest self-made billionaire of all time — so why
turn it down? This converts curiosity into investment. The question stops being "why did he say
no" and becomes "why did he give up becoming the youngest self-made billionaire of all time" —
a much stronger form of interest for the same topic.

**Teasing.** Give hints about the payoff without delivering it. In a video about a creator's
janitor, the identity was kept secret until the end while being teased throughout. Viewers may
guess, but people want to be completely right, so they stay to confirm. It makes watching feel
like solving a puzzle rather than passive consumption.

**The payoff.** Delivered in the last few seconds: the janitor's identity in one case, and in
the other, simply that the offer was considered too low. All the build-up resolves into
something small, which still satisfies because it answers the question they were holding.
Deliver it instantly, do not drag it out, and end the video — the second a viewer has what they
came for, they click off.

**The call to action.** The only place to go off topic, and better if tied in. Their versions
asked viewers to subscribe if they loved the person revealed, or if they had known the answer
the whole time — tying the ask to what the viewer had already invested in the video.

**The outside critique.** He sends a script built on this framework to an anonymous creator
earning a five-figure monthly income from faceless shorts (the figure is stated as over
$20,000 in one place and over $30,000 in another), using a fake account so the critic does not
know who he is. The verdict: a good hook, because it names recognisable people immediately and
creates a question; it reads the audience's thoughts back to them. Suggested improvements —
state the money figure earlier, within the first five seconds; and use a more inflammatory
framing (the critic names an accusation of scamming) because of the attention it attaches,
which the critic acknowledges as unethical and describes as the game being played. The critic
praises the rehooks after the context, which remind people why they are on the video, and the
loop effect at the end, and adds that the payoff should always land in the third quarter of the
video.
