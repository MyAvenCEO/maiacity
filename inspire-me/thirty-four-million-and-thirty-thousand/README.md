## The story

Two videos. Thirty-four million views, and thirty thousand.

Nothing about the production explains that gap, and the creator does not try to make it mysterious: it is the first couple of seconds, and after what he describes as thousands of hours and billions of views, he treats those seconds as a solved problem rather than a talent.

The reason they matter so much is structural, and it is specific to the format. A short does not need your attention to start playing. It plays because somebody scrolled past it. Nobody chose you. The only decision the viewer makes is whether to keep scrolling, and they make it before you have finished a sentence.

Which turns every word into a cost. He describes writing scripts where each word is calculated against something arriving thirty seconds later — no line exists for its own sake, and anything that does not carry the viewer toward the payoff is actively losing the video, because the algorithm reads boredom instantly and one weak sentence is enough.

So the script gets built sentence by sentence, each with a job.

Sentence one states the thing in the most direct way available. His example is a question about a creator turning down a billion dollars: a number big enough to stop a thumb, a name almost everybody recognises, and a gap opened in the same breath — a billion dollars, refused, and you do not know why. There is no faster way to hand a viewer that situation, and the visuals run fast underneath it carrying the same information a second way.

Sentence two is where most people stop, and where he says the real work starts. It is a supporting hook, and its job is to make the video bigger than the viewer assumed. In the billion-dollar example, it names the two explanations anyone would reach for, then rules both out. Now they are not curious, they are stuck — with the additional problem that they have just been told, politely, that they are wrong.

He is blunt about what that is. Make them think they are right, then show them they are not, and they will stay to prove they can work it out. The viewer experiences it as solving something. It is the same passive watching it always was.

Not every video needs that second beat. He shows one where the premise carried itself — a video about someone visiting a remote island community — and selling the viewer any harder would have been spending seconds on a sale already made.

Sentences three and four set the scene: a place, a time, the details that make the situation legible. The offer covered eighteen channels and all his companies. That is the whole explanation, and he warns against making it longer — over-explaining something a viewer can infer reads as an insult and costs you attention you cannot get back.

Then one or two sentences that do the move I think is the real lesson in the piece. They raise the stakes: accepting would have made him the world's first YouTube billionaire, and the youngest self-made billionaire of all time. The question has not changed, but *why did he say no* has become *why did he give up being the youngest self-made billionaire in history* — and curiosity has quietly turned into investment.

From there you tease without paying. Another of his videos kept an identity hidden the whole way through while mentioning the person constantly. Viewers guess. Guessing is not enough, because people want to be certain, and certainty is at the end.

The payoff itself is almost disappointingly small — a name, or a single line about the offer being too low. It works because everything before it was a question the viewer was actively holding. And the instruction attached to it is the one most people get wrong: deliver it instantly and stop. The moment they have what they came for, there is nothing left to hold them, and every second after that is a decline the algorithm can see.

One sentence remains, and it is the only place he permits going off-topic: a call to action tied to what the viewer just did. Not *subscribe*, but subscribe if you knew the answer all along — aimed at the investment they already made rather than at goodwill they never offered.

He then sends a script built this way to an anonymous creator earning five figures a month from faceless shorts, who does not know who he is. The verdict is broadly positive, with three notes: put the number earlier, keep the rehooks that restate why the viewer is still here, and put the payoff in the third quarter rather than at the very end. The fourth note is the one worth examining — a suggestion to reach for a more inflammatory word because it attaches attention regardless of accuracy, offered with a shrug about that being the game.

## From the ground up

- **The scroll.** A short starts playing without being chosen. You are not competing with other videos for a click; you are competing with the reflex to keep moving. Every decision follows from that.
- **The hook.** Sentence one. Its only job is to hand over the situation in the fewest possible words, and to leave one obvious question unanswered inside it.
- **The supporting hook.** Sentence two. It makes the video bigger than the viewer assumed — usually by naming the explanation they are already reaching for and removing it.
- **The payoff.** The answer to the question the hook opened. It is the whole reason anyone is still watching, which is why its timing matters more than its content.
- **Stakes.** What the answer costs or is worth. Stakes are what turn a curious viewer into an invested one, and they are added with a sentence, not discovered.
- **The rehook.** A line mid-script that restates why the viewer is still here. Attention leaks; a rehook plugs it without advancing the story.
- **The tease.** Mentioning the payoff without delivering it. Safe because people will not leave on a guess — only on certainty.
- **The loop / call to action.** The last line, tied to what the viewer just invested rather than to a generic ask.

## The steps

1. Decide the payoff first — the single fact the video exists to deliver. Everything else is built backwards from it.
2. Write sentence one so it hands over the situation in the most direct words available, with one question left open. Use a name, number or image the viewer already recognises.
3. Make the visuals carry the same information in parallel, fast, from the first frame.
4. Write sentence two to remove the obvious answer. Name what they are thinking, then rule it out.
5. Skip step 4 if the premise is strange enough to sell itself. Do not spend seconds closing a sale already made.
6. Set the scene in two sentences: where, when, and only the details needed to make the situation legible.
7. Cut every explanation a viewer could infer. Over-explaining costs attention and reads as condescension.
8. Add one or two sentences of stakes, so the question becomes expensive rather than merely open.
9. Tease the payoff at intervals without delivering it, and rehook — restate why they are still watching — after each block of context.
10. Deliver the payoff around the third quarter, in as few words as possible.
11. Stop. Do not elaborate after the answer; there is nothing holding the viewer any more.
12. Close with a call to action tied to what they just did, not to what you want.

## Beliefs that shift

- *You win attention with a good opening and keep it with good content.* → You never had it. A short plays without being chosen, so every sentence has to re-earn the next one.
- *Give the audience what they came for.* → Give it to them late. Curiosity is the only thing holding them, and the payoff is what ends the video.
- *More context is more clarity.* → Explaining what a viewer can infer costs you attention and reads as an insult.

## What we learn

- **Write the payoff first.** A script written forwards wanders. A script written backwards from its answer has no line in it that isn't carrying weight.
- **Stakes convert curiosity into investment.** The same question, with what it costs attached, is a different and much stronger question.
- **Remove the obvious answer out loud.** Naming what the audience is already thinking and ruling it out is worth more than any new information.
- **Rehook after every block of context.** Restating why someone is still here is not filler; it is the maintenance that keeps the middle alive.
- **End on the answer.** The second the viewer has what they came for, staying is unrewarded — so do not ask them to.
- **Tie the ask to the investment.** A call to action attached to what the viewer just worked out converts; a generic one does not.
- **One bad sentence is measurable.** In a format this short, attention is a graph, and it shows you exactly which line lost people.

## Open questions

- Two of the techniques here are manipulation with the label removed: the advice to reach for a more inflammatory framing because it attaches attention regardless of accuracy, and the deliberately taboo opening he credits for his biggest video. Both work. Neither belongs anywhere near this project, and it is worth being precise about why — a hook that promises something the body cannot honestly pay is the definition of the clickbait he spends the video arguing against.
- "Payoff in the third quarter" is the most useful number in the piece and comes from the anonymous critic, not from measurement shown on screen. Worth testing against our own retention data rather than taking on trust.
- The monthly earnings figure for the anonymous expert is given twice and differently. Treat it as approximate.
- Does any of this survive the jump to long-form writing, where the reader sets the pace and can leave at any line? My own read is that the structure transfers and the timing does not: a post has no "third quarter", but it does have a place where the reader could leave satisfied, and that is the same idea.
