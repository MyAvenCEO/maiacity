## The story

Viewers wrote to her, worried: her home address was on the parcel in her vlog. It wasn't her address. Kariza had made the shipping label herself, and its QR code leads to her channel.

Details like that run all through LifeOfRiza's videos, and they give away how the videos are made. A year into vlogging, she sat down with Jack of Digital Spaghetti to watch a dozen clips from that year. Three weeks after her first vlog, she had passed 100,000 subscribers.

Before that, she made essay videos: a topic, and three points for each. They felt rigid. They took too long to make, and as a creator she wasn't satisfied with them.

Vlogging was meant to be looser, a way to tell many kinds of stories and to grow as a storyteller and a filmmaker. And the vlog everyone knew was the Casey Neistat kind: pick up the camera, live the day, find the story afterwards.

She wanted to keep it up, though, and everything takes long. Filming takes long. Editing takes long. Scripting takes long. A free-for-all where she could make anything was no plan for doing it again and again — and talking to a camera, or carrying one down a public street, never stopped being uncomfortable.

So before her first vlog, she wrote herself rules. A three-act narrative: a problem or a conflict, and its resolution by the end. Keep it simple, fun and playful. One banger cinematic shot. And a bonus, one moment of surprise and delight. A box, as she puts it, big enough to play in.

Every video is then planned like a film. A script in Apple Notes, a shot list in a second note, storyboards of stick figures, sometimes the song already chosen so she knows the mood of a scene. On the day she just works through the shots, up to thirty of them.

The script is where the story learns to move. Every scene has to push it one step: packing a bag is getting from point A to point B. She talks while she does something (an old improv rule says the talk must be about anything but the activity, and the scene gets a little tension from it). Her friend is introduced without a word of introduction — she is already waiting on the curb, used to this. A question hangs in the air, the music comes in, and only seconds later do we see where they are going.

The fake label, the twine round the parcel, the tidy desk with its perfect cookies: art direction, for what she calls her cinematic world. The bloopers stay in. Some moments really do happen in real time.

That is the whole shape of it. A vlog based on a true story: written, shot-listed and storyboarded, every scene one step forward inside three simple rules, and real life as the story it is based on.

## Beliefs that shift

- *A vlog is improvised: you live the day and find the story in the edit.* → A vlog can be written first — based on a true story, scripted and storyboarded.
- *Rules kill creativity.* → A few simple rules are a box big enough to play in, and they make it possible to keep going.
- *A scene is something that happens.* → A scene is a step: new information that moves the story forward.

## The ground rules

| Her rule | How it shows in her videos |
| --- | --- |
| A three-act narrative | each video leads into a problem or a conflict and ends on its resolution |
| Keep it simple, fun, playful | a loose, personal tone; the bloopers left in; a knuckle crack that is obviously dubbed |
| One banger cinematic shot | a dolly shot from a Boosted Board on a New York street, a camera clamped to a car door |
| Bonus: one surprise and delight moment | the fake label whose QR code opens her channel; an address on screen that leads to the bookstore she grew up with |

## What we learn

- **Every scene must earn its place.** She asks what new information the audience gets from it. If the answer is nothing, the story has stopped.
- **Do something while you talk.** An activity makes talking to the camera easier, adds a little tension when the talk is about something else, and can carry the story (packing is A to B).
- **Introduce people through action.** The friend already waiting on the curb tells you who she is and what their friendship is like, without a line of explanation.
- **Let the music say what comes next.** A question, then the music, then the answer seconds later. Trust the audience instead of holding its hand.
- **Change the camera to keep the momentum.** After a line or two on the tripod she goes handheld; a whip pan, once in the hand and once on the tripod, hides the cut between them.
- **Plan so the shooting day is easy.** A script, a shot list and a storyboard mean thirty shots without forgetting one composition, and an edit that isn't a disaster.
- **One style for a season.** A year with the same pillars taught her discipline; the next season can add one new element.
