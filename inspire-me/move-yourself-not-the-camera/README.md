## The story

Just him, a camera and a head full of ideas. No one to hold the camera, no one to check the focus, no one to say the shot is crooked.

Every solo creator starts there, and Mridu Mousam Neog stayed for years. He filmed himself, directed himself and told his own stories, learning by trial, mistakes and repetition until the footage looked cinematic.

Alone, control is the first thing to go. You cannot see your frame. You do not know if you are in focus. Most of the time, he says, you are guessing — and a guessed shot looks like one: one size, flat, the camera parked wherever it was put down.

So before anything else, he took control back.

Seeing came from the phone in his pocket. A remote app (Lumix Sync, for his Lumix S5) shows exactly what the camera sees, so he checks the framing, locks the exposure and checks the focus before he presses record. That alone, by his count, removes half the frustration of filming solo. A tripod does the holding.

Then the harder part. A film moves, and there was nobody to move the camera.

He turned the problem round. The camera stays where it is and he moves — into the frame, out of it, towards the lens, away from it. The motion looks natural, and in the edit it carries one shot into the next.

The frame he builds like a set. Every action is filmed three times: wide for the place, medium for the action, close for the feeling and the detail. Every frame gets three layers, something in front, him in the middle, the world behind, because flat footage almost always lacks depth. And he puts the camera where a person would never stand: low to make a movement powerful, high to show vulnerability, tight at the side for intensity, or on a magic arm clamped to a badminton racket, a motorcycle, a pen.

Light works the same way. No studio lamps — a ray of sun through a window, a beam of light falling through a building onto a wall. He does not move the light. He turns himself round it until the frame feels right, and whenever he can, he shoots in golden hour.

What holds it all together is the slowest part of the process. Speed kills quality, so he visualises the shot, sets the frame, records, walks back, and does it again.

Alone, you stop moving the camera and start directing the frame — and the thing in it that moves is you.

## From the ground up

- **A remote monitor.** An app on your phone that shows what the camera sees, live, and changes its settings from where you stand. Without one you guess your frame, focus and exposure; with one you check all three before you record.
- **A tripod.** The crew member who never gets tired: it holds the frame exactly where you set it, so you can step into it. For a solo creator it is the most important piece of kit.
- **Shot sizes.** How much of the world a shot shows. A wide shows the place, a medium the action and who is doing it, a close-up the feeling and the small things. Three sizes of one action give the edit choice and rhythm.
- **Composition.** Where things sit in the frame, so the eye goes straight to the subject: on a third of the frame, inside a frame (a door, a window), at the end of lines that lead to it, in a symmetrical frame, or alone in empty space. Know the rules before you break them on purpose.
- **Depth layers.** A flat picture has one layer; a cinematic one has three — foreground, midground (you), background. Something close to the lens is the quickest way to add depth.
- **Subject movement.** When nobody can move the camera, the subject moves: into the frame, out of it, towards or away from the lens. The picture moves while the camera stands still, and the movement links shots across a cut.
- **Camera angle.** Where the lens is, compared to a person's eyes. Low makes a movement feel powerful, high makes someone look vulnerable, a tight angle from the side feels intense. The question to ask: where would this shot look most interesting from?
- **Natural light.** Light that is already there: a window, a beam of sun, golden hour. Instead of moving lamps, you move yourself until the light falls the way you want.

## The steps

1. Put the camera on the tripod and open its remote app on your phone (Lumix Sync for a Lumix; Sony's remote app for a Sony). *(a remote monitor)*
2. In the app, set the frame, lock the exposure and check the focus, all before you press record.
3. Find the light first — a window, a beam of sun, golden hour — and turn yourself and the frame round it until it falls right. *(natural light)*
4. Build the frame in three layers: something in the foreground, you in the midground, the place behind you. *(depth layers)*
5. Place yourself by a rule of composition: on a third, inside a frame, at the end of a leading line, in symmetry, or in negative space. *(composition)*
6. Choose the angle: low for power, high for vulnerability, tight at the side for intensity — or clamp a small camera on a magic arm where no person could stand. *(camera angle)*
7. Record the action as a wide shot, walking into or out of the frame, or towards or away from the lens. *(subject movement)*
8. Move the tripod and record the same action as a medium shot, then as a close-up of your face or hands. *(shot sizes)*
9. Walk back, watch the take on your phone, and do it again until it's right. Don't hurry: speed kills quality.
10. In the edit, cut between the three sizes, and let your movement carry each cut into the next shot.

## Beliefs that shift

- *You need a camera operator for shots that move.* → The camera can stay on the tripod while you move through the frame.
- *Cinematic takes expensive lights and gear.* → It takes three sizes, three layers and the light already in the room, met by moving yourself.
- *Filming fast is how a solo creator keeps up.* → Speed kills quality: visualise, set, record, walk back, repeat.

## What we learn

- **Control comes first.** A phone as a monitor turns guessing into checking: framing, exposure and focus before the record button.
- **Film every action three times.** Wide, medium, close — the edit gets choice and rhythm for the price of two more takes.
- **Depth is built, not found.** Something near the lens, you in the middle, the world behind.
- **When the camera can't move, the subject can.** Walking in, out, towards and away gives motion and links shots across a cut.
- **Find the light, then turn to it.** One window or one beam of sun beats lamps you don't have, and golden hour is free.
- **Strange places make shots.** A small camera on a magic arm (a racket, a motorbike, a pen) sees from where no one could stand.
- **Filming alone teaches you to see.** It forces you to think and to observe, which is how the stories get better.
