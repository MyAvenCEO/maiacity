Note: an "Impact Cut" from the How to Live on Earth series, on rethinking food systems, with
Sam Kass and a Michelin-starred chef cooking with alternative proteins. Below are the points
the report card is built on, in the order the film makes them.

---

**The opening.** A response to the idea that if things do not work out on Earth we can live on
Mars: Mars is horrible, and the better question is how to live here first.

**The orchard.** The biggest pear-growing area in the world. For weeks people across the region
collect blossom on an industrial scale; machines separate the stamens, which are dried to
extract the pollen; that pollen is then used to pollinate the remaining flowers by hand,
because something is missing. Since the 1980s pesticides have been used to increase the
harvest, and inadvertently silenced nature's most effective pollinators. The farmers now have
to be as busy as the bees — and nothing matches the bees and other pollinators needed for 75%
of the cultivated crops we grow.

**Smart versus wise.** We call ourselves homo sapiens, meaning wise human, described as a bit
presumptuous: we are smart, but it does not feel as though we are wise yet. As intelligent
humans we developed ingenious ways to raise protein, fuel societies and create prosperity. As
wise humans we will do all of that working with nature rather than against it. Globally,
agriculture has been by far the biggest driver of nature loss.

**Sam Kass.** An expert on the global food system, formerly senior policy adviser on it to the
president and White House chef, now mostly cooking for his children — whom he describes as far
harsher critics than heads of state. His framing of the problem: a burger takes a lot of grain
to bring a cow to full size, which takes a lot of land and a lot of water, so people are looking
for protein with a lighter impact.

**Insects.** Scorpion and part of a tarantula are tried at the table, described as crunchy like
a potato chip. The children are unconvinced. The scene is played for humour, with the serious
line underneath it: the biggest issue we face is how we get our protein.

**Air protein.** A chef friend, Greg, cooks with new alternative proteins that take up virtually
no space to produce. The draw is described as hyper-sustainable — produced out of thin air, and
a source of protein. Flavour is close to neutral with a little umami, so it does not alter a
dish. Used in place of milk it makes a dairy-free preparation in which it cannot be tasted.
Judged inspiring as a technological breakthrough, with resources needed that are minimal
compared with raising a cow — part of the solution rather than a replacement.

**Mycelium.** The newest alternative to steak, farmed rather than assembled: described as the
closest thing to a plant-based steak seen so far, rooted in the natural world — grown in a tank,
but from a strand pulled directly from nature. It grows fast and efficiently, so it is quite
affordable, and is called one of the healthiest things you can eat, incredibly nutrient-dense.

**Blended meat.** Mycelium blended with a little real pork keeps the flavour while adding fibre
and other nutrients — roughly a third of a day's protein and a third of a day's fibre. The
verdict: you cannot tell the difference with mushroom-blend sausages and burgers, and some are
actually better for the extra depth of flavour. Described as the path forward — "the meat in
the middle of the road" — and a win for everyone involved.

**What that would free.** With mycelium farms, blended meats and air proteins we could feed
ourselves on a fraction of the land currently used. Embracing these foods could bring back huge
swathes of wild habitat, locking away carbon and making Earth more resilient and more stable —
and those who help restore that balance would be paid handsomely for their efforts.

**Manuel.** A cattle farmer trying something different: he had cleared forest for his cattle,
and is now planting cacao on those fields and planting the forest back to shade the cacao,
because cacao needs some kind of cover.

**The closing.** We may soon become a force for good on this planet, with a new role: global
gardeners, planetary stewards, the captains of spaceship Earth. Are we ready? Are we wise
enough? Almost.
