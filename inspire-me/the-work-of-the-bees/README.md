## The story

They gather the blossom industrially. Machines separate the stamens and dry them, and the pollen that comes out the other end goes back onto the flowers still on the trees — by hand, in the largest pear-growing region on earth.

The reason is thirty years old. Pesticides came in during the 1980s to raise the harvest, and they did, and they also silenced the insects the harvest depended on. So the farmers are now as busy as the bees, doing a job that used to be done for free, badly, at enormous cost, for a fraction of the coverage. Around three quarters of the crops we cultivate need a pollinator.

That is the whole argument for rethinking food, stated once and never repeated: an input that worked, and a second-order effect that ate the thing it was helping.

The film opens somewhere else entirely, with the escape hatch. If Earth doesn't work out, the story goes, we can live on Mars. Its answer is short — Mars is horrible, why would anyone want to live there, how about we first work out how to live here.

Then the line that does the real work.

We named ourselves *homo sapiens*, the wise human, which it calls a bit presumptuous. Smart, certainly. Smart built the yields, the fuels, the prosperity. Wise would be doing all of that with nature rather than against it, and by that measure we are not there yet. Globally, agriculture is the single largest driver of nature loss.

So what would the wise version of protein look like?

Sam Kass — who advised a president on the food system and cooked in the White House, and now mostly cooks for children who are harsher critics — puts the problem plainly. A burger is a great deal of grain, a great deal of land and a great deal of water, converted through a cow. Insects are one answer, and the film is honest about how that lands at a family table: the kids try a scorpion and a piece of tarantula and are unimpressed.

The interesting answers are the two that need almost no land at all.

The first is protein made out of air — neutral, faintly savoury, invisible in a dish. A Michelin-starred chef uses it to replace milk in a dairy-free preparation and nobody can taste it. Taking air and making protein out of it is an extraordinary thing to be able to do, and the resources it consumes next to a cow are almost nothing.

The second is mycelium, farmed in a tank from a strand pulled out of nature. It grows fast, it grows efficiently, and it is cheap enough to matter. As a steak substitute it is the closest anyone has got. Blended with a little real pork it becomes a sausage carrying roughly a third of a day's protein and a third of a day's fibre — and the verdict in the room is that you cannot tell, and that some of the blends are simply better.

That is the shape of the compromise the film argues for. Not the end of meat. Meat in the middle of the road.

Do that at scale and the land requirement collapses — which is the point, because the land is where the rest of the argument lives. Feed ourselves on a fraction of what we currently use and huge stretches of habitat can come back, holding carbon, making the whole system steadier. And the people who do that restoring, it argues, should be paid handsomely for it.

There is already a farmer doing the small version. Manuel cleared forest for cattle. He is now growing cacao on those fields, and planting the forest back, because cacao wants shade.

Which leaves the job description. Global gardeners. Planetary stewards. The captains of a spaceship that happens to be the only habitable thing we know of.

Are we wise enough yet? Almost.

## Beliefs that shift

- *An input that raises the harvest is a gain.* → The pesticides raised the harvest and removed the pollinators, so the harvest now needs people doing the pollinating by hand.
- *Feeding more people means farming more land.* → Air protein and farmed mycelium produce protein on almost none of it, which turns the land question into a choice rather than a constraint.
- *The alternative to meat is giving up meat.* → A blend of mycelium and a little pork tests as indistinguishable, and carries fibre a sausage never had.

## What we learn

- **Watch the second-order effect.** The intervention worked exactly as intended and destroyed the free service underneath it. That pattern is the one to look for anywhere inputs are being increased.
- **Free services are invisible until they are gone.** Nobody priced pollination while the insects were doing it. The cost only became legible as wages.
- **Smart is not wise.** Producing more is an engineering result. Producing more without dismantling what produces it is a different discipline, and we do not have a track record in it yet.
- **Land freed is the real yield.** The case for these proteins is not the protein. It is the habitat that stops being needed, and the carbon that comes back with it.
- **Blending beats replacing.** The version people actually adopt is the one they cannot taste the difference in — a compromise that ships beats a purity that doesn't.
- **Pay for restoration.** If restoring balance is the work, it has to be someone's paid job, not a moral supplement to a different job.
- **Shade is a crop's requirement, not a concession.** Manuel plants forest back because the cacao needs it. The economics and the ecology point the same way, which is what makes it stick.

## Open questions

- The region, the crop and the pesticide history are described but not named or dated here. The hand-pollination of pears and apples is well documented in parts of China; confirm the specifics before repeating them.
- "Around 75% of cultivated crops need pollinators" is the familiar figure and is usually stated as a share of crop *types*, not of food volume by weight — which is much lower. Worth getting the framing exactly right.
- No numbers are given for air protein or mycelium: energy per kilogram of protein, cost at scale, or what the tanks are fed. Without those the land comparison is directional only.
- For us: this is the clearest argument yet for the food forest at Level 6 — a planted system that keeps its own pollinators is not a nice-to-have but the thing that stops a cell needing a workforce with pollen brushes.
