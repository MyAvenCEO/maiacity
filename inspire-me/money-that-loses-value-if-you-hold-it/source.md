Note: a DW Planet A report from the Chiemgau region of Bavaria. Below are the facts and claims
the report card is built on, in the order the piece makes them.

---

**The stickers.** Shops in the town and nearby towns display stickers saying you can pay in
Chiemgauer — a currency the people there invented, worth the same as the euro.

**Getting hold of it.** The reporter books a shared car (54 euros / 62 US dollars for the day)
and collects a one-off reward of 50 Chiemgauer. Normally you must register first and use
car-sharing ten times before the reward is paid. The office holds 200,000 Chiemgauer, worth
more than 230,000 US dollars. Fresh notes carry infrared and watermark protection like official
banknotes.

**The origin.** Christian Gelleri invented the currency and heads the organisation that
oversees it. Twenty-four years before filming, he taught economics at a regional high school;
he and a handful of students invented the Chiemgauer as an experiment in promoting local
business, at a time when local stores were struggling because customers preferred malls. Store
owners tried it, people began paying with it, and the classroom experiment became a micro
financial system.

**The legality.** Under German law, printing or using money other than euros can result in
prison. The notes were never authorised as legal tender. The federal bank accepted their use on
one condition — that it is regional money, valid only in the region and for a certain number of
people.

**Designed to be spent, not saved.** Every six months holders must buy a sticker costing a few
cents to keep a banknote valid, and after three years bills expire. A private person cannot
exchange Chiemgauer for euros. Businesses can, with a 5% deduction, which supports the
operation of the currency and local associations.

**Circulation, demonstrated.** Two beers are paid for with Chiemgauer; the pub owner can buy
bread at the market with the same notes; the market vendor pays her organic supplier, who also
accepts them. A postcard shop estimates 10–15% of its payments come in Chiemgauer. At a local
organic store, apples are bought with them, and the next customer pays the same way — she runs
an organic food store herself and often receives Chiemgauer as takings, then spends some on
personal purchases. Payment is possible in cash or electronically, with a card linked to a
normal bank account.

**Scale.** An estimated five million Chiemgauer are spent every year, by about 4,200
individuals and roughly 300 participating businesses.

**The wider family.** There are around 300 such "complementary" currencies globally, most of
them in Europe and Brazil — complementary because they sit alongside an official currency
rather than replacing it. Most are small. Brazil's Mumbuca is an exception, with tens of
millions of US dollars per year flowing into the local economy and welfare.

**Ester Barinaga**, who researches complementary currencies, notes that pushing local shopping
shortens supply chains, because businesses start sourcing locally produced goods. A few such
currencies focus mainly on the environment: Vilawatt rewards citizens for saving energy;
Plastic Bank issues tokens in Indonesia, the Philippines and elsewhere for collecting and
handing in plastic bottles. Her summary: money can be designed, and if it is created to reward
pro-environmental behaviour — an incentive system — more people will behave that way.

**The climate bonus.** The Chiemgauer has evolved from a local currency into one that rewards
climate-friendly behaviour: rewards for car-sharing, installing thermostats, having jeans
repaired, insulating a house with natural materials, or fitting a balcony or backyard solar
panel. One homeowner received 100 Chiemgauer for a balcony solar set that will save 11 tons of
carbon dioxide over 20 years. The scheme has expanded from this region to four more in Germany.

**How it is funded.** Rewards are paid for by other locals and local businesses who pay into a
fund to offset their CO₂ emissions — essentially a small emissions trading system. For each ton
of emissions offset, nine tons are saved through the rewarded behaviour. Over the past four
years this has saved 12,800 tons of CO₂, as much as 2,000 average cars emit in the same period.
Independent auditors have checked those numbers and say it works.

**The limits.** Clothing and technology are still produced abroad, shipped around the world and
sold locally. Fewer than one percent of locals use the Chiemgauer, and it has to stay that small
or Germany's federal bank could choose to regulate it.

**The conclusion drawn.** Regional currencies can be thought of as cleverly made vouchers going
round and round, with real effects on local economies and CO₂ emissions. Small in the grand
scheme, but the lesson is large: money and monetary systems are not untouchable. They can be
designed — for example, to cut emissions. And if it works with small money, it could work with
big money too.
