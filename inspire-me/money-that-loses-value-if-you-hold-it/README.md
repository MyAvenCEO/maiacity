## The story

There are stickers in the shop windows of this Bavarian town telling you that you can pay here in Chiemgauer.

Not euros. Chiemgauer — notes printed locally, worth one euro each, carrying watermarks and infrared protection like a real banknote. Under German law, printing or using money that isn't the euro can land you in jail. These notes were never authorised as legal tender. The federal bank simply accepted them, on one condition: they stay regional. Valid only here, for a limited number of people.

It began as homework.

Twenty-four years ago Christian Gelleri taught economics at a high school in the region, and he and a handful of students invented the Chiemgauer as an experiment in propping up local shops. The problem they were looking at is the ordinary one: customers were driving to malls, and the money they spent left the valley and did not come back. Shopkeepers, with nothing to lose, agreed to accept the classroom's money. People started paying with it. The experiment turned into a small financial system, and Gelleri now runs the organisation that oversees it.

The interesting part is not that they made money. It is what they designed into it.

Ordinary money rewards you for keeping it. This does the opposite. Every six months a Chiemgauer note has to have a sticker bought for it — a few cents — or it stops being valid, and after three years the note expires entirely. Hold on to it and it costs you. Spend it today and it costs you nothing.

You also cannot cash out. A private person holding Chiemgauer has one option: spend them, here. A business can convert back to euros, at a 5% deduction that funds the running of the currency and local associations. There is exactly one direction of travel, and it is around the valley.

So it circulates. A beer bought at the pub becomes bread the landlord buys at the market; the market trader pays her organic supplier with the same notes, because he takes them too. One shop reckons a tenth to a fifteenth of its takings come in this money. An organic store owner pays for her own apples with Chiemgauer earned in the shop she runs herself.

Roughly five million Chiemgauer are spent a year, and the whole thing rests on about 4,200 individuals and around 300 businesses. There are something like 300 complementary currencies in the world, most of them in Europe and Brazil — currencies designed to sit alongside a national one rather than replace it. Brazil's Mumbuca moves tens of millions of dollars a year into a local economy and its welfare system.

Ester Barinaga, who researches these systems, puts the whole lesson in four words: money can be designed. If you build it to reward a behaviour, you get more of that behaviour, because that is what an incentive system is.

Which is what the Chiemgauer did next. It stopped being only a local currency and started paying people for the climate. Fifty Chiemgauer for using a car-share ten times. A hundred for putting a solar panel on your balcony — a panel that will keep eleven tons of carbon dioxide out of the air over twenty years. Rewards for a thermostat, for natural insulation, for having your jeans repaired instead of replaced.

The money for those rewards comes from local people and local businesses paying into a fund to offset their own emissions, which makes it a very small emissions trading scheme with an unusual ratio: for every ton offset, nine more are saved by the behaviour the rewards buy. Over four years, 12,800 tons — roughly what two thousand average cars put out in the same period. Independent auditors have checked the figures, and the scheme has spread to four more German regions.

It is also, on its own terms, tiny. Fewer than one percent of locals use it. Clothes and electronics are still made on the other side of the world and shipped in, and no sticker on a banknote changes that. It has to stay small, because if it grew, the federal bank would have reason to regulate it.

Call them clever vouchers going round and round, and you would not be wrong. You would also be missing what a classroom in Bavaria proved by accident: the rules of money are not weather. Somebody wrote them, and somebody can write different ones.

## Beliefs that shift

- *Money is issued from above and the rest of us just use it.* → A teacher and a handful of students issued a currency that has run for two decades and 300 businesses accept.
- *Saving money should always be rewarded.* → A note that costs a few cents every six months to stay valid is a note that keeps moving, which is the point of it.
- *A currency's job is to store value.* → This one's job is to stay inside a valley and to buy behaviour — and it is audited on whether it does.

## What we learn

- **Design the decay, not just the issuance.** Demurrage — the sticker every six months, the three-year expiry — is what turns a token into circulation. Without it people hoard and the system stops.
- **Make the exit asymmetric.** People cannot convert back at all; businesses can, at 5%, which both funds the system and keeps the money local by default.
- **Scope is what makes it legal.** The federal bank tolerates it because it is regional and small. Legitimacy came from limiting reach, not from asking permission.
- **A currency can be an incentive system.** Once you accept that money is designed, paying people in it for car-sharing, insulation and balcony solar is an obvious next move rather than a strange one.
- **Leverage beats offsetting.** Nine tons saved for every ton offset is the number that matters — the fund is buying behaviour change, not absolution.
- **Get audited.** Independent verification of the 12,800 tons is why this reads as evidence rather than as a good story.
- **Small is a design constraint, not a failure.** It has to stay under one percent of locals to remain unregulated, which is an honest ceiling and worth naming out loud.

## Open questions

- "Twenty-four years ago" dates the founding relative to filming, not to a year. Confirm the launch date and the current figures before citing them.
- What happens at the boundary? A system that must stay under one percent of the population cannot be the answer for a city — so what is the version that survives being adopted by everyone, and does it stop being legal at that point?
- The 5% conversion fee is the whole revenue model. Is that enough to run the system without volunteer labour, and what would the same design cost at ten times the size?
- Demurrage punishes holding, which is fine for a currency you pay with — and wrong for one you own with. Worth reading against [Money is an agreement](/inspire-me/money-is-an-agreement) and against our own split between a currency that circulates and a currency that accumulates.
