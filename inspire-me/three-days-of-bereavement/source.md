Note: Simon Sinek's *A Bit of Optimism*, in conversation with psychotherapist Amy Morin, author
of *13 Things Mentally Strong People Don't Do* and *The Mental Strength Playbook*. Below are
the points the report card is built on, in the order the conversation makes them. Sponsor
segments are omitted.

---

**The paradox that opens it.** The mental health industry is booming and advice is everywhere,
yet anxiety and depression are rising. If the guidance being followed worked, the numbers would
be falling. Morin adds that she does not hear people saying they feel better for all the extra
conversation, and that in her practice people arrive on a quest to identify what happened to
them at seven in the hope of feeling better today.

**Health versus fitness.** Both prefer "mental fitness" to "mental health": health sounds like a
goal to be achieved or a state you have or lack, while fitness is a practice — sometimes ahead,
sometimes behind. Morin cites a CDC figure that roughly 17% of people are operating at optimal
mental health at any given moment, and notes that where a person falls varies through the day
depending on food, sleep and who they have been talking to.

**Feelings are not disorders.** Sadness, anger, frustration and overwhelm are life, not illness.
The problem is that an anxiety disorder shares its name with the feeling, so someone nervous
before a presentation concludes they have a disorder. People are not taught how to tolerate and
work through emotions, and phones become the escape.

**Cognitive behavioural work, in practice.** Morin describes a client with major depression
whose automatic thoughts ran to *I'm worthless, people don't like me, it's all my fault*,
resolving to a belief of being flawed. Less participation in life made him feel worse, which
made him participate less. The intervention included examining those thoughts (if someone
doesn't call back, what are ten other explanations?) and a written list of the top ten reasons
to go to work when he didn't want to, kept on his nightstand.

**The mental health day.** Staying in bed does not reduce depression. Coping strategies that
feel better in the moment create larger problems later; avoidance does not make the thing go
away, and Tuesday is usually harder than Monday.

**The therapy incentive.** Sinek raises a financial disincentive for therapists to discharge
clients, noting that insurance reimburses little while private pay is higher, so therapy has
shifted from treating mental illness to serving people processing ordinary difficulty. He
recounts his sister — who lost her husband young, felt genuinely better, and was told by her
therapist that stopping would be foolish. Morin accepts the pressure exists, notes it is not
unique to therapy, and gives the counter-case: someone with bipolar disorder having a good week
is not cured, and the responsible move is education, spacing sessions out, and leaving the door
open rather than either clinging or discharging abruptly.

**Her own losses.** Her mother died of a brain aneurysm at 51. Morin was 22 when she became a
therapist and 23 when her mother died; she took two weeks off. Her husband died of a heart
attack at 26, on the three-year anniversary to the day of her mother's death. Working at a
mental health agency, she received three days of bereavement leave. Short-term disability was
denied because grief is not covered; her primary care doctor then diagnosed acute stress
disorder — the precursor to PTSD, as she had been present when he died — which bought two
months. She returned to work because there was a mortgage on a house bought on the assumption
of two incomes, no wealthy family to fall back on, and the alternative was packing up the house
at the lowest point of her life. She decided instead to work two jobs. Later, shortly after
remarrying, her father-in-law was diagnosed with terminal cancer.

**Why she pushed.** As a therapist she had seen people arrive ten years after a loss asking why
they still did not feel better, having believed that time heals. She did not want to be that
person at 36, and describes it as a daily choice: let yourself feel awful and go through the
pain, while holding some hope that life could get better and asking what the next chapter
should look like.

**Compromise rather than all-or-nothing.** Not forbidding herself bed and not surrendering to
it: stay a little longer, then get up at eleven, go for a walk, call someone — because changing
the environment shifts the mood, and lying there until two in the afternoon does not improve
two in the afternoon.

**Deferring feelings.** Emotions arrive unscheduled — she describes being caught out in aisle
seven of a supermarket by one of her husband's favourite foods. When the timing is impossible,
the move is to say *this isn't helpful right now, I'll let myself feel that later*, and to do
something that restores function in the moment. Distraction is a legitimate skill used
occasionally and a trap used permanently; "staying busy" gets complimented but is avoidance.

**Solve the problem or solve the feeling.** The central distinction. Unopened bills are a
problem; the anxiety about them is a feeling, and Netflix solves the feeling while the bills
remain. Conversely, a partner who drinks too much is not your problem to solve — pouring out
the alcohol is an attempt to control someone else instead of managing your own anxiety. Morin's
formulation: if I can control you, then I don't have to control my feelings.

**Narrative.** For years her story was that she had lost her two favourite people in her
twenties and life would never be good again. At some point it became a possible comeback story,
which required getting up and building it: a motorcycle licence, and a decision to continue
fostering as a single parent. She is explicit that she is not grateful the events happened.
Sinek distinguishes a comeback built on your own life from one built on sabotaging someone
else's, and adds his own practice of balancing the equation — asking what a good thing cost,
and, after a reasonable interval, what a bad thing gave.

**Victimhood.** Being a victim (of a crime, abuse, a disaster) is distinct from playing the
victim. Sinek reports a social worker's observation that playing the victim is powerful and
feels good. Morin links it to self-pity: we underestimate ourselves, overestimate the
challenge, and tell ourselves we had no choice then and have none now — which licenses not
changing. She notes the explosion of "my ex was a narcissist" online against a very small
actual prevalence, and that some clients return each week to retell how awful things are rather
than to change anything. Both distinguish this from healthy short-term venting.

**Breaking patterns.** Recognise the pattern first; write it down by hand, which slows thinking
and engages the brain differently from typing. Look for when it started — as an origin story,
not a blame story — and ask whether you are trying to heal an old wound by reliving it. Morin's
rule: you will usually choose familiar pain over the unknown unless you get proactive. A trusted
friend who can say *you're doing it again* is valuable precisely because they are not inside
the emotion.

**Language.** Avoid the verb *to be*. *I am an anxious person* becomes *I have anxiety
sometimes*; *I'm avoidant* becomes *I push people away sometimes*. A label adopted is a label
lived up to; a tendency can be changed next time. Sinek adds that *sometimes* and *yet* at the
end of a sentence preserve hope.

**Doing versus feeling.** Mental health talk is about what you feel; mental strength is about
what you do. Waking up sad is not a verdict but a question — what will I do about it? Taking
action starts to help before it works. Morin also concedes that insight does not change
behaviour, citing her own scrolling before bed, which she knows costs her sleep and does
anyway. Sinek adds that the pain of staying the same has to begin to outweigh the fear of
change.

**Scheduled worry.** Research-supported, and one of her favourite exercises: fifteen minutes a
day, put in the calendar with an alarm, sitting at the kitchen table, writing down or simply
thinking through what you are worried about — and when the time is up, it is up. Clients who
actually do it look, within a couple of weeks, as though a weight has lifted. Sinek recounts a
man badly injured by a car while cycling who scheduled twenty minutes a day to feel sorry for
himself and hate his life, then went to physical therapy — and credits that with his recovery.

**Not suppression.** Mental toughness is commonly confused with shoving things down. Morin says
what is suppressed comes back when least expected — someone short-tempered about something
trivial, months after an event nobody connects it to, including them. The resilience narrative
of bouncing back the next day skips working through the pain.

**Social support.** One of the biggest indicators of how people emerge from difficulty. Sinek
describes his rule of no crying alone, and calling a friend simply to sit in the mud with him —
arguing that being invited to do that is an honour, and that declining to ask denies a friend
that honour. Morin agrees it is a muscle, and suggests starting small: ask to whine for five
minutes before you ask to cry.

**What changed in her.** She grew up extremely shy — she thinks she could have been diagnosed
with selective mutism, did not talk for years, and had friends who ordered for her in
restaurants, a habit that persists. Public speaking was her greatest fear. She gave the eulogy
at her husband's funeral, deciding she did not care how she sounded because it was not about
her. She says many of her insecurities are simply gone: having been through the worst of the
worst, a negative review no longer stings. She is not glad the events happened, but is glad to
have become someone braver, who takes more risks and does not need all the answers in advance.
