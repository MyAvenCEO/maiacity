## The story

Amy Morin was twenty-three and already working as a therapist when her mother died suddenly of a brain aneurysm at fifty-one. She took two weeks.

Three years later, to the day, her husband died of a heart attack. He was twenty-six. She was working at a mental health agency, and the agency gave her three days of bereavement leave. She applied for short-term disability and was denied, because grief is not a covered condition. Her own doctor eventually diagnosed acute stress disorder — the precursor to PTSD, because she had been there when he died — and that bought two months.

Then she went back to work, because there was a mortgage on a house bought on the assumption of two incomes, and no family able to cover it.

That is the position the rest of this comes from, and it is worth stating plainly, because the advice that follows is not academic.

The conversation opens on a paradox that Sinek puts bluntly: the mental health industry is booming, advice is everywhere, and anxiety and depression are going up. If what we were being told worked, something would be falling.

Morin's first move is the language. She will not say mental health, because health sounds like a state you either possess or lack, and nobody walks around announcing they are physically healthy. She says mental fitness — a thing you are always mid-practice on, sometimes ahead, sometimes behind. She cites a figure that only around 17% of people are operating at optimal mental health at any moment, and that where you land moves through the day depending on sleep, lunch, and who you have been talking to.

The second move is separating feelings from disorders. We gave the feeling and the illness the same word, so a person nervous before a presentation concludes they have an anxiety disorder. Everybody has anxiety. What almost nobody is taught is how to sit inside an unpleasant feeling long enough to work through it — which is why phones are so effective, and so bad for us.

Then comes the line that reorganises the whole episode. Ask, every time: am I trying to solve the problem, or solve how I feel about the problem?

Unopened bills on the table are a problem. The anxiety about them is a feeling. Watching Netflix solves the feeling perfectly, and the bills are still there tomorrow. Run it the other way and you get the person whose partner drinks too much, pouring out the bottles — trying to solve a problem that is not theirs to solve, because controlling someone else is easier than managing your own state. As she puts it: if I can control you, I don't have to control my feelings.

Almost every bad coping strategy turns out to be that substitution. The mental health day spent in bed feels better at nine in the morning and worse by Tuesday. Avoidance, ghosting, quitting — all of them work, briefly, which is exactly why they are so popular.

What she did instead was refuse the all-or-nothing version. Not *you may not stay in bed*, and not *stay in bed as long as you like*, but a negotiation: stay in bed a little longer, get up at eleven, go for a walk, because changing your environment changes your mood and lying there until two in the afternoon has never once produced a better two in the afternoon. There is grace in that, and it is the part people skip.

The same trick works on feelings themselves. She cannot cry between therapy clients, so the feeling gets deferred rather than denied — *this isn't helpful right now, I'll let myself feel it tonight* — and in the evening she does. The formal version is scheduled worry: fifteen minutes in the calendar, at the kitchen table, with an alarm. She says the clients who actually do it look, within a couple of weeks, like the weight has come off them. Sinek adds the version he heard from a cyclist badly hurt by a car, who booked twenty minutes a day to hate his life and then went to physical therapy.

None of that is suppression, which is the thing mental toughness usually gets confused with. Suppressed grief comes back four months later as a person shouting about something trivial, and nobody in the room — including them — connects the two.

The last piece is narrative, and it is the one with a grammar rule attached. Stop using the verb *to be*. Not *I am an anxious person* but *I have anxiety sometimes*. Not *I'm avoidant* but *I push people away sometimes*. A label you adopt is a label you live up to; a tendency is something you can do differently next time. The words *sometimes* and *yet* do the same work at the end of a sentence — they leave the door open.

Which is how she got from one story to another. For years the story was: you lost your two favourite people in your twenties and life will probably never be good again. At some point it became: this could be a comeback. She got a motorcycle licence. She decided to keep fostering, alone. Not gratitude — she is explicit that she is not grateful it happened — but a decision that the next chapter was going to have to be built rather than waited for.

And the thing that changed in her, the part she will admit she is glad about: she grew up so shy she thinks she could have been diagnosed with selective mutism, didn't speak for years, and had public speaking as her single greatest fear. She gave the eulogy at her husband's funeral, and stopped caring how she sounded, because it was not about her. Now a bad review does not reach her. She has been through the worst of the worst.

## The practices

| Situation | The move |
| --- | --- |
| Something is wrong and you want to act | Ask: solve the problem, or solve the feeling? Pick the right one |
| It is not your problem to solve | Manage your own state instead of controlling the other person |
| You want to stay in bed all day | Compromise — a bit longer, then up and out, environment changed |
| A feeling arrives at the wrong moment | Defer it, with a real time attached. Never deny it |
| Worry is constant and unfocused | 15 minutes a day, scheduled, in the calendar, with an alarm |
| You catch yourself labelling | Drop the verb *to be*. "I have…", "sometimes…", "…yet" |
| A pattern keeps repeating | Write it out by hand; look for the origin, not the blame |
| You feel awful and don't want to bother anyone | Call someone. No crying alone |

## Beliefs that shift

- *Mental health is a state you have or lack.* → It is a fitness you are always mid-practice on, and where you land moves through the day.
- *A mental health day means staying in bed.* → The coping strategy that feels best at nine in the morning is usually the one that makes Tuesday worse.
- *Feeling something painful means something is wrong with you.* → The feeling and the disorder share a name, and confusing them is what turns ordinary difficulty into a diagnosis.

## What we learn

- **Ask which one you are solving.** Almost every bad strategy is solving the feeling when the problem was solvable, or attacking the problem when only the feeling was yours to handle.
- **Compromise with yourself instead of commanding.** The instruction that gets followed is *get up at eleven*, not *get up now* and not *stay down*.
- **Defer feelings; never deny them.** A deferral with a real time attached is a skill. The same move without the appointment is suppression, and it returns.
- **Schedule the worry.** Fifteen minutes, in the calendar, with an alarm — including, if needed, scheduled self-pity before scheduled physiotherapy.
- **Cut the verb *to be*.** *I am anxious* becomes a person; *I have anxiety sometimes* stays a weather report. Adopt a label and you will live up to it.
- **Action precedes the mood.** Doing something — even reading for fifteen minutes — starts to help before it works, because it is what you do rather than what you feel.
- **Insight does not change behaviour.** She knows what the phone does at night and does it anyway. Knowing is not a strategy; the pain of staying the same has to outweigh the fear of change.
- **No crying alone.** Social support is among the strongest predictors of how people come out of hard times, and being asked to sit in the mud with someone is an honour rather than an imposition.

## Open questions

- The figure that around 17% of people are at optimal mental health is attributed to the CDC without a source. Worth locating before repeating.
- The claim of a financial disincentive for therapists to discharge clients is argued from anecdote by both speakers, who flag it as uncomfortable. It deserves actual data rather than two people agreeing.
- The narcissism figure floated in the conversation is far above the usual clinical estimates for the diagnosis. Check it.
- Scheduled worry is presented as research-backed, and the underlying technique is a standard one in cognitive behavioural therapy — but no study is named here.
- For us: mental fitness as a daily practice with a checklist is structurally the same claim as a city built one entry at a time in public. And the "solve the problem or the feeling" question is the sharpest thing in this list — it applies to a founder's week as directly as to a bad morning.
