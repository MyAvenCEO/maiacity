## The story

A founder puts months into a rebrand. New colours, new fonts, new photos, a sharper slogan. And it doesn't work. People drop by, nod and don't come back.

Darren, a Writers Guild Award-winning creator and producer, says the problem isn't the look. Social media is TV now. The founder brands people grow attached to behave like shows. They give the viewer a character to follow, a quest to root for, a tension that keeps the story moving, and plot lines that make the next episode feel necessary. Alex Hormozi, Codie Sanchez and Steven Bartlett each lead their own show. If a viewer can't tell what story they are following, a new font won't bring them back.

So he holds a production meeting and rebrands a made-up founder live.

Julie's line is: "I help women over 50 find clarity, confidence and purpose." The audience is clear and the subject is clear. Nobody feels anything.

First, he turns her expertise into a quest. Her posts are advice: five ways to find your purpose, three signs you're ready for a fresh start. Useful, and it only reports what she already knows. A quest is active. "What advice can I give about reinvention?" becomes "What does it actually take to build a fulfilling new chapter?" Her knowledge is still there, now on the move. A show lasts as long as its lead still wants something. Once there is nothing left to move toward, the show ends.

Second, he gives her a role. Not a persona copied from three other creators in her niche, but the part she can naturally play for her viewer. YouTube, he says, is a scene for two people in which you never see your co-star. Darren reads Julie as part underdog, part problem solver. Her line becomes: "At 52, I'm rebuilding the life I thought I would have figured out by now, and helping other women design their next chapter alongside me." Now she has a backstory, a quest and a place beside the viewer instead of on a mountaintop. The first line told us what she sells. The second gives us someone to follow.

Third, he gives her a tension: one unresolved question she can come back to in every episode. How do you build a bold new chapter without blowing up the life you worked so hard to create? Now he wants to see what she tries, what works, and whether the life she imagines is possible at all. If she would rather not share that much, the tension can come from outside: a world that keeps telling women over 50 to slow down, and makes her want to prove it wrong.

That is why he plans plot lines, not content pillars. Julie's old pillars were reinvention, confidence, purpose, career and relationships, a tidy list nobody binges. His own tension is that YouTube has become TV, so experts now have to learn to build shows. It gives him something new to investigate every week, and this video is an episode of it.

Rebranding doesn't mean becoming someone else, or changing your logo or your hair. Give people a character to follow, a quest to root for and a reason to wonder what happens next, and you have more than a rebrand. You have a show.

## Beliefs that shift

- *A rebrand is a new look and a better slogan.* → If people can't tell which story they are following, the new look won't bring them back. The rebrand that works is a better plot.
- *Expertise means posting what you know.* → Advice reports. A quest moves. Turn the subject into a question you are still working on, and every post becomes the next episode.
- *Content pillars keep a brand clear.* → Pillars are a list. Plot lines each carry a tension and an open question, so the viewer comes back to see what happens.

## What we learn

- **We already have the lead, and the role fits his advice.** The storyteller's episode format casts Samuel as a leader setting out, "never the lonely genius, never the guru". That matches Darren's "not preaching from a mountaintop". His addition is the word "alongside": the viewer is the co-star, and for us that means the founders, named in the episode.
- **One open question for the whole show.** Our storyteller rules give every film one arching question. Darren adds one more above them, asked again in every episode. Day 19's cover line already sounds like one: a city of tomorrow that feeds itself, starting with 233 settlers. Can a handful of founders really build it? That question doesn't close after one episode.
- **Turn our topics into plot lines.** Domes, the food forest, the films, avenDB, theOS and the avens are pillars as long as each is a topic. Each becomes a plot line once it carries its own open question. Will a dome hold 24 °C through a Munich winter? Will the avens stop dying world after world? Will the founders' vaults stay theirs alone?
- **A brand line in his shape.** Backstory, quest, role, alongside. For example: "In a 14 m² room in Munich, I'm building the city I wish existed, and inviting you to found it with me." That is only a draft, and Samuel should rewrite it until every word is true.
- **Names are the font, not the plot.** The game's top bar says avenCITY, the Mac app is called The OS, the paper is theOS and the site is maiaCITY. Each of those names can change. Viewers should recognise the same quest under every one.
- **Show the attempts, including the ones that fail.** He wants to see what Julie tries and what doesn't work. Our world analyses (the avens' deaths, the frozen prices, the fixes) are exactly that kind of episode, and the devlog half of each day can tell them as wins and setbacks, not as release notes.
