Note: Just Have a Think on Sun-Ways' railway solar trial and the land-use problem behind it.
Below are the points the report card is built on, in the order the video makes them. Patreon
credits at the end are omitted.

---

**The installation.** A railway line near the village of Buttes in western Switzerland, carrying
ordinary trains. Between the rails sit 48 photovoltaic panels, forming what the developers
claim is the world's first removable solar power station installed directly onto an operational
railway. Since the trial began in April 2025, more than 11,000 trains have passed over them
without causing significant problems.

**The company and the scale.** Developed by Sun-Ways. A demo plant only, 100 metres long, with
a peak generating capacity of about 18 kW — described as roughly enough to boil six ordinary
electric kettles simultaneously.

**The compromises.** Unlike a conventional solar farm the panels do not sit on angled frames;
they are laid horizontally between the rails, so they generate less than optimally angled
panels. They are subject to vibration, brake dust, dirt, snow, ballast and several hundred
tonnes of train passing just above them. Part of the solution was making them easily removable
for track maintenance, achieved by developing specialist railway machinery that installs and
removes them mechanically so conventional maintenance crews can reach the track.

**Swiss potential, as stated by Sun-Ways.** 5,000 km of rail track in Switzerland, with an
exploitable area of about 5 million square metres — equating to 2.5 million panels, enough to
produce just over one terawatt-hour a year, about 20% of Switzerland's total solar PV
generation.

**Reported trial results.** No vibration cracking of the panels, no significant damage from
ballast, no reported glare problems for train drivers. Airflow from passing trains apparently
provides some incidental cleaning, and Sun-Ways has also developed brushes that can be attached
beneath trains.

**Interest elsewhere.** Italy is considering its own pilot project, with interest reported in
France and South Korea. A 2024 academic study investigated applying the idea to Britain's
railways and calculated the total surface area occupied by railway tracks across the UK at
about 56 million square metres — while noting that tunnels, stations, junctions, points and
shaded sections would rule out large parts of the network. The point of the study was to
identify an enormous artificial surface that already exists and ask whether some of it could
perform a second job.

**Why it matters: land.** Meeting decarbonisation targets will need a quantity of solar that
makes today's capacity look tiny, and all of it has to go somewhere. The obvious solution is
large solar farms on large, relatively flat, unwooded land — which is generally what we call
farms.

**USDA findings.** Looking at utility-scale solar installed in rural America between 2009 and
2020, more than 70% of large-scale commercial solar projects in rural areas had been built on
agricultural land, either cropland or pasture; 43% were on cropland alone. The total farmland
affected by solar remains very small compared with the vast area devoted to agriculture, so
there is no evidence of solar farms consuming the world's food supply. But of the agricultural
sites where solar farms were installed, while surrounding agricultural land typically remained
in agriculture, about 15% shifted out of agricultural use following installation — and the USDA
concluded there is already evidence of some direct competition between farmland and solar
farms.

**A global study.** Published in *Earth's Future* in 2025, mapping existing ground-mounted
photovoltaic installations against historical land-use data, and estimating that between 13%
and 16% of existing ground-mounted PV installations worldwide were associated with land changing
from cropland to non-cropland use. Again not an impending food catastrophe, but enough to
establish that the conflict is real.

**Indirect land-use change.** The IPCC's term for the secondary effect: solar displaces
agricultural activity, that agriculture has to go somewhere else, and in some cases that means
clearing forestry land or damaging another ecosystem to create new space. The same controversy
that already exists with biofuels — though the video is explicit that it is not conflating the
two, since producing a unit of useful energy from solar PV requires vastly less land than
growing bioenergy crops. The underlying problem is the same: land is finite, and we are asking
it to do an increasing number of things.

**Agrivoltaics.** Panels can be raised above crops, spaced further apart, or integrated into
pasture so animals graze beneath them, often enjoying the shade. In hotter, drier environments
that shading has been shown to reduce evaporation and water stress. So the choice does not have
to be food or electricity — sometimes it can be both.

**Built surfaces.** The other option, which the railway exemplifies: find ways of putting solar
panels on things already built. Rooftops are the obvious example, plus car park canopies,
canals, reservoirs, balconies, and motorway infrastructure such as noise barriers, which
researchers in South Korea have been experimenting with. Described as lateral thinking in the
spirit of Edward de Bono.

**The industry's pinch point.** The panel itself is increasingly not the expensive part. The
challenge now is everything around it: planning permission, grid connections, land acquisition,
installation and maintenance — and finding somewhere socially and environmentally acceptable to
put them.

**The honest assessment.** None of this means bolting panels between railway tracks is
necessarily a great solution. The Swiss panels operate in one of the most mechanically hostile
environments imaginable. They get dirty. They sit horizontally and so produce less energy than
optimally angled panels. They need specialist installation equipment. They have to be removable
for track maintenance. And they will almost certainly cost more per kilowatt-hour than panels
in a conventional solar farm. The real test is not whether they work — the trial has shown they
can — but whether the additional engineering and maintenance costs can be made low enough to
justify putting them there, which this small demo has not yet answered.

**The reframe.** Perhaps that is the wrong question. Arguably the comparison needed is not
between railway solar and the cheapest existing solar farms, but between railway solar and a
solar farm that needs new land — land that has to be bought or leased, that might currently
produce food, that might support wildlife, and that somebody somewhere might rather was not
covered with solar panels.

**The closing point.** Thanks largely to the volume ramp-up of solar manufacturing and
development in China, making modules cheaply enough to compete with fossil generation has been
cracked in large parts of the world. The big hurdle now is deploying them at enormous scale
without creating a whole new set of conflicts. So perhaps the future is not vast fields of
panels, but panels hidden in plain sight — on the roofs above us, the car parks around us, the
barriers beside the roads, and even underneath the trains we are travelling on. All of those
applications, and others not yet thought of, are judged well worth pursuing aggressively.
