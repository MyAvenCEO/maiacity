## The story

A hundred metres of track near Buttes, in western Switzerland, with forty-eight solar panels lying flat between the rails.

Every argument against it is obvious and correct. Horizontal panels generate less than angled ones. The environment is about as hostile as glass can face: vibration, brake dust, ballast, snow, and several hundred tonnes of train passing overhead. They have to come out whenever the track needs maintenance, which meant building special machinery to lift and replace them. Whatever a kilowatt-hour costs from this, it costs more than one from a field.

Since the trial started in April 2025, more than eleven thousand trains have run over them without significant trouble. No vibration cracking, no meaningful ballast damage, no glare complaints from drivers — and the airflow from passing trains turns out to clean them a little, with under-train brushes developed to do the rest.

So it works. The question the piece is actually chasing is why anybody would want it to.

Because solar panels are now among the cheapest electricity humanity has ever built, which should make this whole exercise pointless. Except the panel has quietly stopped being the expensive part. What costs now is planning permission, grid connection, land acquisition, installation, maintenance — and finding somewhere that is socially and environmentally acceptable to put the things.

Which brings you to the ground underneath.

The obvious way to deploy solar at scale is to find large, flat, unwooded land. Large, flat, unwooded land is what we normally call a farm. The US Department of Agriculture looked at utility-scale solar built in rural America between 2009 and 2020 and found that more than seventy percent of it went onto agricultural land, with forty-three percent on cropland alone. Of those agricultural sites, about fifteen percent shifted out of agriculture entirely after installation.

A global study published in *Earth's Future* in 2025 landed in the same territory, estimating that somewhere between thirteen and sixteen percent of ground-mounted PV worldwide sits on land that moved from cropland to something else.

None of that is a food catastrophe, and the piece is careful to say so — the total farmland involved is tiny beside the area farmed worldwide. What it establishes is that the conflict is real, and that it has a second-order version the IPCC calls indirect land-use change: displace agriculture and it reappears somewhere else, sometimes where a forest used to be. The same argument that has followed biofuels around for twenty years, though solar produces vastly more useful energy per hectare than bioenergy crops ever will.

There are two honest answers to that. One is agrivoltaics — raise the panels, space them out, graze underneath, and in hot dry places let the shade cut evaporation and water stress, so the land does both jobs.

The other is the one the railway is an instance of: stop looking for new ground, and put panels on surfaces that already exist. Roofs, car park canopies, canals, reservoirs, balconies, motorway noise barriers. A 2024 study asked what Britain's railways would offer and put the total track surface at about fifty-six million square metres — not usable, most of it, between tunnels, stations, junctions and shade, but the exercise was to notice an enormous artificial surface already sitting there and ask whether it could hold down a second job.

Switzerland's own version of that sum: five thousand kilometres of track, roughly five million square metres exploitable, two and a half million panels, a little over a terawatt-hour a year — about a fifth of the country's entire solar output.

And then the sentence that reframes everything, which is worth stealing whole.

The comparison people reach for is railway solar against the cheapest existing solar farm, and by that measure it loses badly. But that is the wrong pairing. The real comparison is railway solar against a solar farm that needs *new land* — land that has to be bought or leased, that might be growing food, that might be holding wildlife, that somebody would rather was not covered in glass.

Judged that way, an expensive kilowatt-hour from a surface nobody wanted anyway starts to look like a different kind of bargain.

Manufacturing got solved. Deployment did not. The future probably is not shimmering fields of panels — it is panels hidden in plain sight, on the roofs above, the car parks around, the barriers beside the road, and underneath the train you are sitting on.

## Beliefs that shift

- *Solar is cheap, so deployment is a solved problem.* → The module is cheap; permission, connection, land and acceptability are what now set the pace.
- *An expensive kilowatt-hour is a bad kilowatt-hour.* → Not if the cheap one requires ground that was growing food, and the expensive one requires ground nobody was using.
- *Solar farms don't threaten farmland.* → The totals are small, but more than seventy percent of US rural utility solar went onto agricultural land, and a measurable share of it never came back.

## What we learn

- **Compare against the real alternative.** The whole argument turns on refusing the flattering comparison. Measure a hard option against the option you would otherwise be forced into, not against the easiest one on the board.
- **Look for surfaces already built.** Roofs, canopies, canals, noise barriers, track beds — the search is for an artificial area that could hold down a second job, not for empty land.
- **Land is the binding constraint, not generation.** Once you accept that, the design question changes from *where is the sun best* to *what is this ground already doing*.
- **Removability is a feature, not a compromise.** The panels had to come out for maintenance, so machinery to lift them became part of the product. Designing for the incumbent use is what made it permissible at all.
- **Hostile environments clean themselves sometimes.** Airflow from passing trains does part of the job for free — worth looking for the incidental benefit in any awkward siting.
- **Watch for indirect land-use change.** Displaced farming does not evaporate; it moves, and sometimes into forest. The second-order effect is where the real cost hides.
- **Two answers, not one.** Agrivoltaics makes one piece of ground do both jobs; built-surface solar avoids needing the ground at all. Both, aggressively.

## Open questions

- "World's first removable solar power station on an operational railway" is a developer claim, and this is a hundred-metre demo. Durability over years, not eleven thousand trains, is the open question.
- Neither the 2024 UK railway study nor the 2025 *Earth's Future* paper is named here. Track both down before citing the 56 million m² or the 13–16% figure.
- No cost per kilowatt-hour is given for the railway installation, which makes the central trade-off unquantified. Without that number the reframing is persuasive but not yet decidable.
- For us: our dome cell already budgets every hectare — domes, food forest, glass, paths and water — so the land conflict this video describes is one we have designed ourselves into. Worth asking which of our surfaces could hold a second job, and whether the Solar dome factory should be making panels for roofs and paths rather than for fields.
