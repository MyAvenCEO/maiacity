## The story

Air moves moisture up to 100 times faster than vapor can seep through a wall by itself.

That one number undoes the most repeated piece of advice in natural building: "your house needs to breathe."

For a long time, houses got away with leaking. They were drafty, heated by wood stoves blasting dry heat through every room, and many of them were masonry. Whatever moisture got into a wall simply dried out again.

Then came forced-air heating and cooling, running on electricity or gas — on money. In a leaky house, that paid-for air poured out through every crack. So builders sealed the houses, and the energy drain stopped.

The seal brought a new failure. The first air barriers were plastic sheets, closed to vapor on the cold side, and nobody could install them perfectly. Every wire, pipe and outlet needed a cut. Warm, humid indoor air rushed through those small slits, hit the cold framing, and turned to liquid water — trapped behind the plastic, with no way to dry.

So "breathe" came back as the cure. Christina Ransbury, who designs resilient homes and trained as a Passive House designer, calls it her biggest pet peeve.

A house does breathe — through its lungs: the heat- or energy-recovery ventilator that brings fresh air in and pushes stale air out, or simply an open window. The walls, roof and floor are something else. They are the skin.

Skin has no open gaps; a leak there is a medical emergency. It keeps bath water out, and it still lets you sweat. That is the building envelope she wants, taken from building scientist Joe Lstiburek's "perfect wall": the structure inside, the three control layers for water, air and vapor wrapped around it like skin, then the insulation around that like a wool sweater, and the cladding over everything like a leather jacket.

Then the order of the work. Water first: roofs, big overhangs, water-resistant barriers, every flashing lapped like shingles — and always assume it fails somewhere. Air second, which matters more than insulation, for moisture and for energy. Vapor third: seal the ceiling strictly, because warm vapor rises; leave the walls vapor-open, or at least semi-open on one side. Insulation last, and outside the structure, so the frame stays warm and no cold surface is left where water can condense.

The wall that lasts for centuries is not the one that leaks. It is the one that can always dry.

## Beliefs that shift

- *A house needs to breathe through its walls.* → A house breathes through ventilation; its walls need to be airtight and able to dry.
- *Sealing a house is what makes it rot.* → Unplanned air leaks through a badly placed plastic seal make it rot; a continuous air barrier with the vapor control in the right place keeps it dry.
- *Insulation is the first thing that makes a home efficient.* → Water control comes first, then air sealing, then vapor, then insulation — in that order.

## What we learn

- **Ventilation is the lungs, the envelope is the skin.** Don't ask a wall to do a ventilator's job.
- **Air is the moisture carrier.** Air leaks move up to 100 times more moisture than diffusion, so air sealing matters more than insulation.
- **Four layers, in order.** Liquid water, air, vapor, heat — design each, in that sequence.
- **Insulate outside the structure.** It keeps the frame warm, so condensation never forms on it — easier than drying a wall afterwards.
- **Lock the top and the bottom.** A capillary break and vapor control under the slab (foam glass gravel does it without plastic), strict vapor control at the ceiling; then the walls can stay vapor-open.
- **Natural walls want natural skins.** Straw bale, hempcrete or rammed earth get lime or clay plaster, never cement stucco; inside, lime wash, clay paint or high-perm latex, never oil or epoxy paint.
- **Smart membranes switch with the season.** In mixed climates they close against winter humidity and open in summer so the wall can dry inward.

## Open questions

- She says a house chasing "breathability" can lose 40% of its energy bill to leaks — the source gives no study behind that figure.
- How does this translate to a glass geodesic dome, where the skin is the glass and the frame sits inside it — where is the condensing plane, and how does a dome dry?
- Foam glass gravel under the slab: availability, embodied energy and cost compared with a plastic vapor barrier.
- Vapor-smart membranes are plastic too — which natural or low-plastic options match them in a mixed climate?
