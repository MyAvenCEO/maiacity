## The story

Dan Gilbert had twenty-one minutes to give a room a concept they had never heard of, and he did not say its name until he was most of the way through.

He opened somewhere else entirely: two million years, and a brain that nearly tripled in mass over them — from the pound-and-a-quarter brain of Homo habilis to the roughly three pounds everyone in the room was carrying. Then a question, not a claim. What is it about a big brain that nature was so eager for every one of us to have one?

That question is the whole technique in miniature. A curious mind opens. A mind being told things does not.

He laid the first block: the prefrontal cortex, the new part that came with all that size. Then the second, and this is where it starts working — the prefrontal cortex runs an *experience simulator*. Pilots train in flight simulators so they don't make real mistakes in real planes; people can run an experience in their heads before living it.

The metaphor does something a definition cannot. It hands the mind a shape it already recognises, so the new thing has somewhere to sit.

Then one example, and only one. Ben & Jerry's has no liver-and-onion ice cream, and not because somebody made a batch and tasted it. You can simulate that flavour from your armchair and reject it without leaving the chair.

At that point the audience is comfortable, which is exactly when he breaks it.

He asks them to simulate two futures — winning the lottery, or losing the use of their legs — and to say which they would prefer. Then he shows a chart of how happy each group is, and it looks exactly right. Then he tells them he invented that chart. Here is the real one: a year on, lottery winners and paraplegics report roughly equal happiness.

Now the room is confused, and the confusion is the point. There is a hole between what they believe and what they have just been shown, and a hole like that is uncomfortable enough that people will sit through an explanation to close it.

He closes it by naming the thing. *Impact bias* — the tendency of the simulator to run badly, and to tell us that different futures will feel far more different than they turn out to be. A named mystery is a believable mystery. The studies follow: elections won and lost, relationships gained and lost, promotions, exams — all of them shorter and weaker in effect than anyone predicts. And a finding he says almost floors him, that major life traumas older than three months, with few exceptions, show no measurable effect on happiness at all.

Only now, with every block underneath them, does he say the idea: happiness can be synthesised. We run something like a psychological immune system — largely unconscious machinery that adjusts our view of the world so we can feel better in the world we actually got. A second metaphor, doing the same job as the first. An immune system protects the body from infection; this protects peace of mind.

Nothing about that sentence would have landed in minute one. It lands in minute fifteen because there is a tower under it, and each block was made recognisable by a metaphor and proved by an example.

The reason most explanations skip the tower has a name too. Robin Hogarth called it the curse of knowledge: once you know a thing, you cannot reconstruct what it was like not to know it. So you start at the top, where you live, and the audience never gets off the ground.

## From the ground up

- **Curiosity.** The state where a mind is reaching for something instead of receiving it. You open with a question or a strange fact, not a thesis, because everything that follows only sticks to a mind that is already leaning forward.
- **A building block.** One concept the big idea depends on. If your idea needs three things understood first, those are three blocks, and they have to go down in an order where each one rests on the one before it.
- **A metaphor.** Something the listener already understands, handed over as the shape of the new thing — a flight simulator for an experience simulator, an immune system for synthesised happiness. A definition tells the mind what something is. A metaphor tells it what to file the thing next to.
- **An example.** One concrete case that proves a block. Liver-and-onion ice cream does more work than a paragraph of theory because it happens in the listener's own head while they read it.
- **A knowledge gap.** The hole that opens when someone is shown a fact that does not fit what they believe. It is deliberately uncomfortable, and that discomfort is what buys you the next three minutes.
- **Naming.** Giving the unexplained thing a label — *impact bias* — before you fully explain it. A named mystery feels solvable; an unnamed one feels like a mistake in the argument.
- **The curse of knowledge.** Once you know something, you cannot remember not knowing it, so you skip the blocks that feel obvious to you and invisible to everyone else. Assume every block you find obvious is missing from your audience.

## The steps

1. Write the one idea you want the audience to leave with, in a single sentence. This sentence does not appear until near the end.
2. Write down what you believe the audience already knows. Be honest and be generous with how little that is.
3. List every concept that sits between the two. Those are your blocks.
4. Order the blocks so each one only needs what came before it. If a block needs something later in the list, the order is wrong.
5. Give every block a metaphor drawn from something ordinary the audience already handles — a machine, a body, a room, a tool.
6. Give every block exactly one concrete example. One strong example beats three weak ones, and three makes the block feel like the point.
7. Open on curiosity: a question, a number that does not fit, a fact that shouldn't be true. Never open on the idea itself.
8. Lay the blocks in order, each with its metaphor and its example, until the audience is comfortable.
9. Break the comfort on purpose. Show the data or the case that contradicts what they now expect — this is where you open the knowledge gap.
10. Name the thing before you explain it. The label is what turns confusion into a question worth waiting out.
11. Deliver the big idea, with its own metaphor. It should feel like something they nearly arrived at themselves.
12. Show it in action, then say what changes now that they can see it.

## Beliefs that shift

- *If I say it clearly enough, they will understand it.* → Clarity at the top of a tower is useless to somebody standing on the ground.
- *Examples and metaphors are decoration on top of the explanation.* → They are the explanation. The metaphor gives a new idea its shape, and the example is what proves the block holds.
- *Confusing the audience is a failure.* → A deliberate gap between what they expect and what is true is the engine that carries them to the end.

## What we learn

- **Curiosity before content.** A mind that is not reaching for something will not retain what it is handed.
- **One block at a time, in dependency order.** Understanding is built like a tower; you cannot place a block in mid-air.
- **Metaphor is a filing instruction.** It tells the mind what the new thing is like, so it has somewhere to live.
- **One example per block.** The armchair test — if the listener can run it themselves while hearing it, it has landed.
- **Name it before you finish explaining it.** A label converts an uncomfortable mystery into a solvable one, and buys patience.
- **Withhold the idea until the tower is up.** The same sentence that would bounce in minute one lands in minute fifteen.
- **Audit for the curse of knowledge.** The blocks you find too obvious to mention are the exact ones your audience is missing.

## Open questions

- The claim that major life traumas older than three months show no measurable impact on happiness is very strong and stated without a citation here. Track down the study and its exceptions before repeating it.
- "The curse of knowledge" is credited to Robin Hogarth in this piece; the term is usually traced to Camerer, Loewenstein and Weber's 1989 paper, which itself credits Hogarth. Worth getting the attribution right.
- Synthesised happiness is Gilbert's own research area, so this piece is teaching explanation *and* advocating the idea being explained. Read the underlying work before treating the example as settled science.
- How much of this transfers from twenty-one minutes on a stage to a written post, where the reader controls the pace and can leave at any line? Our own bet is that the knowledge gap matters more in writing, not less.
