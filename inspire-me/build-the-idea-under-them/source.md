Note: a TED-Ed piece that uses Dan Gilbert's TED talk on synthesised happiness as a worked
example of how to explain a difficult idea. Below are the points the report card is built on,
in the order the piece makes them.

---

**The task.** Explaining a complicated idea so someone can understand it is one of the ways
society moves forward. Harvard psychologist Dan Gilbert arrived at TED with twenty-one minutes
to explain "synthesised happiness" and why it makes us predict our own futures wrongly.

**The opening.** Gilbert starts on evolutionary time: two million years is nothing
evolutionarily, and yet in two million years the human brain nearly tripled in mass — from the
pound-and-a-quarter brain of Homo habilis to the almost three pounds everyone in the room has.
He ends with a question rather than a claim: what is it about a big brain that nature was so
eager for every one of us to have one? The piece names this as the first step of a successful
explanation — once a mind is curious, it opens.

**Block one: the prefrontal cortex.** Tripling in size did not just make the brain bigger; it
gained new structures, notably the frontal lobe and the prefrontal cortex. Gilbert asks what it
does that justifies rebuilding the human skull in the blink of evolutionary time.

**Block two: the experience simulator.** Among other things, the prefrontal cortex runs an
experience simulator. The metaphor offered: pilots practise in flight simulators so they don't
make real mistakes in real planes; humans can have experiences in their heads before trying
them in life. Described as an adaptation on a level with opposable thumbs, upright posture and
language.

**The example.** Ben & Jerry's has no liver-and-onion ice cream, and not because they made a
batch and tasted it. Without leaving your armchair you can simulate the flavour and reject it.

**The turn.** Gilbert asks the audience to simulate two futures — winning the lottery, or
becoming paraplegic — and say which they would prefer. He shows a chart of how happy each group
is that matches expectations, then reveals he made that chart up. The real data: a year after
losing the use of their legs and a year after winning the lottery, the two groups are equally
happy with their lives.

**The knowledge gap.** The audience is now confused, missing information they need to make
sense of what they have been shown. The piece names this state a knowledge gap, and treats it
as productive rather than a failure.

**Naming: impact bias.** Gilbert and colleagues study impact bias — the tendency of our
experience simulators to work badly, making us believe different experiences will be more
different than they turn out to be. Putting a name on the mystery makes it more believable and
raises curiosity further.

**The evidence.** From field and laboratory studies: winning or losing an election, gaining or
losing a romantic partner, getting or not getting a promotion, passing or failing a college
test — all have far less impact, less intensity and much shorter duration than people expect.
A recent study on major life traumas is cited as suggesting that if the event happened more
than three months ago, with only a few exceptions, it has no impact on happiness at all.

**The big idea.** Happiness can be synthesised. Sir Thomas Browne wrote in 1642 that he could
convert poverty to riches and adversity to prosperity — and the machinery he describes is the
machinery everyone has. Human beings have what might be thought of as a psychological immune
system: largely non-conscious cognitive processes that change our view of the world so we can
feel better about the world we find ourselves in. Metaphor: as the immune system protects the
body from infection, synthesised happiness protects peace of mind.

**The method, summarised.** Gilbert did not explain the idea in one leap; he got there piece by
piece, building on prefrontal cortex, experience simulator and impact bias, with metaphors and
examples strengthening each step. Understanding is described as a tower where each new block of
information supports the next, and metaphors show the mind the shape of each new idea so it
knows how to recognise it.

**The questions to ask when preparing.** What do you think your audience already knows? What
building blocks do you need to explain your idea? What metaphors and examples will help
listeners understand things they are not yet familiar with?

**The curse of knowledge.** Economist Robin Hogarth's term, cited here: we find it hard to
remember what it feels like not to know something we ourselves know. The piece calls this
possibly the most important requirement for clear communication — you cannot give a powerful
new idea to an audience unless you can work out how to explain it, step by step, fuelled by
curiosity.
