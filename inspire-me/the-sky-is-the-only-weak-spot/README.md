## The story

From the surface, this house does not exist. No roof, no facade, no windows. Just ground, and a circle cut into it.

The channel behind it has spent two years on courtyard houses: the Chinese siheyuan, the Spanish patio, the Persian chahar bagh, the Rajasthani haveli, more than a dozen others. Every one keeps the street out and turns its rooms toward a private garden. And every one stands above ground, so at least one face still meets the weather, a temperature the walls can soften but never escape.

That is the old deal of every house. The walls are a defence line, and insulation, air conditioning and heating are the troops. A tiny stick-frame home in a hot, dry climate runs its air conditioning from May to September and its heating from November to March, for $2,800 to $4,600 a year.

So the host follows the courtyard idea one step further, down.

Two to four metres below the surface, the ground sits close to the place's average air temperature and barely moves with the seasons. Where the year averages 18 °C, the air swings from 45 °C in summer to 5 °C in winter, and the earth at three metres stays at 18. A room buried there never has to climb from 5 to 21 or fall from 45 to 21. It only has to find three degrees.

Then the shape. A ring of rooms around a round courtyard, ten metres across outside, three and a half deep. The circle wraps the most floor in the least wall, has no corners pushing out into less stable ground, and leaves no dead air in the courtyard.

The build is five stages: dig the pit, pour a curved concrete ring wall 200 to 250 mm thick, form the rooms inside it with an arched opening each, waterproof everything, then put the dug earth back over the roofs. Only the courtyard stays open.

Down there the earth brings its own problems, one after another.

Groundwater first: above four metres it pushes through any membrane in time, so the site is chosen by its water table before anything else, and waterproofing is the one line in the budget not to cut. The curved formwork adds $8,000 to $18,000, unless curved concrete blocks take its place and save $5,000 to $12,000. Rain falls into a hole that has nowhere to drain, so it needs a gravity outlet down the slope or a pump. The back of each room is dark, so a 400 mm skylight goes through the earth roof. And many codes don't know what to do with a home below grade, until the courtyard is sized to count as light and escape.

What's left after all that is a house with one opening to the sky, and a fig tree in it.

The tree stands at the exact centre, the same distance from every room. Its leaves shade the courtyard in summer and drop to let the low winter sun in. The ring wall shelters the courtyard from wind, so the cool from the leaves, the ground cover and a small fountain stays put instead of blowing away. There are no corridors. To get from the bedroom to the kitchen you step outside, under the sky, past the tree.

The host counts seven passive mechanisms working at once, none of them using electricity, and puts the heating and cooling bill at $400 to $900 a year. It still costs $234,000 to $570,000 to build, and he asks openly whether this is the most correct home that the fewest people will ever build.

The house doesn't fight the weather. It sits where the weather can't reach, and lets in only the sky, through a garden.

## Beliefs that shift

- *Comfort comes from better insulation and better machines.* → Three metres of earth already sits at the year's average temperature; a home inside it only has to find the last few degrees.
- *A courtyard is a feature you can walk around.* → With no corridors, the courtyard is the hallway, the dining room and the garden, crossed every time you change rooms.
- *Underground means dark and damp.* → It means a site chosen for its water table, waterproofing done once and done right, drainage planned, and light from a sky framed by a garden.

## What we learn

- **Earth is the cheapest climate control there is, but only to the local average.** In a hot, dry climate the earth gives 18 °C for free. In Munich it gives about 10 °C, so a buried room still has to find eleven degrees, not three. Our 150 m dome is held at 24 °C and models 1.75 GWh of heat a year; buried rooms along its rim would start from 10 °C instead of a Bavarian winter.
- **We already put the cold side against something stable.** Every dome closes its north 120° sector in hemp up to three-quarters of its height. His house does the same with earth on every side but the sky, and a north band dug into a slope or bermed with the dug soil could add the earth's mass to the hemp's insulation.
- **A dome is a courtyard with a glass sky.** His one weak spot is the open sky, and a garden moderates it. In maiaCITY the dome glazes that sky and the food forest moderates it. Rooms in a ring that open onto the forest, with no corridors, are the same plan at a bigger scale; the Small dome already holds six rooms for twelve people.
- **Use the dug earth twice.** He puts the pit's soil back over the roofs. Our tunnels and the underground ring road at tower stage need 1.5 m or more of soil on top; earth-covered rooms beside them can share the dig, the clay seal and the drainage.
- **Build like the tunnels: in compression.** He chooses arches over the room openings because they carry the load in compression. Our tunnel research picked compression-only arches of fired clay or lime-pozzolan, no steel, for centuries of life. The same voussoirs could form buried rooms instead of curved poured concrete.
- **The site and the water come first.** The water table decides whether this house is possible at all, and rain into the courtyard needs a way out downhill. Sandbox 5 already seals its tunnels against groundwater; a buried home in the game should only be allowed where the land drains.
- **Budget the boring parts.** A sewage pump and heat-recovery ventilation are the two items most often left out of an underground budget, and he calls both non-negotiable.

## Open questions

- The opening refers to a 25° difference between the surface and the courtyard "documented at the beginning", but the transcript never gives it. Treat it as unsourced.
- The cost lines don't add up to his totals: summed with 20% contingency they give roughly $256,000 to $494,000, not $234,000 to $430,000 for the budget build. Recheck before quoting a figure.
- Some of the geometry is overstated. The circle does wrap the most floor in the least wall, but the claim about corners being "thermal bridges in the earth" needs a source.
- What does a ring of earth-covered rooms around a dome's food forest really save in our climate? The dome climate model could run it, with the rim rooms at ground temperature instead of open air.
