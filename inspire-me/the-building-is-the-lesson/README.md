## The story

There are no walls to speak of, and that is the first problem anyone raises.

Green School Bali is open to the air. No glazing between the students and the jungle, no barrier against the noise of it. Staff admit the obvious: it is distracting. Their answer is not to fix it. It's okay to get distracted by nature.

That answer only makes sense if you have already decided what the place is for. The school's position is that education has to change — that its purpose is to make the world better, and its subject is how to make the world sustainable. A room full of desks cannot teach that, because a room full of desks is not an example of anything.

So the learning stopped being about the world and started being the world.

A group of middle-school students built a kitchen. Not a model of a kitchen — an actual restaurant, with a service system they designed themselves: waiters, chefs, managers, accountants, the whole staffing structure, feeding the rest of the school. Somewhere else on campus, other students put up a new home for the chickens.

Staff there describe the difference in what a child says at the end of the day. Not *I read a really cool book*. Instead: *we built a new place for the chickens*. One of those is a report. The other is a memory, and it belongs to the person who made it.

Then there is the campus itself, which is the part that almost did not exist.

Across the tropics, bamboo was understood as a short-term, humble material — fine for village life, useless for anything you expected to keep. Not because it is weak, but because insects eat it. No treatment, no longevity, and no architect with a serious commission would touch it.

That changed roughly two decades ago with a natural treatment: the bamboo is made salty enough that the insects stop eating it. Treated that way, bamboo buildings last as long as wooden ones — and as treatment and design keep improving, the expectation is structures that stand for centuries.

Which put one material at both ends of the housing question at once. The same stuff can be a glamorous, luxurious home and the frame of emergency relief housing — and the designers there are clear about which of those matters more.

The proof of it is the Green School Arc, a sports hall built to span its whole floor with no columns anywhere in the middle. The brief was not structural. They wanted a space that made your whole body want to throw a ball as high and as far as it would go.

That is the argument the campus is making without saying it. The buildings are a promise to the kids that a beautiful, sustainable world is possible — and beauty is not decoration in that sentence. Wonder is what makes the promise land. You believe a world you have walked into.

## Beliefs that shift

- *Distraction has to be designed out of a classroom.* → If nature is the subject, being pulled toward it is not a failure of the room.
- *Students learn about the world, then enter it.* → Running a real restaurant and housing real chickens is the lesson, not a rehearsal of it.
- *Bamboo is a temporary material for temporary buildings.* → Salt-treated bamboo carries buildings as long as timber does, from relief housing to a column-free sports hall.

## What we learn

- **A campus can be a curriculum.** If the buildings demonstrate the thesis, every student is inside the argument before a class begins.
- **Give students a system, not a task.** The kitchen mattered because they designed the roles — service, cooking, management, accounting — not because they cooked.
- **Judge learning by what a child tells someone at home.** *We built a new place for the chickens* is a different category of outcome from *I read a good book*.
- **A material becomes structural when its weakness is solved, not when it gets fashionable.** Bamboo's problem was insects; the salt treatment is why architecture became possible.
- **The same material has to work at both ends.** Luxury home and relief housing from one supply chain is what makes it worth industrialising.
- **Beauty is a functional requirement.** Wonder is the thing that makes a promise about the future believable enough to act on.

## Open questions

- The exact treatment is described only as making the bamboo "salty". Boron and borax salts are the common method — worth confirming which is used here, at what concentration, and what it costs per culm.
- "As long as wooden buildings" is a claim without a number. The oldest treated-bamboo structures are roughly two decades old, so the durability figure is a projection, not a record.
- Speakers are not named in the clip, and the campus architecture is credited only to "my team and I". Confirm attribution before citing anyone.
- How does an open-air, jungle-adjacent campus translate to a northern climate? Our dome cells solve weather with glass; the pedagogy transfers, the building may not.
