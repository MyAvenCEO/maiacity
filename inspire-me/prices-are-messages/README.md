## The story

When Gorbachev asked Margaret Thatcher how she made sure people got food, she answered — as the story goes — "I don't. Prices do."

The video is a summary of *Basic Economics* by the Stanford economist Thomas Sowell, 700 pages read in two weeks and boiled down to seven ideas. Its promise is that most people never learn how an economy works, and so they are easy to fool.

It opens with a tax. In 2008 Maryland wanted more money and raised taxes on its roughly 8,000 millionaires, expecting $106 million more a year. By the video's account, a year later there were 6,000 left, and the state lost $257 million in tax revenue instead.

The lesson it draws is the backbone of the whole video: people respond to rewards, not intentions. The state meant to raise money; what it actually did was reward leaving.

Every choice has a price too, even when no money changes hands. A $50,000 car instead of a $20,000 one is not a $30,000 decision — invested for 30 years, that difference could grow to almost a quarter of a million.

Nobody could plan that by hand. Imagine running all the pizza in a country: how much cheese, so how much milk goes to cheese instead of ice cream, how many tomatoes, how many ovens, and all of it changing every day. No person and no computer holds that many moving numbers. Prices do it without anyone in charge: when pizza is scarce the price rises and suppliers make more; when there is too much, it falls and they slow down.

So when a price is held down by force, the message stops. The video's examples: rent control in New York, where it says there came to be four times as many abandoned apartments as homeless people, because landlords who could not raise rent stopped repairing, then walked away; Zimbabwe, where food held below cost left shops empty and crops rotting on farms. Held too high, the same break runs the other way: India guaranteed farmers a high price for wheat, and 11 million tons of it piled up and rotted in warehouses while people elsewhere went hungry.

Profits and losses are messages as well: do more of this, stop doing that. A company kept alive with taxes when it keeps losing money goes on using engineers, steel and trucks on things nobody wants. A wage is a price too — set above the value a job creates, and the job is the thing that disappears. And trade makes both sides richer, even when one of them is better at everything.

What changes, once you see it, is the question you ask of any rule. Not what does it intend — what does it reward?

## Beliefs that shift

- *A rule does what it was meant to do.* → A rule does what it rewards, and people follow the reward, not the intention.
- *Someone has to plan who gets what.* → Prices carry more information than any planner can hold; they coordinate millions of choices with nobody in charge.
- *A loss, or a failed project, is waste.* → A loss is a message to stop, and it frees people, time and materials for something that works.

## What we learn

- **Ask what a rule rewards.** Before any policy — a tax, a subsidy, a cap — ask what behaviour it pays for, not what it hopes for.
- **Every choice has a cost you don't see.** Money, time, attention: each can be used once. The real price is what it could have become.
- **Prices are information.** A price that angers you is telling you something is scarce, or wanted by too many.
- **A capped price hides the shortage it creates.** Too low: queues, hoarding, decay. Too high: surplus that rots.
- **Losses are feedback.** Stopping what doesn't work is how people and resources get to what does.
- **Trade is cooperation.** Both sides trade because both come out ahead — for countries and for how you spend your own hours.

## Open questions

- The Maryland figures come from the video, and they are disputed: the drop in millionaires fell in the 2008–09 recession. Check the state's own revenue data before using them.
- The New York abandoned-apartment ratio is from Sowell's book and describes decades past — what are today's numbers, and what did rent control's defenders find?
- The minimum-wage argument is presented as settled; the research on moderate increases is mixed. Read both sides before relying on it.
- For Maia City: inside a dome cell, much is shared rather than bought — food from the forest, the commons. What carries the price's message there — what tells a cell that something is scarce, or wanted, when nothing has a price?
