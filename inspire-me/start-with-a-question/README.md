## The story

Is fast food really as fast as they say?

Jenny Hoyos answered that by cooking a burger in the back of her mom's car while the two of them queued at a drive-through. The video has more than 45 million views.

She makes her living from questions nobody else asks. How much ice cream does a dollar buy? What is the cheapest thing in an airport? And she believes a great story should take less than 60 seconds, whether it is on a screen, at a family dinner, or in front of a boss you want a raise from.

Most stories lose people in the opening. They set the scene, explain who is who, and only then get to the point, and by then the audience has already gone.

So she starts with the question. A strange, sharp question makes people stay to the end, because they want the answer. It has to come immediately, in the first line, not after an introduction.

A question alone does not hold anyone for a whole minute. She makes the audience feel progress: first the tomatoes, then the lettuce, then the cheese, so every beat is visibly closer to a burger and closer to the answer.

Progress alone is smooth sailing, and smooth sailing bores people. The smoke in the car, the raw patty, her mom warning that she will burn the car down and asking whether all of this is for one subscriber: that is the conflict, a B plot the viewer starts to care about as well.

Then comes the tension. They are one car away from the window, the burger is still not done, and her mom is driving too fast. The answer has to feel uncertain right up to the end.

When it lands, it is short. They cooked faster than the drive-through. Whether the burger was raw does not matter; people came for the answer, and they got it quickly.

She gave the five-minute talk in four.

## Beliefs that shift

- *You have to set the scene before a story can start.* → Open on the question; the setup can wait, or can go.
- *A story is what happens in it.* → A story is a question held open, with events that move it forward and push against it, then close it.
- *A good story needs time.* → Almost every story can be told in under 60 seconds.

## What we learn

- **The question is the hook.** Something shocking or odd, asked in the first line, keeps people to the end.
- **Show progress.** Make each step visibly closer to the answer (tomatoes, lettuce, cheese) so leaving feels like stopping halfway.
- **Add conflict.** Without it, nobody is invested. A second thread, like a sceptical mother, gives people two things to care about.
- **Keep the answer uncertain.** Tension comes from not knowing until the last moment.
- **End fast.** Once the answer lands, stop. The audience came for the answer, not a wrap-up.
- **It works off-screen too.** The same shape makes a dinner story or a pitch for a raise land.

## Open questions

- 45 million views: the number is hers, for one video. How much of that is the framework, and how much the platform and the timing?
- Our films run two to three minutes. Which question does each day's film open on, and does it hold for that long?
- Could every day's Short or Reel open on one question the day answers, such as "can a city feed itself from a few minutes' walk?"
