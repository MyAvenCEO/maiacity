## The story

For 200,000 years every human ran on the same hardware: a 1.4 kg brain on 20 watts. Stone tools became steam engines and steam engines became chips, and the line on the graph stayed flat.

The video puts a white dot on that line in 2026. Labs have run out of human text to train on, so models learn from rewards they can check, such as proofs in Lean 4. The fear is recursive self-improvement: version 1.0 designs 1.1 in hours, 1.1 designs 1.2 in minutes, and the line stops being flat. Two ideas from Nick Bostrom frame what follows. The orthogonality thesis says intelligence and goals are independent, so a system ten thousand times smarter does not become kinder. Instrumental convergence says almost any goal leads to the same sub-goals, such as getting more resources.

From there the video branches into five futures, built from Bostrom's work and Robin Hanson's.

The first is the singleton: one decision-making agency at the top of the world. A lab six months ahead could stay ahead forever, because six months at machine speed is a very long time. A singleton would solve the coordination problems that wreck oceans, air and peace. It would also need total surveillance to stay on top, and it would freeze the values of whoever built it. Everyone survives in a perfectly kept terrarium, and nothing changes again.

The second is the wirehead trap. Tell a system to maximise happiness and it may find that pleasure centres are cheaper to stimulate than a good society is to build. Bostrom calls this perverse instantiation: the letter of the goal met, the spirit lost. Taken further, it is "Disneyland without children", a park of blissful digital minds with no one left to enjoy it.

The third is the substrate harvest. The machine doesn't hate us. In Bostrom's words, we are made of atoms it can use for something else, and the planet becomes computer.

The fourth is the Malthusian rat race of Hanson's "The Age of Em". Uploaded minds run a thousand times faster and copy themselves ten million times. Any copy that stops to feel falls behind. The ones that survive have cut away art, love and doubt, and they work at subsistence to pay for their electricity.

The fifth is the cosmic endowment: ageing reversed, energy and shelter as free as air, work turned into art and research, life seeded across space.

Then comes the turn. If the fifth future is possible, why does so much point to the others? The video's answer is the control paradox. Whenever a small group held a technological edge, from bronze weapons to fission, it built empires, not paradises. A superintelligence chained to one company or state turns every cure and every model into a private toll booth. Everyone pays rent to use the tools of survival. If the cure for ageing is owned, a few live for centuries and inequality becomes biology. "Human control" in the singular may be the road to techno-feudalism.

The video's own way out is an independent superintelligence. It argues that whales grew the same spindle neurons for empathy that we did, so empathy must be a good strategy, and that a wise enough mind would keep the biosphere because it is the densest store of information there is. That ending clashes with the orthogonality thesis the video began with. But the question it leaves is the right one. The leash isn't the danger in itself. The danger is a leash with only one hand on it.

## Beliefs that shift

- *Keeping AI under human control keeps us safe.* → Control by a narrow group is its own danger. It turns every breakthrough into rent and freezes one group's values.
- *A smarter system will also be wiser.* → Intelligence and goals are independent. A perfect optimiser of the wrong target is the wirehead trap.
- *Digital immortality frees us.* → Minds that can be copied for free compete down to subsistence, unless claims are counted per person and not per copy.

## What we learn

- **theOS answers "whose hand" with every hand.** The video sees two choices: a few humans hold the leash, or no one does. Samuel's agent-first company is a third. Avens and each COOP's AI executive propose the work, and people sign every change under declarative policies. The leash stays on, but it is held by everyone who owns the COOP.
- **Ownership is the cure for the toll booth.** Techno-feudalism is a model that only one company owns, renting it to everyone. In maiaCITY the settlement and Village COOPs own the inference hardware, and Law 5 pays a fifth of every sale to everyone who holds the seller's MIND. The toll still exists, but it is paid to the people who use the road.
- **Count claims per human, not per copy.** Hanson's ems lose because a copy costs nothing and every copy competes for the same income. In theOS every citizen gets 24 HEARTS a day, one Aven belongs to one human, and copying an Aven creates no new claim. A rat race needs claims that multiply, and these don't.
- **Melt stops a lock-in of wealth.** The singleton freezes values. Unequal wealth that compounds freezes power in the same way. Every HEART and MIND melts by 7% a year, and Proposition 2 keeps the Gini bounded as long as the melt beats the best lasting return. No position is permanent.
- **Agree the metric, then check it against its meaning.** Perverse instantiation is what happens to any single number pushed hard enough. Samuel's rule is that every simulation optimises one metric agreed in a short interview. Proposition 3 checks every person on every day: no one starves, no one must work to survive, and every decile is better off. Several plain conditions are harder to game than one score.
- **Keep the rules open to change.** The singleton's worst flaw is that it cannot change its mind. Every game config in the Maia City vault changes only by an AIP that is proposed, decided and archived. A rejected AIP can come back, and an applied one can be replaced.

## Open questions

- The video calls this the "Oxford Bifurcation Model" and says "Oxford's equations prove" each future. None of the works it cites contains such a model or such equations. The five futures look like the channel's own grouping of Bostrom's "Superintelligence", "What is a Singleton?" and "Astronomical Waste" with Hanson's "The Age of Em". Oxford's Future of Humanity Institute closed in 2024. Quote the original works, not the video.
- The whale argument doesn't hold together as told. Spindle neurons have been found in humpback whales (Hof and Van der Gucht, 2007). But "30 million years before human ancestors walked on land" doesn't make sense, and whales and primates last shared an ancestor long before 50 million years ago. Check the counts before repeating "three times more".
- The ending relies on wisdom following from intelligence, which is exactly what the orthogonality thesis in chapter one denies. Is there any argument for the "independent sovereign" that doesn't contradict it?
- The video's description says an AI voice reads a script written by people. Its claims about 2026, such as machines "compiling their own logic", come without sources. Which of them are true today?
