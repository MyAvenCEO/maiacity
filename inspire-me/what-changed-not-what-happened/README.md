## The story

His film opens on him running. A year ago he had 381 subscribers and wanted to be a YouTuber; now he has about 80,000 and makes his living from it. Still, he calls it a story against following your dreams.

He doesn't say why he's running, or from what. He admits you'll want to know and promises to come back to it — one small sentence, and the viewer and the film have an understanding: this matters, and it will return later.

Tim Runia worked for years as a filmmaker before he started on YouTube, and he once believed a video only worked if it was complex. More ideas. Flashier shots. Fancier editing.

Again and again, the videos people connected with most proved him wrong. They were the ones that let you feel what they were about. A lot of the others, as he sees it, stop being interesting because too many ideas pull in different directions, or lose people because one ingredient is missing or drowns the rest: beautiful shots over no clear story, a good story filmed in a way that doesn't carry it.

His film about that year had to be felt, not just watched. On the outside, not much happened in it. Written down as events, it was a man behind a computer, editing, filming, doing YouTube: hardly a list for an action movie.

Inside, it was a different film. The excitement at the start, the rush when things began to work, the doubt creeping in, pressure, fear, and the moment he realised he wasn't chasing something any more but running away — all of it hangs on one question, why living the dream feels heavier than he expected. When a script of his starts to feel like a mess, he has usually drifted from that question, and he steps back to ask what it is really about emotionally. Not what happens. What changes.

A camera can't point at a change inside.

So he gave each feeling a body. When life felt like flying, he lay on a skateboard while a friend helped, for a shot of himself floating over the concrete — awkward to make, and worth it, because that was how it felt. When it felt like being chased by something he wasn't ready to face, he ran through the woods, and the camera turned chaotic with him. Nobody was chasing him.

Once a feeling is on screen, the pull is to explain it. He doesn't. Ice cream melts untasted in his hand on a bench, his image for a life you're too lost in thought to enjoy, and if some viewers just find it funny, the feeling still gets through.

Those moments still have to land in the edit, and for him the edit is about pacing, not speed. Shots come quicker while things go well and last longer once the story turns reflective. Skating with the energy high, he reaches a staircase and stops, the music dies on a turntable effect, and his voice-over observes that fulfilment doesn't last. Nobody announces a new chapter. The momentum stops for the viewer where it stopped for him.

The music came before the camera. He chose most of it from the script, which already knew where the energy rose and fell, and cut the songs to fit.

None of it is complicated. It all points one way. A video works when it is simple: one emotional question about what changed inside, and every shot, cut and song chosen so people feel it.

## Beliefs that shift

- *A video that works needs more: more ideas, more impressive shots, fancier editing.* → It works when it is simple enough to feel, with one emotional question under everything.
- *A story is the events that happen in it.* → A story is what changes inside; a year spent behind a computer can carry a film.
- *You film what happened.* → You film what it felt like: a float that never happened, a chase with nobody behind you.

## From feeling to film

| What it felt like | How the film makes you feel it |
| --- | --- |
| Calm and in control, as the story begins | one long take, the camera following him smoothly, so you have time to settle in |
| Freedom and momentum, while things went well | a shot of him floating over the concrete (lying on a skateboard, a friend helping); the shots coming quicker |
| Fulfilment that doesn't last | skating with the energy high, he reaches a staircase and stops; the music cuts out on a turntable effect |
| Not really present in his own life | an ice cream melting in his hand on a bench, untasted, its meaning left open |
| Chased by something he wasn't ready to face | a run through the woods with nobody behind him; the camera chaotic, the shots unpredictable |
| Reflection | longer shots, more space between moments, music that feels reflective |

## What we learn

- **Find the one emotional question.** His film hangs on one: why living the dream feels heavier than expected. When a script turns into a mess, you have usually drifted from yours.
- **Ask what changes, not what happens.** A year behind a computer carried a whole film, because excitement, doubt, pressure and fear moved through it. A small shift or a single realisation is enough.
- **Leave the opening's questions open, and say so.** He keeps back why he runs, where to and what came before, and promises to come back to it. The promise tells the viewer the moment matters.
- **Film what it felt like.** Neither the float nor the chase happened as shown, and both are honest. Honesty about the feeling counts for more than getting the shot exactly right.
- **Let an image land before you explain it.** Record it and let it work; add your perspective in a voice-over later, only if it helps. A meaning left slightly open still carries the feeling.
- **Cut for feeling, not speed.** Ask what a part needs to feel like. Quick against slow does the work, and a hard stop in the sound can turn a story without a word.
- **Choose the music before you film.** A written script already knows where the energy rises, slows and breathes, so pick music for each part: momentum, pressure, reflection. Chosen early, it suggests movement, rhythm and how long a moment lasts, and the songs themselves can be stretched or reshaped to fit.

## Open questions

- Which film is it? The breakdown calls it his latest story-driven video and never names it. Find it, and watch the opening, the staircase and the ice cream scene whole.
- What he is running from is never said outright, only that the dream felt heavier than expected and that there was something he wasn't ready to face. The answer is in his film, not in this breakdown.
- The 381 and about 80,000 subscribers, a year apart, are his own figures, spoken in the film. Check them against the channel.
- Choosing the music first and editing the songs takes time, he says, but not how much. Would it fit a daily journal film?
