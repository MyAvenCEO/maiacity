## The story

Two cob walls took three months. Two walls of straw packed into a wooden frame took two weeks. Both are holding up the same kitchen, and the comparison is the whole point of building them side by side.

The place is Free and Real, on the Greek island of Evia, in the village of Agios Ioannis. The name is an acronym: freedom of resources for everyone everywhere, respect, equality, awareness, learning. Fifteen years, more than twelve buildings, all of them different from one another.

Apostolos grew up in Athens and spent years as a web designer in front of a screen. He describes it as a void — something always felt wrong. So he quit, came here, and calls it the best decision he ever made.

The plan, at the start, was an eco community. That is the standard dream and it is the one they abandoned first.

Because they looked at what they actually knew and found it wasn't enough to run one. Nobody there knew how to build a yurt, filter greywater, or feed fifty people off-grid. **Therefore** the goal changed: not a community, a school — train ourselves and other people, and maybe one day the graduates can build the community properly.

The first yurt is the proof. They stitched it together from several sets of online instructions and made, in his words, many many mistakes. It stood in the wrong spot for thirteen years. Two years ago they took it down, moved it, rebuilt it traditionally with fabrics crowdfunded from the village grandmothers, added a window and better insulation. Fifteen years old and still in service.

**But** a school with no space cannot teach through a Greek winter. So they built the mega yurt — 120 m², four levels, every technique they had learned combined into one structure, basic shell up in twenty days and finished over a run of workshops. At its centre sits a rocket mass heater: the flue runs through a cob bench, the cob traps the heat, and forty-five minutes of fire warms the room for hours.

Everything else is the same logic applied outward. A solar shower for sunny days and a wood-fired one for the rest. Greywater through two beds of bamboo — the roots run parallel to the ground and make a filter layer, stone underneath — then out to the garden. Composting toilets into bins. A cellar dug two metres down because even with solar panels and a good fridge, fifty people need more cold storage than electricity can cheaply give.

Nothing belongs to anyone. Everything belongs to Free and Real, a nonprofit, with no individual ownership — and decisions are made by putting every piece of data on the table until, as he puts it, the decision makes itself.

They are honest about the limits. Some years the olives make enough oil for the whole year; some years they don't. Food production is good when the team is small and the team's size changes constantly. They are not obsessed with total self-sufficiency, because that was never the product.

The product is the five hundred people who have passed through, the second site now planted with 500 fruit trees, and the 100 m² straw bale Godzilla yurt going up there as the hub of the community they finally know how to build.

The eco community didn't fail. It got postponed by fifteen years of deliberately making mistakes in public, on a hillside they call the test site.

## Beliefs that shift

- *Build the community, and the knowledge will follow.* → Build the school, and the community becomes something its graduates are qualified to make.
- *A building is finished when it works.* → A building is finished when it has taught you something the next one will use — every structure carries one new experiment.
- *Self-sufficiency is the goal.* → Self-sufficiency is a teaching material; the output is trained people, not closed loops.

## What we learn

- **Name the site what it is.** Calling it the test site gives every builder permission to try something unproven, and converts failures into curriculum instead of embarrassment.
- **Measure techniques against each other in the same building.** Four wall methods in one hexagonal kitchen is a teaching tool no textbook matches — and it produced the 3 months vs 2 weeks number.
- **Reversibility beats permanence early.** The first yurt was wrong for thirteen years, then moved and upgraded rather than demolished. Light structures let a mistake be corrected instead of lived with.
- **Thermal mass beats output.** The rocket mass heater's trick is not a bigger fire; it's cob holding the heat long after the fire is out.
- **Water gets used twice.** Washing water runs through bamboo roots and stone, then waters the garden — a filter that is also a crop.
- **Ownership at the entity, not the person.** Everything belongs to the nonprofit, which removes the question of who keeps what when someone leaves.
- **Decide by putting the data on the table.** Their stated method: gather every relevant fact with everyone present, and the decision makes itself.

## Open questions

- What is the actual share of food, energy and water they cover on site, across a full year? The video claims a "good level" and admits it varies with team size.
- Does the data-on-the-table method genuinely avoid deadlock, or does an informal hierarchy decide when the data is ambiguous?
- How much of the operation depends on European volunteer programmes (European Solidarity Corps, Erasmus youth trainings) — and would it stand without them?
- The location is inferred: the transcript garbles it as "North A" and "Yokabos village", read here as North Evia and Agios Ioannis. Worth confirming before it is repeated.
- Fifteen years of training before starting the community: is that the real minimum, or an artefact of learning everything from scratch without a school like theirs already existing?
