## The story

A PhD in fusion energy, and no practical skills at all.

That is how Marcin Jakubowski describes the end of his twenties. The world offered him options and he took them — the consumer lifestyle, as he puts it.

So he started a farm in Missouri, and learned the economics of farming the hard way. He bought a tractor. It broke. He paid to have it repaired. It broke again.

Pretty soon he was broke too.

The machine wasn't the real problem. The tools a small, sustainable farm and settlement actually needed simply didn't exist: robust, modular, efficient, low-cost, made from local and recycled materials, built to last a lifetime rather than designed to wear out.

Nobody was going to sell him those. He would have to build them himself.

He did, and he tested them, and found that industrial productivity can be reached on a small scale. Then he did the part that changed everything: he published it all. The 3D designs, the schematics, the instructional videos and the budgets, on an open wiki.

Contributors started arriving from all over the world, prototyping new machines on dedicated project visits. The project became Open Source Ecology, and its goal became a list: the fifty machines it takes for modern life to exist, from tractors to bread ovens to circuit makers, each as an open, do-it-yourself version anyone can build and maintain at a fraction of the cost. The Global Village Construction Set.

Eight of the fifty had been prototyped when he gave this talk. A hundred trees planted in a day. Five thousand bricks pressed in one day from the dirt beneath his feet. A tractor built in six days.

Open source had already won for knowledge and creativity. Hardware was next — and hardware is what changes lives in the most material way. Lower the barrier to farming, building and manufacturing, and you release enormous amounts of human potential, not only in the developing world but for any farmer, builder or maker who wants to start something.

His target is a set of designs so clear and so complete that a single burned DVD is a civilization starter kit.

A greater distribution of the means of production. Supply chains that respect the environment. A maker culture strong enough to move past artificial scarcity.

## Beliefs that shift

- *Serious machines have to be bought from manufacturers.* → Industrial productivity can be reached on a small scale, with machines anyone can build.
- *Keeping designs secret protects their value.* → Publishing every drawing is what brought contributors from all over the world.
- *Owning a tool means paying someone else to fix it.* → A machine whose drawings you hold is one you can repair yourself, for a lifetime.

## What we learn

- **Start from what broke.** The whole project came out of one tractor that kept failing — a real problem, not a theory.
- **Design for a lifetime, not for obsolescence.** Robust, modular and repairable beats cheap and closed, even at a slightly higher first cost.
- **Local and recycled materials make tools sovereign.** A machine built from what is nearby doesn't depend on a supply chain that can break.
- **Publish everything.** Designs, schematics, videos and budgets together are what let strangers build and improve the machines.
- **Small scale can be productive.** Five thousand bricks and a tractor in six days are industrial numbers from a single farm.
- **Hardware changes lives materially.** Open tools for farming, building and making turn directly into businesses and livelihoods.
