## The story

The test bench holds one small round cell, 21 mm across and 70 mm long, the size used in cordless drills and many electric cars. The engineers connect it and turn the current up to 100 amps.

For a cell this small that is almost 30C: a rate that would fill it in about two minutes. Ordinary cells charge at 2 to 3C. Really fast ones manage 5C. Ten is about the most anyone tries, and only as a short peak. On this bench, 10C is where the cell slows down once it passes half full. It goes from 40 to 50% in under ten seconds and reaches 80% after 150 seconds. The makers allow 75 °C. The cell gets to about 55 °C, then starts cooling while it is still charging. They say it can do this 500 times.

The presenter is Tom, a battery scientist who developed cells for Tesla and founded his own battery startup. He came to show that Germany can make battery cells.

Every cell has the same four parts. An anode and a cathode hold the lithium. A thin separator keeps them from touching. A liquid electrolyte carries the ions between them. The factory mixes a black paste, spreads it thinly on metal foil and dries it, then winds the anode, cathode and two separators into a tight roll that goes into the can.

Then comes the trade-off the whole industry lives with. Thin electrodes give short paths and fast charging. Thick ones hold more energy but charge slowly. So you choose power or energy.

This cell gets close to both by shortening every path. It has no tabs. In a normal cell the current leaves each long electrode strip at a few points, so the electrons travel a long way to get out. Here the whole edge of each strip is connected. Inside, the cathode is NMC (nickel, manganese and cobalt), held together with carbon black and costly carbon nanotubes that carry the current. The electrolyte is made to slip into every pore, because a corner that stays dry charges more slowly than the rest. The cell's internal resistance is 1.1 milliohms. Low resistance means little heat, and little heat means it can charge fast without wearing out.

In a car, it goes into a Porsche 911 that still has a petrol engine. 216 of these cells make a 2 kWh, 400-volt pack that does the old lead-acid battery's job and much more. It heats the catalytic converter before the engine starts. It drives an electric motor for extra push. And it spins an electric turbocharger, so the driver doesn't wait two seconds for exhaust gas to spool it up. The pack drains in seconds when you accelerate and refills just as fast when you brake.

The catch is cost. The cell is built in Germany for performance, with more of the expensive additives than a cheap cell would use. So it goes where price matters least: sports cars, drones, spacecraft. The makers don't try to beat China on price. As they put it, the cell will never go into a flashlight or a remote control.

Their real edge is where they come from. The company combines VARTA, a battery maker with more than a hundred years of experience, with Porsche's industrial engineering. They built the whole factory in about 14 months. It records more than 3,000 measurements for every cell and checks about 250 critical features with cameras. Driverless vehicles bring in all the material, so no person handles it. It makes up to 22 million cells a year, with a supply chain kept mostly inside Europe. That matters to customers who need a steady supply when tariffs and borders make trade uncertain.

Tom's verdict is that money alone doesn't build a cell factory. It takes experienced people and time. Germany has both, and has picked a niche. Whether it should also go after the mass market, where the only large gigafactory running in Germany is Chinese, he leaves open.

## Beliefs that shift

- *Germany can't make battery cells.* → With VARTA's century of cell experience and Porsche's engineering, it built one of the most automated cell factories in the world in about 14 months.
- *A cell gives you power or energy, never both.* → With no tabs and an electrolyte that reaches every pore, the cell's resistance drops to 1.1 milliohms. It charges to 80% in two and a half minutes, and its energy version still stores 850 Wh per litre.
- *The only way to survive is to beat China on price.* → Choose the markets where performance and a steady local supply are worth paying for, and stay out of the rest.

## What we learn

- **Pick the cell for the job: power, energy or price.** This cell is built for power, and the price shows it. A village center's geothermal plant gives a steady 3.4 MW and can store energy by throttling its wells, "without any batteries", as our village energy research puts it. Where a village does store electricity, it wants cheap cells that last many cycles, not 15 kW per kilogram.
- **A small fast buffer next to a steady source.** The Porsche keeps its engine and adds 2 kWh that covers the seconds the engine can't. A village plant runs flat all day, while kilns, steelworks and trains pull power in spikes. A small fast pack beside the plant could cover those spikes, so the plant doesn't have to be sized for its worst minute.
- **Trains that charge at every stop.** Our underground trade routes run straight between village centers 1.76 km apart. Like the Porsche's pack, a small pack of power cells could fill up in two or three minutes while a container is unloaded and take back energy when the train brakes into a station. That could save running power lines through every tunnel or carrying a heavy pack. The game already counts the energy of the trade routes' trains, so a rough model of a charge-at-every-stop train is a small step.
- **Experience is what money can't buy.** The factory went up in 14 months because VARTA and Porsche brought people who had done it before. In Sandbox 5 a new factory stage runs at full output on its first day. A ramp-up, or a cheaper path through a partner who already knows the trade, would make the crafting engine closer to real life.
- **Stay local where steady supply matters.** The makers pay more to keep their supply chain in Europe and sell that steadiness to customers who need it. The settlers game makes all five wares locally for the same reason. The game could show that steadiness as a value, not only as a cost.
- **Measure every piece.** 3,000 measurements per cell and driverless deliveries are why errors stay rare. The village center's storehouse and factory domes could log every batch the same way, so the books show where losses happen.

## Open questions

- The presenter calls himself Tom: a battery scientist who worked on cells for Tesla and founded a startup. The transcript is in English, but the ending ("Yours, Top") and the German factory suggest a dubbed German channel. YouTube is blocked from here, so check the channel name before adding an author.
- The transcript garbles the company's name (Smarty, WIF Smart, VFlowSmart, WF Smart, V4Drive). It also calls Porsche the majority owner of "Customcells", which doesn't match the VARTA and Porsche company the rest of the video describes. It is most likely V4Smart, VARTA's former V4Drive business, but check this.
- Some numbers don't add up. A 21700 cell at 850 Wh/L holds about 20 Wh, so 22 million cells come to about 0.45 GWh, not "just under 1 GWh". If 100 A is almost 30C, the cell holds about 3.3 Ah, and 216 of them would make about 2.6 kWh, not 2. More than 599 MB of data per cell is about 13 petabytes a year, so the figure may have been kB. And the bench shows 80% after 150 s, while the claim is full in 2 min 40 s.
- The only drawback named is cost, and no price is given. What does a kWh of this cell cost compared with a cheap stationary cell? Does any part of a village need power cells at all, or only the trains?
