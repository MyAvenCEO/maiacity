Note: "6 Principles To Restore Nature, Anywhere" by Leave Curious, filmed on a late-summer walk in
Britain. The presenter has visited ambitious rewilding projects across Britain and met the people
running them, and distils six guiding principles that apply to a 5,000-acre site, a small back
garden or a balcony. Below are the points the report card is built on, in the order the film
makes them. (The auto-transcript renders the channel's community as "Leaf Curious".)

---

**The opening.** Nature seems to be struggling everywhere, and has been for the best part of a
century or longer. That is only part of the story: nature is coming back and is surprisingly
resilient. The challenge to the viewer: go out and do something, now — it matters, and it is fun.

**1. Mess.** Nature does not like to be neat. It sprawls, grows bramble, dies, and takes over what
people put in the landscape (a fence post's barbed wire being swallowed by growth). Mess needs
reframing: not a problem, but abundance. The messier nature is allowed to be, the healthier and
more resilient it is. In a garden: decide which areas get to grow up — a corner, half the garden —
and cut one path through. Let things die off instead of dead-heading and clearing leaves. The real
mess is litter (a condom wrapper on the path is the example). Tolerate the species people are
quick to remove, such as bramble, one of the best habitats for birds. Keep seating areas for
yourself: it is not all or nothing.

**2. Connectedness.** People have degraded habitats and fragmented them. Temperate rainforest, one
of the most biodiverse habitats he has seen, has been reduced to scattered islands — he says
around 99% has been lost. Fragments become less resilient because fewer species can move between
them. In a garden: remove a fence panel, or at least cut a hedgehog hole. He tells of hearing a
hedgehog eating snails after rain, and then a badger coming through the same hole. Connect the
garden's own micro-habitats, and make ponds easy for animals to get out of. He says the UK has more
hedgerows than any other nation, though many were destroyed during and after the war; hedgerows are
safe passages across hostile landscapes. A community member planted a hedgerow with a bug hotel.

**3. Keep it dead.** Death is part of the cycle and is cleared away too fast. Fallen leaves and
branches feed the soil for next season; a whale carcass becomes a feeding frenzy. Slow down: leave
seed heads for winter birds and dead grass for hibernating insects; let leaves fall, or at least
pile them. Let a dead tree stand — standing deadwood is vital habitat — unless it threatens a house.
Leave a fallen tree where it lies. He moved a dead long-eared bat (he thinks, a drought casualty)
out of his children's reach into a flower bed, where it feeds the small organisms that recycle it.

**4. Hold more water.** People have engineered water everywhere: settlements along rivers, rivers
forced into single channels. A natural river is ribbons of water with freedom to move. More water
in a landscape means more life. In a garden: a water butt — his took under half an hour to fit and
caught 100 litres in one rainfall. Dig a pond: his small container pond had frogs within a couple of
months, in a row of town gardens. A warning from his own mistake: it froze over in winter and two
overwintering frogs died, so keep small ponds from freezing solid. With more land: managed
realignment of a sea wall to let salt marsh return; let a river spill onto its floodplain; plant
willow along the banks; prepare the land for beavers ("the holy grail of how to hold water"),
though licences make reintroduction hard. Dig many small ponds; on slopes, terrace them.

**5. Reduce pressure.** Taste varies and a cultivated garden can still be diverse, but stop using
pesticides and weed killers (he notes a fellow creator's criticism of garden centres stocking
poison). Mow less: cut a winding path instead of a flat lawn — past the pond, the climber, a patch
to sit in. He asks whether some US communities fine people for unkept lawns. Light is pressure too:
angle security lights down so they do not flood nocturnal habitat, and see the stars again. Soil
compaction kills microbial life; paths keep footfall in one place. Much of this principle is about
what you stop doing, which often has more impact than what you do.

**6. More variety.** Variety of species, ages and structure. Go native where possible, because
native plants evolved with the local animals; failing that, any pollinator flowers help. Create
niches: tall and short plants, deadwood piles, shallow and deeper water. With more land, a mosaic of
habitats, following what the land naturally wants to do — and large grazing animals, moved around by
you as the missing predator. Experiment: short of releasing an invasive species, it is hard to get
restoration badly wrong.

**The community.** Throughout, an invitation to join the channel's community, which learns, acts,
and pools money into a restoration fund.
