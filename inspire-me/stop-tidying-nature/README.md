## The story

A hole the size of a hedgehog, cut into a garden fence.

One night after rain the presenter heard crunching in the dark: a hedgehog, eating snails. Then more rustling behind him. A badger had come through the same hole.

He has travelled Britain to its most ambitious rewilding projects and met the people who run them. The headlines say nature is losing everywhere, and for a century or more that has been true. What he found on the ground was the other half of the story: nature comes back, and it comes back fast, once people stop getting in its way.

That last part is the problem. The instinct of anyone who cares about a garden is to do things to it. Cut the grass, clear the leaves, take out the bramble, chop down the dead tree, keep the edges neat. Each of those feels like care. Each one takes something away.

So he boils the projects down to six principles that work at any size, from a 5,000-acre estate to a balcony.

**Mess.** Nature wants to sprawl, and a messy patch is abundance, not neglect. Give it a corner, or half the garden, and cut a single path through. The real mess is litter. Bramble is one of the best homes a bird can have.

**Connection.** Habitats have been cut into islands. He says about 99% of Britain's temperate rainforest is gone, and what is left is scattered too thinly for species to move between. A hedgehog hole in the fence, a hedgerow, a pond an animal can climb out of: each one turns fragments back into a landscape.

**Death.** Leaves, seed heads and dead grass feed next year's soil and shelter this winter's insects. A dead tree left standing is one of the richest habitats there is.

**Water.** Rivers have been forced into single channels. Let them spread again, plant willow, dig ponds. His water butt took half an hour to fit and caught 100 litres in one rainfall. A small pond in a row of town gardens had frogs in it within a couple of months.

He is honest about getting it wrong. That pond froze over one winter, and two frogs sleeping at the bottom died.

**Less pressure.** No poisons, far less mowing, lights angled down, soil kept uncompacted by keeping footsteps to one path.

**Variety.** Tall and short, wet and dry, young and old, native wherever possible. More kinds of place make room for more kinds of life.

Put side by side, half of the six are things you stop doing. That is the real turn of the piece. Restoring nature is less about what you add and more about what you stop taking away, and you can start on any patch of ground, today, by leaving it alone.

## The six principles

| Principle | In a garden | With more land |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Mess | Let areas grow up; cut one path; let things die off | Tolerate bramble and scrub |
| Connection | Hedgehog holes; ponds animals can leave | Hedgerows as safe passages |
| Keep it dead | Leave seed heads, dead grass, leaf piles | Standing and fallen deadwood stays |
| Hold water | Water butts; small ponds that don't freeze solid | Floodplains, willow, beavers, managed realignment, terraced ponds |
| Reduce pressure | No poisons; mow a path, not a lawn; dim lights | Avoid compaction; less machinery |
| More variety | Tall and short plants, deadwood, shallow and deep water | A mosaic of habitats; grazing animals moved by people |

## Beliefs that shift

- *A tidy garden is a cared-for garden.* → Tidiness removes the dead wood, leaf litter and scrub that most life depends on. Care can look like mess.
- *Helping nature means doing more.* → Much of it is stopping: the mowing, the poison, the floodlight, the fence.
- *Small spaces don't count.* → A hedgehog hole, a pond or a water butt changes what lives there within weeks, even in a row of town gardens.

## What we learn

- **Give ground back, and keep a path.** Let most of a space grow up and cut one route through it. People get their place, and the rest is left to nature.
- **Connect the pieces.** Openings in fences and hedgerows between plots, so animals can move across a landscape. For Maia City, the paths between dome cells should be hedgerows, not walls.
- **Leave the dead where it falls.** Leaf litter, seed heads and deadwood are next year's soil and this winter's shelter. The food forests should keep them.
- **Catch water where it lands.** Butts on every roof, ponds wherever there is a hollow, rivers allowed onto their floodplains.
- **Subtract before you add.** Poisons, constant mowing, light at night and soil compaction are pressures you can simply stop.
- **Variety makes resilience.** Different heights, ages, wet and dry, native species. The more kinds of niche, the more kinds of life.

## Open questions

- "About 99% of temperate rainforest lost" is stated from memory in the video. Check the actual figures for Britain's temperate rainforest before quoting them.
- The claim that the UK has more hedgerows than any other nation needs a source.
- Beaver reintroduction depends on licences and local law. Check what applies wherever a city like ours would be built.
- How the six principles work inside a designed food forest, which is cultivated on purpose, and where the line between permaculture and rewilding should sit.
