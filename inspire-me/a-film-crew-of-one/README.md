## The story

A small bedroom, one person, no crew. The video that comes out of it looks like a film set.

That is the whole promise, and she does not pretend it is talent. It comes down to three rules: framing, lighting and movement. She explains them with a LEGO set. The framing is the base plate that holds everything. You are the minifigure in the middle. The light is the bright bricks that give it life and depth. The movement is turning the model round so people can see it from every side. Leave one out and it falls apart.

It does not start with the camera. It starts with a song. Without a crew, she says, the music is your director: she hears a track and sees the whole video before writing a word, so it sets the pace and the mood before anything is filmed. Then a script, a shot list, and reference pictures of what she has in her head.

The first problem of filming alone is practical and maddening: you cannot see yourself. You run to the camera, check focus, run back, and it is wrong again. Her fix is to connect her phone to the camera as a live monitor. She sits down, locks the focus by hand, checks the exposure, and never gets up.

Then the shots, one piece at a time. A wide main shot, never at eye level, because that looks like a video call: the camera drops to chest height and tilts slightly up, and the room grows. The frame gets dressed, because an empty frame looks dead: a lamp, a plant, a stack of books. A second camera goes to the side with a longer lens, pushed back against the wall, so the background compresses and blurs. A tiny camera sits inches from her hands for the small movements, typing, writing, turning a knob. Another looks straight down on the desk.

Each extra angle is more work alone. That is exactly why it works: mixing perspectives is what makes the viewer imagine a crew.

Light is where the money myth breaks. Good light makes a cheap phone look like cinema; bad light makes an expensive camera look cheap. What matters is contrast and direction: light on one side of the face, shadow on the other. A window does it for free, if you turn sideways to it instead of facing it. Studio lights repeat the same idea, a soft main light to the side and a small one to lift the shadows, and a few cheap coloured lights hidden behind a plant or a screen give the room depth.

The last rule is about moving yourself, not only the camera. Someone frozen in the middle of the frame for ten minutes looks stiff under any light. So she walks in with a coffee, leans over the desk, talks while walking, changes angle and posture.

She is honest about the cost. Filming alone is slow and frustrating. The focus slips, the light turns out wrong halfway through, the battery dies. Over time it becomes meditative: music on, lights set, shot framed, one piece clicked onto the next.

The film crew was never the requirement. The pieces already in the room are enough, put together one by one.

## Beliefs that shift

- *Cinematic means a crew and expensive gear.* → It means framing, light and movement, and one person can build all three with what is already in the room.
- *Music is added at the end.* → Chosen first, it becomes the director: it sets the pace, the energy and the shots.
- *Good light means more light.* → Good light means direction and contrast: light on one side, shadow on the other, from a window if that is what you have.

## What we learn

- **Pick the soundtrack first.** It decides the pacing before a single shot, which is what a director would do.
- **Make your phone the monitor.** Seeing the frame from where you sit removes the worst part of filming alone.
- **Never shoot at eye level.** Chest height, tilted slightly up, makes a small room feel bigger.
- **Dress the frame.** A lamp, a plant, some books: an empty frame reads as dead.
- **Mix focal lengths and angles.** A wide shot, a long lens from the side, a close-up of hands, a top-down view: the mix is what suggests a crew.
- **Light from the side.** Turn sideways to the window; hide small coloured lights behind objects for depth.
- **Move yourself.** Enter the frame, lean, walk, change posture. Stillness looks stiff.

## Open questions

- The kit named (Sony FX3, ZV-E10 Mark II, DJI Osmo, Amaran lights) is what she uses, not a requirement; the video itself argues you can start with a phone and a window. Worth testing how far our own daily films get with the phone alone.
- Several shots mean several cameras or a lot of re-staging. For our Day films: which two angles give the most "crew" for the least time?
- The music-first rule fits her mood videos. Does it hold for our story-driven films, where the words carry the arc?
