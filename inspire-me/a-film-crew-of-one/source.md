Note: "How I Master Solo Filmmaking: 3 Rules for Cinematic Videos in 2026" by kaye.creatives, a
solo creator filming at home in a small bedroom. Below are the points the report card is built
on, in the order the video makes them.

---

**The three rules.** A solo video that looks high-budget comes down to framing, lighting and
movement. The analogy: building a LEGO set. Framing is the base plate, you are the minifigure in
the middle, lighting is the bright-coloured bricks that give it life and dimension, and movement
is turning the model round so people see it from every angle. Miss one and it falls apart.

**Music first.** Every video starts from an idea, a camera angle to test, or a song. Without a
crew, the music acts as the director: she hears a track and imagines the whole video before
writing the script. Do not leave music for post-production; picking it early sets the pacing,
energy and vibe before shooting.

**Plan.** Turn the vision into a script and shot list; sketch scenes if you can draw, or collect
reference images from social media or Pinterest.

**Your phone as a monitor.** The hardest part of filming alone is running back and forth to check
focus and framing. Connect the phone to the camera (she uses Sony's Creators app) as a live
monitor, lock manual focus and check exposure from where you sit.

**The main shot.** Wide, around 24 mm, to capture the room (Sony FX3; sometimes a DJI Osmo for
its tracking). Never leave the tripod flat at eye level ("a boring zoom call"): drop the camera to
chest height and tilt it slightly up, which makes the room feel bigger.

**Dress the set.** The frame should never feel empty: a desk lamp, a plant, a stack of books
change the texture and mood.

**A second angle.** From the side, a tighter 50 or 85 mm lens with the camera pushed back to the
wall: long lenses compress space and blur the background. She uses a Sony ZV-E10 Mark II, her
first camera, shooting 10-bit S-Log. It gives a behind-the-scenes feel.

**Micro details and B-roll.** A tiny camera inches from your hands at a slight angle (typing,
turning a knob, writing) adds intimacy. B-roll can be anything: a sip, dropping something, a pen
tip, ticking off a list. A top-down camera shows work, sketches, notes. Mixing perspectives is what
tricks the viewer into imagining a crew.

**Light: contrast and direction.** Good light makes a cheap phone look like cinema; bad light makes
a $5,000 camera look like an old phone. Light one side of the face, shadow on the other. A window
works: facing it is flat, turning sideways to it gives natural shadow. With studio lights the same
idea: a key light in a softbox to the side (Amaran 300c), a smaller fill to lift the shadows
(Amaran 150c). Cheap pocket RGB lights hidden behind a plant or monitor add coloured accents and
depth. It is not about blinding the room but about painting shape, contrast and depth.

**Movement — of yourself.** Sitting frozen in the centre for ten minutes feels stiff however good
the light. Characters in films and anime rarely just stand there: walk into shot with a coffee,
lean over the desk, walk while talking, change posture and angle.

**The close.** Filming solo is slow and frustrating — missed focus, wrong light, dead batteries —
but it becomes meditative. You build it piece by piece like the LEGO set. Don't wait for the
perfect camera, light or room; use what you have.
