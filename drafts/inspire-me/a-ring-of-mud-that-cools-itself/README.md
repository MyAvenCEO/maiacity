## The story

A ring of earth 61 m across, four storeys high, with 400 rooms around one open courtyard. In summer its inner rooms stay cool, and nothing in it uses electricity.

It is Chengqi Lou, in Yongding County in Fujian, 515 m up in the hills where Fujian, Jiangxi and Guangdong meet. More than 20,000 earth buildings like it, called tulou ("earth building"), are scattered across those hills, and 46 are UNESCO World Heritage Sites. Only about one in five is round. The round ones just ended up on the postcards. Chengqi Lou is more than 300 years old, and 250 of its rooms are still lived in.

Yet no one has built a new tulou in over 40 years. The people who would inherit the craft are moving into concrete flats. So Japanese researchers went to measure what is being lost, against the standards we use to grade new towers.

It starts with the wall. At the base it is 1.5 to 2 m thick, on a stone footing that keeps the damp out. It was pounded in layers inside timber forms, from local soil mixed with lime and sand, with strips of bamboo laid through the critical courses like steel in concrete. Chengqi Lou holds about 3,200 m³ of packed earth, 26% of the building's whole volume. A wall that heavy takes a whole afternoon's heat and never lets it through before the cool of night pulls it back out. Over nine days in the summer of 2009, the outside air swung by about 6.5 °C between afternoon and dawn. In a closed, windowless storeroom on the second floor, the temperature moved 1.7 °C in the whole week, and the humidity held at 76%, give or take 2%.

Mass alone would only make a steady box. The courtyard is a chimney. A wind model of a round tulou showed air entering the gate at about 0.5 m/s and speeding up by about as much again as warm air rose and left through the top of the ring. It is a fan with no blades, and the courtyard ran about 2 °C milder than the air outside.

The third part is the kitchens. They sit on the ground floor, open to the courtyard, and they evaporate water all day. That pulls heat out of the air the way sweat cools skin, and the ground floor was the coolest floor in the building. The water comes from wells inside the walls, so the same buckets that cool the house would keep a family alive through a siege. One system, two jobs.

Then the rooms are sorted by climate. The steadiest place in the building, that windowless second floor, would be the best bedroom anywhere else. Here it is the granary, because a room that holds 76% humidity keeps rice from spoiling. Kitchens and shared life go on the cool ground floor. Bedrooms go on the third and fourth floors, which follow the outside air within about 1 °C but have the windows, the light and the breeze. One great gate is the control: open in summer so the chimney runs, shut in winter to hold the warmth in.

The builders also knew how their material would behave over time. They used fir for the beams, pine for the stairs and floors, and bamboo nails, so nothing rusts in a damp mountain winter. They charred the timber with smoke and brushed it with tung oil. A wet earth wall 2 m thick shrinks and settles for years as it dries, so they set the rafter ends high and out of level. As the wall sank, it pulled them down, and the floors settled level.

Measured on CASBEE, Japan's green building assessment, Chengqi Lou scored 2.9 on the new-construction version and 2.7 on the existing-building version. Both are rank A, which the video compares to LEED Gold and BREEAM Very Good. It scored best on thermal load and on natural energy use.

So why did people stop building them? Not because of a villain. Everything that keeps a tulou cool in summer keeps it cold in winter. The mass that won't warm up in the afternoon won't warm up in January either, and rammed-earth rooms in this climate sit at about 5 to 6 °C all winter. That fails both ASHRAE's comfort standard and China's own. The people moving into concrete flats are choosing a heated home and running water, and who can blame them. The tulou solved summer in a place that also has a winter. The physics never stopped working. It only needed the other half.

## Beliefs that shift

- *Old earth buildings are primitive.* → Measured with today's tools, a 300-year-old earth ring scores the same rank as the best new green offices, on thermal load most of all.
- *Cooling needs machines.* → Mass holds the swing down, the shape moves the air and water takes the heat out. The tulou does all three on zero watts.
- *They were abandoned because they didn't work.* → They were abandoned because they only solved summer. Add a winter answer and the summer half is still among the best ever measured.

## What we learn

- **Our villages have the winter answer the tulou lacked.** The tulou failed only on heat. Each village centre's geothermal plant gives about 12.6 MW of heat beside its power. Earth mass and shape for summer, geothermal heat for winter: together they answer both halves.
- **The north pond is the dome's earth wall.** Hemp insulates the north band, but it isn't mass. The heat store is the 3 m deep fish pond. In the 150 m dome, its 4,700 m³ hold about 5.5 MWh per degree and give about three quarters of the heat the dome needs. Like the tulou's wells, it does two jobs, holding heat and raising fish.
- **The crown is the courtyard.** Our climate model of the 150 m dome gets rid of more heat through vents than any other way: about 4.6 of its 8.1 GWh a year. That is the tulou's chimney: warm air rising out through the top costs nothing to run.
- **Give the steadiest place to the food.** The tulou's best room went to the grain. In a village, the steadiest places are underground: the lime and clay vaults of the tunnels, and the earth under a dome floor. Seed and grain stores belong there, before any bedroom does.
- **No metal to rust.** Bamboo nails and wood-on-wood joints survived 300 damp winters. Our 1,000-year answer for the tunnels follows the same rule: compression-only arches of fired clay or stone in lime, with no steel in them.
- **Design for how the material moves.** The rafters set high so the floors would settle level are a lesson for every earth, lime or hemp part we plan. Write down how it shrinks, settles or swells over its first years, and build that in.

## Open questions

- The CASBEE scores, the 2009 measurements and the wind model come from Japanese studies the video names only loosely ("Yoshino"). Find the papers before quoting, and check the video's own comparison of rank A with LEED Gold.
- The video gives about 1,260 heating degree days a year for Yongding without a unit. In °F-days that is about 700 °C-days, which seems more likely for this climate than 1,260 °C-days. Check before comparing it with Munich.
- The channel is named only in the narration ("Here at Wild Line"). Confirm the channel name before linking an author page.
