## The story

William James wrote 1,400 pages about the human mind. In the middle of them he named the one skill he thought mattered most: "the faculty of voluntarily bringing back a wandering attention, over and over again." No one, he wrote, is *compos sui*, in charge of himself, without it. An education that improved it would be "the education par excellence".

Look at what he asked for. Not a mind that never wanders. He took the wandering for granted. The skill is the bringing back, and he put judgment, character and will on top of it. Then, in the next sentence, he admitted it was easier to define this ideal than to give practical directions for it. His only tip was about schoolchildren: a child with more interest in a subject pays better attention. True, and no help at 11 at night with a phone in your hand.

The odd thing is that the directions are in the same book, and James found them twenty years earlier, when he couldn't do this at all.

He was born in New York in 1842 and spent years changing course: painting in Newport, sciences at Harvard, then medicine, then a trip up the Amazon with the naturalist Louis Agassiz, then Germany, where he was ill much of the time. He finished his medical degree in 1869 and fell apart. Back in Cambridge he sank into a depression he hid from almost everyone. Decades later he described one evening, as if it had happened to an anonymous French correspondent, and only later admitted it was him. At twilight, in a dressing room, "there fell upon me without any warning … a horrible fear of my own existence." For months he couldn't go out into the dark alone.

On 30 April 1870 he wrote in his notebook that the day before had been a crisis. He had been reading the French philosopher Charles Renouvier, who defined free will as "the sustaining of a thought because I choose to when I might have other thoughts." Not doing whatever you like: holding one thought on purpose while others push in. James saw no reason that need be an illusion. Then came the famous line: "My first act of free will shall be to believe in free will." He couldn't prove he was free, so he decided to act as if he was for a year and see. He gave himself orders too. Care little for speculation, much for the form of the action. And two lines that are easy to skip: never forget "how one link dropped undoes an indefinite number", and that this day had given him the strong first push that the psychologist Alexander Bain said a new habit needs. A strong start and no dropped links.

Nothing changed overnight. In 1872 Harvard hired him to teach physiology, then psychology. In 1878, the year he married, he agreed to write a textbook. It took twelve years. When he reached attention, he described it as withdrawing from some things to deal well with others. Its opposite is the dazed state where the eyes rest on nothing and the sounds of the world melt together, the paragraph read three times without one word going in. Millions of things reach the senses at every moment, and almost none of them become experience. "My experience is what I agree to attend to." Two people on the same train: one gets an afternoon of thinking done, the other replays Tuesday's argument.

Then the practical part. "There is no such thing as voluntary attention sustained for more than a few seconds at a time." What feels like long focus is a series of efforts that bring the topic back. So a mind that drifts is not proof of failure. The drift is where the skill starts, and being good at it mostly means noticing sooner. Later he went further: "Effort of attention is thus the essential phenomenon of will." Strip willpower down and that is what is left, the same definition he held on to in 1870.

The directions he said he couldn't give are in his chapter on habit. "The great thing in all education is to make our nervous system our ally instead of our enemy." The more of daily life we hand to "the effortless custody of automatism", the more the higher powers of the mind are free for their real work. The most miserable person, he wrote, is the one for whom every cigar, every cup, every bedtime and the start of every piece of work is a fresh decision. Swap the cigar for a phone. Then four rules for a new habit, the first two from Bain:

1. Launch with as strong and decided a start as you can. Make the old way harder, and pledge it in public if you can.
2. Never allow an exception until the habit is rooted. Each lapse is like dropping a ball of string you are winding: one slip undoes more than many turns wind again.
3. Act on every resolution at the first chance you get, while it is still warm.
4. Keep the faculty of effort alive with "a little gratuitous exercise every day": do something small for no reason except that you would rather not, so you are in practice when a hard day comes.

These are the two lines from his notebook, written into a textbook twenty years later. The host reads them as training for attention too, and says so openly: decide loudly, no exceptions while it is new, act the first time, and practise a little every day on something that doesn't matter. The exercise can be tiny. Read one page without leaving it. When the mind goes, and it will, notice, come back, and count that as one repetition.

James was blunt about the cost. In Joseph Jefferson's stage Rip Van Winkle, the drunk excuses every slip with "I won't count this time." James's answer: it is being counted all the same, down in the nerve cells. It works the other way too. People become experts "by so many separate acts and hours of work", and someone who keeps at it can expect to wake "some fine morning" as one of the competent ones of their generation.

Was he right that it matters? In 2010 Matthew Killingsworth and Daniel Gilbert at Harvard used an iPhone app to ask 2,250 adults, at random moments, what they were doing, how they felt and whether their mind was elsewhere. Minds wandered in 46.9% of the samples. Drifting to something pleasant made people no happier than staying with the task, and drifting anywhere else made them less happy. The timing suggested the wandering came first and the unhappiness followed. The same paper is honest about the limit: thinking about what isn't happening is how people learn, reason and plan. James would agree. He never asked for a mind that stays put. He asked for one that comes back.

## Beliefs that shift

- *Good focus means the mind never wanders.* → Nobody holds voluntary attention for more than a few seconds. Focus is many returns in a row, and the skill is noticing sooner.
- *Willpower is a big force for big moments.* → James found it is effort of attention: holding the hard thing in mind and bringing it back when it slips.
- *Small slips don't count.* → Every lapse and every return is counted. Expertise is built from separate acts, and so is a bad habit.
- *More choices means more freedom.* → Every fresh decision costs attention. Hand the routine to habit and the mind is free for what matters.

## What we learn

- **Agents carry the automatism, people keep the attention.** James's miserable man makes every small choice fresh. In theOS, avens do the routine and people sign changes under declarative policies, so human attention goes to the decisions only humans can make. Samuel's standing rules ("merge your own PRs", "rebuild and restart on every new main yourself, never ask") are habits in James's sense: one decision made once, so it never costs attention again.
- **Only promoted proposals may ask for attention.** The HITL card stack asks only about promoted AIPs, never ideas. That protects the scarce thing. Every new kind of prompt, notification or card should have to earn its place the same way.
- **A strong start and no dropped links.** A daily practice such as the journal Days follows rules 1 and 2: start it loudly and in public, and don't allow an exception while it is new. One skipped day undoes more than one written day builds.
- **Films need places to come back to.** James takes the wandering for granted, and so should our films. The hook-writer's rehooks (headings, act turns, title cards) are the points where a drifting viewer can return. A film is not judged on whether nobody drifts but on whether they come back.
- **Count the returns.** The exercise is to notice the drift and come back, and to count it as a repetition. That is a mechanic a game can reward honestly: practice that counts every return, not only unbroken streaks that one bad day destroys.
