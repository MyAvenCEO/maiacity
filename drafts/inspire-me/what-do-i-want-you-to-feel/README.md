## The story

For years Mridu Mousam Neog, a travel filmmaker, chased the look. First camera movement: gimbals, tracking shots, slow push-ins, transitions. Then the cinematic image: framing, exposure, the grade. They studied other filmmakers' shots, went out and recreated them, and got good at it. Then they found sound design and learned the basics, the whooshes, risers and impacts that make a transition hit and a montage feel energetic.

Looking back, Mridu says they treated sound like everything else, as a tool to make the video look cooler. They weren't thinking about what it did to the viewer. Part of that came from freelance work, where an edit is judged on what is in front of you: does the transition work, does the music hit on time, is it energetic enough. Those things matter. But once they made their own travel films, of places and things that mattered to them, they kept watching the finished film and asking: why doesn't it feel like it did when I was there?

Because when you are somewhere, you don't take it in with your eyes alone. You hear monks chanting in the distance, your own footsteps, the wind, a motorcycle passing. Small sounds you never notice consciously still become part of the memory. They had been treating sound as something that supports the visuals, when it changes how the visuals are experienced. It tells you where to look. It makes you notice what you would have missed. It can make the same shot feel peaceful, tense, lonely or exciting.

So the question changed. Not "what sounds should I add here?" but "what do I want the audience to feel?" Once that is known, the sounds needed are much easier to find.

Then Mridu shows it, building the sound for one snowy trekking sequence from their last travel film from nothing but the camera's own audio. Every shot gets the same question: what do I want you to feel at this exact moment?

1. **Two aerials, the mountains and the valley.** First the place and its scale. A subtle layer of wind, not a storm but something distant and spacious that says how open it is, a little stronger as we move down into the valley. That bed runs under the whole sequence: the camera angles change, the environment doesn't. Played back, the atmosphere is already doing most of the work.
2. **Close-ups: snow landing on a glove, adjusting the headwear.** A wide shot shows how beautiful a place looks; a close-up lets you imagine what it feels like to be there. So the glove gets something delicate, a little texture of snow on a surface over the wind, not every flake exaggerated. The headwear gets fabric movement, perhaps a breath. Sounds we hear all the time without noticing make a shot personal when they come forward at the right moment.
3. **Walking in the snow.** Now it becomes tactile: the crunch of a boot in snow, which almost anyone can imagine, from Foley. But not unnaturally loud. Close enough to notice, still belonging to the place. "I'm not just adding these sounds, I'm deciding what sounds deserve what amount of attention."
4. **Trekking, medium then wide.** Sound follows perspective. In the medium shot, steps, clothing and wind, with the wind a little up and the steps a little down. In the wide shot the walkers are small in a huge landscape, so the steps are pulled back and softened and the ambience leads. The camera makes the distance; the sound confirms it. Footsteps as loud in a wide as in a close-up would cut the picture and the sound apart.
5. **Sitting down to share biscuits.** After a sequence of huge mountains, something entirely ordinary, and the sound turns with it. The wind steps back, and the interaction comes forward: the packet rustling, the biscuits handled, the crunch of a bite. Enough to feel real, never a food commercial. Same snow, but the scene is no longer about the mountains. It's about two people sharing a moment.
6. **The fist bump.** Prayer flags behind, snow driving through the frame. In reality you would never hear the fist bump. Mridu adds a soft, natural thump anyway, so the focus doesn't go entirely to the storm, and brings the wind forward to make the snowfall felt.

Only then music, and not to make it more emotional or dramatic. Out there it is beautiful and peaceful, and also isolating, with the sense that there is much more to the place than you can see. So they look for something peaceful and mysterious at once: an ambient texture, distant tones, a gentle progression that sits under the sounds already built and doesn't take the attention.

## Beliefs that shift

- *A film is made by its picture.* → Being somewhere is as much heard as seen. A film that sounds empty won't feel like the place, however good it looks.
- *Sound design is whooshes, risers and impacts.* → Mostly it is wind, footsteps, cloth and a biscuit packet, each given exactly the attention the moment deserves.
- *Choose sounds to fit the edit.* → Choose a feeling for each shot first. The sound follows from it.
- *Only use sounds the camera could have heard.* → You would never hear a fist bump in a snowstorm. Add it if that is where the viewer should look.
- *Music makes the emotion.* → Music comes last and sits under a feeling the sound has already built.

## What we learn

- **Ask the feeling before the sound.** Our sound-designer skill already says to write a sound script from the story's beats, per beat what the viewer feels and hears. This video is that rule done shot by shot, and it is a good test of a mix: for every clip in a Studio 2 cut we should be able to say in one word what the viewer should feel there. If we can't, the sound is decoration.
- **Spot sounds follow shot size.** The medium-to-wide trek is our "near, middle and far" law made concrete: a close-up brings the steps forward, a wide pulls them back and lets the bed lead. In Studio 2 the mix is data on the cut (each clip's `vol`, `eq`, fades), so a Foley clip's level can be checked against its shot's size, and an agent can propose that pass as an AIP.
- **One bed per place, unbroken.** "The camera angles change, but the environment still remains the same." That is law 3 of the sound-designer skill, and it applies to the 3D worlds too: a world's ambience (the geese, the frogs, the bees, the wind on the tired land) should run under every cut filmed in it, never restart with each shot.
- **Record the place, not just the picture.** Mridu's question, "why doesn't it feel like it did when I was there?", is the reason to record 30 to 60 seconds of ambience and room tone wherever Samuel films, and real field recordings for every world we rebuild from a real place.
- **The small sounds of people.** The biscuit packet turns a landscape film into a film about two people. Our films join huge 3D worlds to Samuel's own life; the cloth, the breath, the mug on the table are what pull a scene from the world back to Samuel.
- **Music last.** Mridu builds every sound before choosing a track, as our score skill says: watch it with voice and beds only, then spot the music. For our worlds, music should sit under the bed and never replace it.
