## The story

A machine puts caps on bottles more than 1,000 times an hour. A cap the right way up stays on the rail. A cap upside down just drops off. Air blows the good ones into a chute, and when a sensor at the top is covered, the feeder turns itself off.

It stands in a shop of about 56 m² (600 sq ft). The brand makes care products for boats and outdoor furniture, such as a mildew stain remover. The owner, Steve, says corporate factories spend millions on automation, and that they did the same job by building everything from scratch.

It began when a product video went viral, with more than 10 million views. They had to make 10,000 bottles in the first month. The first machine they built labelled bottles on its own. Now there is a line. Empty bottles start on a feeding conveyor, and a labeller turns each bottle so it picks up its label, up to 1,800 an hour. An Arduino board they wired themselves sets the conveyor's speed and timing for each bottle size. Next comes a filler that fills bottles as they pass. Then a cap elevator and feeder, and an induction heater that melts the foil seal inside each cap. Plain bottles go in one end and sealed product comes out the other. Last year that line made more than 20,000 bottles.

Everything starts in 55-gallon (about 208 L) mixers under a negative-pressure air filter, filled with drum pumps. The products are not eaten or put on skin, so they need no food-grade equipment and none of the red tape that comes with it. The space works because goods are picked up and delivered every day, and they rent a second room for storage.

The line is not finished. Caps are still screwed on with a drill and a special bit, and an automatic capping head is next. The point is that it doesn't need to be finished all at once. They built one machine, then the next, and they keep spare parts on hand. When something breaks, they fix it in minutes, because they built it.

Then the lesson Steve calls the biggest. A few months in, a batch with a defect went to Amazon, and the bottles moved too fast to stop it. The rating of their stain remover dropped just under four stars. On their own website they could reach the buyers and replace the bad bottles. On Amazon they couldn't even message the customers, who didn't know the problem was one batch. They just thought the product was bad. People leave a bad review after a bad experience far more often than a good one after a great experience. Since then, every batch is tracked.

## Beliefs that shift

- *Automation needs millions.* → It can be built one machine at a time, from cheap parts and an Arduino, by people who then know how to fix it.
- *A small shop caps output.* → Floor space matters less than flow: daily pickups, a second store room and a line that runs on its own.
- *Selling on a big platform is the easy way to grow.* → The platform owns the customer. When one batch went wrong, the brand could fix it on its own site and not on Amazon.

## What we learn

- **Build the line, then own the repair.** The brand fixes a broken machine in minutes because it built the machine. That is the case for open machines like OSE's LifeTrac and Power Cube, which are already among our actors: a settlement that can build and repair its own tools doesn't wait for a vendor.
- **Find the bottleneck before buying speed.** The labeller does 1,800 an hour, the cap feeder about 1,000, and caps are still screwed on by hand with a drill. The slowest step sets the line, and it is the one being automated next. This is exactly what the factory skill simulates: stations, cycle times and the bottleneck that moves when one machine is added.
- **A batch is a version.** After the defect, every batch got its own record. avenDB already keeps every version of every asset as a frozen entry with its history. A COOP that makes physical goods can do the same, so a defect is traced to one batch and the people who bought it.
- **Keep the customer relationship.** On its own site the brand could explain and replace. On Amazon it could do neither. Maia City's posts and stories go out from our own vault and public feed first, and other platforms only get derivatives. A COOP's shop should work the same way.
- **Choose the product class with the paperwork in mind.** Cleaning products need no food-grade line. A village making its first goods can start where the rules are lightest, and grow into food and skin care later.
