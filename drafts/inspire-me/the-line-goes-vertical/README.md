## The story

The big leaps in AI used to come years apart. Then months. The host, Matt Madigan, counts five of them in September 2026 alone. The line isn't rising any more, he says. It's going vertical.

When that happens, whole fields fall one after another. Coding first: by spring, AI was writing most of the code at the companies that build it. Then images, video, animation and music, where a studio's work now takes one person an afternoon. Now mathematics. The video says OpenAI's AI cracked a million-dollar problem that had stood for nearly a century, and that on 6 October it published more than 700 claimed solutions to open problems in a single day. Medicine, robotics, materials, energy and biology are next.

So why doesn't it feel like that? Because it happens in labs, on servers and in papers, not yet in daily life. Look where the experts talk, though, and mathematicians and programmers who spent their lives on these problems are having a reckoning. Some are amazed, some are grieving, and most are still making sense of it.

The reason it speeds up is the scaling laws. Each time AI was made ten times bigger, it got predictably smarter, seven times in a row, and the laws still hold. On top of that come three multipliers that feed each other: more computing power, better ways to train, and AI helping to build the next AI.

The field Matt thinks will change lives most is biology. DNA is code: four letters, about 3 billion of them, roughly 750 MB, less than a film download. AI is learning to read it and write it. There are about 9 million researchers on Earth. Soon, he says, there will be billions more made of software, none of them asleep, doing in weeks what would take us centuries. Picture it: ageing cured, grandparents who look your age, a person at a bar who looks 21 and was born before the moon landing, every disease a footnote. And not just no illness: every day as clear as your best one.

What about work and purpose? Only about one in five workers worldwide is engaged at work, and about half of American adults say they are lonely. We pour years into teams we leave and never see again, which isn't how humans lived for hundreds of thousands of years. When almost everything is abundant, the paycheck stops being the point. Being free to build a lasting tribe around people and a purpose you chose isn't losing meaning, he says. It's getting it back.

He doesn't hide the risk. In a survey of nearly 3,000 AI researchers, the median chance of catastrophe, "p(doom)", was 5%, and his own is about 6%. That is why safety matters. But slowing down has a cost too. About 60 million people die each year, most of them from diseases of ageing, so every year of delay means tens of millions more who miss the cut-off.

Last, why so few people see it coming: our gut is bad at big numbers. Fold a sheet of paper in half 42 times and it would reach the Moon. The galaxy has hundreds of billions of stars, so even one civilisation per billion stars means hundreds of them here alone. Yet light takes 100,000 years to cross the galaxy, and a century of our radio has reached less than a millionth of it, so two civilisations meeting is close to impossible. The gut says no one is out there. The numbers say there are many, too far apart to meet. The gut says next year will look like this one. The numbers say otherwise.

## Beliefs that shift

- *AI is improving steadily, like any other technology.* → The gaps between leaps are shrinking, because scale, training and AI-built AI multiply each other.
- *Ageing is a fact of life.* → If DNA is code that can be read and written, the diseases of ageing become problems to solve, and the host says they're starting to be solved.
- *Without jobs, people lose their purpose.* → Most people aren't engaged at work and many are lonely. Abundance could free them to build lasting groups around a purpose they chose.
- *If it were real, I would feel it.* → Intuition fails on exponentials. Paper folded 42 times reaches the Moon.

## What we learn

- **The tribe is the village.** The video's answer to "what about purpose" is a lasting group around people and a purpose you chose. That is what a COOP is: one hex is a settlement, seven hexes a village, owned by the people who live in it, built to last a lifetime and longer.
- **Abundance is only shared if ownership is shared.** Billions of software researchers belong to someone. "Five futures, one leash" showed how the cure for ageing becomes a toll booth when one company owns it. In maiaCITY the settlement and Village COOPs own their inference hardware, and every human gets 24 HEARTS a day, so "the paycheck stops being the point" is a rule and not a hope.
- **Speed belongs to the agents, the signature to the people.** The host argues against slowing down. theOS doesn't have to pick: avens propose as fast as they can, and people sign every change under declarative policies. A 5% chance of catastrophe is a reason to keep a human hand on every accepted AIP, not to stop the proposals.
- **Train the gut on curves.** Our own numbers compound too: the 7% yearly melt, a village's growth from one hex to seven, energy from one geothermal plant. Show them as curves over decades in the games and the films, the way the paper fold does, so players feel what their gut misses.
- **Plan for long lives.** If ageing really slows, the people who start a village may live in it for a century. That favours what we already chose for other reasons: domes and tunnels built for hundreds of years, and governance that lets rules change by proposal without anyone having to own them for ever.

## Open questions

- The OpenAI claims (a million-dollar problem cracked, 700+ solutions on 6 October 2026) and the five September leaps come without sources in the video. Find OpenAI's own posts and independent checks before quoting. "Claimed solutions" are not verified ones.
- "Ten times bigger, smarter, seven times in a row" is a simplification of the scaling laws. Name the papers it means before quoting.
- The p(doom) survey is probably Grace et al., "Thousands of AI Authors on the Future of AI" (2,778 researchers, 2023). Its 5% median was for extremely bad outcomes such as human extinction. Check the wording.
- The argument that every year of delay costs tens of millions of lives assumes superintelligence will cure ageing, and soon. That is the claim under debate, not a fact.
- "Between 2 and 20 trillion galaxies" comes from a 2016 estimate that later work revised downwards. Check before quoting.
