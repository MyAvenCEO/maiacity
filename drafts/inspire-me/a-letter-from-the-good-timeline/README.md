## The story

Jon Spaihts, who co-wrote *Dune* and *Dune: Part Two*, speaks at Intelligence Everywhere, an event Big Think and the John Templeton Foundation held to launch an AI research fund. The scientists, he says, have spent the day hedging. They know what they don't know and won't claim what they can't defend. "But I am a screenwriter, and my job is to speculate irresponsibly." So he tells a story. He comes from about a hundred years ahead, from a timeline where we got most of this right. Historians there call the moment "when we put the machines in their place" the beginning of the great awakening.

We were slow to see it, he says. The barriers to making AI a pillar of human productivity and happiness were moral and philosophical as much as technical. The machines had to do our bidding and stay out of our business, and the companies building them had to serve the public good as well as profit. The machines were no help with that. Human experts did it, and real change came when we understood that the tools needed purpose as well as power.

It began with repair. On old battlefields, swarms of self-guided machines found and cleared the mines and bombs that drones had scattered, and the wounded earth went back to being farmland. Where AI had once taken artists' and writers' work wholesale, forensic data systems traced the lines of influence and paid royalties to millions of creators, and the living arts had a renaissance. Versions of a universal basic income were tried around the world, so that everyone came along.

Free of predatory surveillance, people shared data on their own terms, and medicine changed: tumour recognition, personal vaccines. Faces were shielded from the cameras, but the same cameras pointed at animals ended lost pets, and showed how many raccoons and coyotes live among us. Matchmaking tuned for happiness, not engagement or spending, found people love, and also tissue donors, collaborators and friends. *We saw that we were not alone.*

Some awakenings looked back. AI helped archaeologists decode lost scripts and dead languages, recover lost crafts from tool marks, and find sites without number under sea, sand and jungle in satellite and lidar data. In his story our ancestors turn out stranger and older than we thought. *We were not alone in history.*

Machine learning became a lens for scientists, letting them see processes too slow or too large for a human eye. Forests talking to each other over centuries through their fungal networks. Primates trading stories. The health of an ecosystem read from bird flocks and insect migrations. Fluency, at last, with dolphins and whales, who became partners in repairing the climate and had some salty things to say about it. *We were not alone on Earth.*

It wasn't all good. In his story, the Kessler event of 2042, a cascade of collisions, made low orbit almost unusable for years. Then swarms of drones cleared the debris, and materials scientists released public-domain coatings so black that later satellites kept the night sky dark for astronomers. Sterilised probes went to every body around the Sun and found life under the ice of Europa and Ganymede. *Not alone in the solar system.* Then come signals from other stars: three in radio, one in light, and a slow one in gravity waves that no human or AI could read until someone showed it to the blue whales. It came from a universe next to ours. *The universe itself is not alone.*

"Our eyes are open," he ends. "We are looking, and we're trying to see."

## Beliefs that shift

- *AI's future is mainly an engineering problem.* → It is a question of purpose: who the tools serve, what they may look at, and who is paid.
- *Privacy and progress pull against each other.* → In his story, progress came from the data people chose to share, after surveillance ended.
- *A good future means surviving the risks.* → It means repair first: clearing mines, paying the artists whose work trained the models, cleaning up orbit.
- *Speculation is unserious.* → A story told from a future that went right shows what to build now, the way scientists' hedging can't.

## What we learn

- **Data shared on our own terms.** His good timeline begins when surveillance ends and people share what they choose. That is avenDB's design: every vault is the user's own and end-to-end encrypted, and anything leaves it only through a share the owner signs, such as a Public share on one post.
- **Trace the work, pay the makers.** The forensic royalty systems are close to what we already build. Every inspire piece names its source and author, every avenDB edit credits the humans who signed it, and Law 5 pays a fifth of every sale to everyone who holds the seller's MIND. Credit and payment follow the chain of work.
- **Tune for happiness, not engagement.** His matchmakers optimise the right thing. Our rule is the same: every simulation optimises one metric agreed with people, and Proposition 3 checks each person every day, not an engagement score.
- **Begin with repair.** In his story the machines first turn battlefields back into farmland. maiaCITY's first world is the tired land, and a settlement's first job is to bring it back to life with a food forest. Our films can tell that order of events: repair first, then abundance.
- **Purpose before power.** "Put the machines in their place" is theOS in four words: avens propose, people sign every change under declarative policies, and no Aven signs off its own change.
- **Tell it from the future.** A story told from a timeline where it went right is a planning tool. A Day or a film can do the same: start from the village a hundred years on and tell the steps back to today.

## Open questions

- All of this is fiction, by the speaker's own description, including the Kessler event of 2042, life on Europa and Ganymede, and the signals. Don't quote any of it as a forecast.
- The "forests socialising" image draws on the wood-wide-web idea. A 2023 review (Karst et al., *Nature Ecology & Evolution*) found the evidence for trees sharing resources and signals through fungal networks much weaker than popular accounts claim. Check before using it as fact.
- The video's description names the story "The Universe Wakes Up". Is the full text published anywhere?
