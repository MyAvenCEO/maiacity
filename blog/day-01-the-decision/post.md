There is a city I have walked through a thousand times, and nobody else has ever been there.

Imagine it with me. It is early. Light comes through glass, and under the glass there are lemon trees. A path of gravel runs between domes — glass and timber over stone — and disappears into a forest that people planted on purpose. A child runs ahead of you, barefoot, in January. There is no engine anywhere. Somebody you know waves from a doorway, and the dome behind them is one they helped raise — with their own hands, next to people they chose. A million people live like this, and every one of them helped build it.

I have seen that morning with my eyes closed for years. Never once did it flicker. It was never a question of whether. Only a matter of when, and how.

There was only one thing wrong with it.

I was the only one who had ever been there.

## A vision that only one person can see has no door

For years it lived in a folder. Sketches, numbers, whole neighbourhoods. On a quiet evening I would open it, walk the streets again, close it. Nobody saw.

Four startups before this taught me exactly what it costs to say a thing out loud. The number somebody points at. The idea that does not survive its first contact with a stranger. I knew that price by heart, and a vision this size felt too precious to pay it.

So it stayed perfect, and private, and useless.

A vision kept private is not safe. It is sealed. Nobody can argue with it, and nobody can walk into it either. It cannot make one single person braver on a Tuesday morning, because nobody knows it is there.

That is the whole difference between a vision and a daydream. A daydream is yours. A vision has a door.

## Martin Luther King did not say "I have a plan"

Standing on the steps of the Lincoln Memorial in August 1963, in front of a quarter of a million people, he said "I have a dream" — and every person on the Mall heard their own inside it.

A dream, a vision — the word matters less than what it does. A plan belongs to whoever wrote it. A vision belongs to everyone who recognises it.

And that was the turn. I was not afraid of the size of the city. I was holding it wrong — like a plan, mine to edit in private — when the entire point of a city of a million co-founders is that it cannot be one person's. Not because one person is too small. Because a city is, by definition, the thing we build together.

The day you say it out loud, it stops being yours.

That is not the risk. That is the point.

## You cannot invite a million people. You can invite one.

Here is where the number tries to stop you.

A million sounds like a first step, and no first step is that size. Nobody has ever founded anything with a million people on day one. Cities do not start as cities.

They start the way a forest starts.

One seed. Then, next to it, a second. Not a leap — a neighbour. Then three, then five, then eight, thirteen, twenty-one: each step exactly as big as the two before it grown together. That is the Fibonacci sequence, written down in 1202, and it is the same rule that packs the seeds in a sunflower and the scales on a pine cone. Nature does not grow in leaps. It grows from what is already there. Follow that rule and you pass a million on the thirty-first step.

Thirty-one steps. Each one only slightly braver than the last.

Imagine the fifth. Five people on a call, arguing about the size of a dome, and one of them has never met the other four. Imagine the thirteenth: a room, a real one, with a whiteboard nobody wants to erase. By the twenty-first, nobody is in every conversation any more, and the city has started to grow without asking anyone's permission — the way living things do.

The first step was mine. One person, all in, out loud, on the day this film was shot. It is the only step anyone ever has to take alone.

The second step is not a million. It is you.

## So this is day one

What changed today is not the vision. The vision never moved.

What changed is who it belongs to.

From today the city gets built in public, in front of everyone: in a game first, then in soil. Every wrong number in the open, and every one that survives. One entry in this journal, every day, until the morning I described at the top is a morning someone actually wakes up to.

Imagine the morning someone I have never met reads this, opens their own folder, and says out loud the thing they have carried for years.

That is the second seed.

The city doesn't exist yet. The vision is out of the drawer, and there is room in it for you.

One. Then two. Then we.

When we unite in vision, the impossible becomes possible.
