# Hook writer

The title sells the click, the first line sells the read, the first three seconds sell the film. A hook is not
written, it is **assembled** from parts — and then pushed as far as the truth goes. Its one job: **grab attention and
make leaving impossible.**

## The anatomy of a hook — every part, named

A complete hook has up to nine parts. Six are the words of the first line (Callaway's power words); three are what the
first two or three lines must do.

| # | Part | Required | What it is | Test |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **SUBJECT** | yes | who it happens to: *I, we, you*, or a named thing (*233 people*, *your home*) | can you point at the word? |
| 2 | **ACTION** | yes | the verb the subject does: *grew, built, live, took out, pick* | is something *happening*? |
| 3 | **END STATE** | yes | what the subject ends up with — the payload; the more it should not be possible, the better | would a stranger say "no way"? |
| 4 | **CONTRAST** | yes | the base state the reader believes now against the new one: *box → dome, supermarket → forest, winter → strawberries* | is the A → B visible in the words? |
| 5 | PROOF | optional | why believe this voice: *again, after four startups, two and a half years, 93.4%* | does it earn trust in ≤ 4 words? |
| 6 | TIME | optional | how fast / when: *in January, every morning, for good, in 90 days* | does it add urgency or wonder? |
| 7 | **ANCHOR** | yes | the feeling the line creates in the first second: curiosity, disbelief, tension, fear of missing out, longing | name the one emotion |
| 8 | **PROMISE** | yes | what the reader gets for staying, in their terms — the line after the hook ("By the end you'll…") | does it answer *is this worth my time?* |
| 9 | **OBJECTION KILLER** | yes | the line that shuts the reader's first reflex (*"that's a village with a vegetable patch"*, *"I already know this"*) | does it disqualify the obvious answer? |

One phrase often carries two parts (*"before you build it"* is end state and contrast).

**Always annotate.** Every hook shown to Samuel is written out with its parts labelled — never a bare line:

```
HOOK      In January, our children pick strawberries barefoot under a lemon tree.
          SUBJECT our children · ACTION pick · END STATE strawberries under a lemon tree
          CONTRAST January (winter outside) ↔ summer fruit, barefoot · TIME in January
          ANCHOR disbelief
PROMISE   By the end you'll know how a home holds a summer through the whole winter.
OBJECTION Not a greenhouse you visit. The house they live in.
```

If a part cannot be pointed at, the hook is not finished.

## The extreme dial

Write every hook, then turn it up — until the next step would no longer be true:

| Level | Example | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| 1 mild | *Domes are a good way to grow food.* | a topic, no subject acting: bin |
| 2 bold | *We grow food inside our homes.* | a hook, but anyone could say it |
| 3 extreme | *We live inside a glass dome with a forest growing through the living room.* | **aim here** |
| 4 false | *We never eat anything bought, ever again.* | beyond the facts: bin |

Always ship level 3 — the most impossible-sounding line that is still true.

## Push it to the extreme

Every hook goes as extreme as the facts allow. Take the biggest end state that is true, the sharpest contrast with
what the reader believes, the most concrete number. If a hook could be said about any project, it is too mild.

- Ask: what is the most impossible-sounding thing that is *true* here? That is the payload.
- Ask: what does the reader believe right now that this breaks? That is the contrast.
- Then cut every word that does not carry one of those two.

Extreme is never false: no invented numbers, no promise the body cannot pay off. A true extreme beats a vague
superlative every time ("233 people, almost nothing from further than a few minutes' walk" — not "a revolutionary
way of living").

## Anchor and promise — the two jobs of every hook

A hook does more than grab attention: it gives the reader an **irresistible reason to keep going**. That takes two
things, in the first line or the first two:

1. **An emotional anchor** — a feeling in the first seconds, not just noise: curiosity, tension, fear of missing out,
   sheer disbelief. *"Creating content completely changed my life in just 90 days"* makes the viewer ask *how — and
   could I?* The six power words (below) build the anchor; the end state and the contrast are where the feeling lives.
2. **A clear promise** — what the reader gets for staying, in their terms. Readers care about what they get, not about
   us, our setup or our project. The promise answers the question in their head: *is this worth my time?* Big
   creators say it plainly — "in this video…"; in a post it is the line right after the hook: *"By the end you will
   know the number, and why it changes how you would want to live."* A promise the body cannot keep is worse than
   none.

Anchor without promise is a thrill that lets them leave; promise without anchor is a brochure. Both, and the hook
becomes irresistible.

**Head off the objection.** The reader's first reflex — *"I already know this"*, *"that's not possible"*, *"not for
me"* — is answered before they act on it: *"Before you think it's a village with a vegetable patch — it isn't."* The
line after the hook disqualifies the obvious answer.

## The six power words

A hook is built from six parts (after Callaway's "six hook power words"). Four are required.

| # | Part | What it is | Ours, e.g. |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Subject** | who it happens to: I, we, you, or a named thing | *we*, *you*, *233 people* |
| 2 | **Action** | the verb the subject does | *grew*, *took out*, *live inside* |
| 3 | **End state** | what the subject ends up with — the more it should not be possible, the better | *a forest that feeds them* |
| 4 | **Contrast** | the base state the reader believes now, against the new one — it intensifies the A → B | *one tent → 233 people*, *box → dome* |
| 5 | Proof (optional) | why this voice is worth hearing: "again", "after four startups", "two and a half years" | needed to teach, skipped for a scene |
| 6 | Time (optional) | how long, how fast, when: "in January", "in one afternoon", "before you build it" | speed makes a result more desirable |

The parts can be single words or whole phrases, and one phrase often carries two (in *"walk through your home
before you build it"*, "before you build it" is both end state and contrast).

**Point at them.** Before keeping a hook, point at its subject, its verb and its contrast. If you cannot point at all
three, it is an aphorism, not a hook — and aphorisms are our recurring failure: *"The shape you live in was never
chosen for you."* *"216 people, and not one more."* They sound finished, which is the problem: nobody is doing
anything, and there is nothing to click for.

**Name the end state, never the topic.** A topic is what the post is about; an end state is what the reader walks
away with.
- topic: *geodesic domes are thermally efficient* → end state: *the heating bill leaves and the forest moves in*
- topic: *why I am building in public* → end state: *a city with a million co-founders, and the first one is me*

## The question hook (Jenny Hoyos)

The strongest opening is often **a strange, sharp question** — asked in the first line, with no setup before it:
*"Is fast food really as fast as they say?"* People stay to the end because they want the answer.

- The question must be odd enough that the reader cannot guess the answer, and concrete enough to be answered.
- It is the arching question of the whole piece (`arc.md`): the answer lands only at the end.
- Build it from the same parts: a subject, an action, an end state that sounds impossible, a contrast.
- A number the reader cannot guess makes a perfect answer ("How many people does it take to …?").

## The title card — the hook as a picture

The hook is also set into the day's title cards (`scripts/film/thumbnail.mjs`; four ratios 16:9, 1:1, 9:16, 5:2 — the
5:2 is the blog's wide header and the X Article cover):
- **Set like a YouTube thumbnail:** few words, heavy, readable at phone size; the words that carry the end state big
  (gold for the new world).
- **Always "DAY NN" in a gold-edged badge in the bottom-right corner** of every card (read off the day's folder, or
  `"day"` in `thumbnail.json`).
- **The contrast as a picture — the split card.** When the hook is a before/after, show both: the old world on the
  left (or on top in a square or tall card), cold and grey; the new world on the right (below), warm; a gold seam
  between; each half carries its half of the title — the old in white, the new in gold. Day 02: *"Every city on earth
  is **built wrong.**"* over Manhattan's steel towers above a jammed avenue, *"This is what **starting over** looks
  like."* over domes along a river in their forest. `thumbnail.json`: `"split": { "old": <cid>, "new": <cid> }` and
  `"title": { "old": { kicker, big }, "new": { kicker, big, after } }`.
- **The new world is always the day's own footage** — a frame from that day's article or film, never one borrowed
  from another day. The old world may be generated when the day has none: fal, the latest GPT Image
  (`openai/gpt-image-2.5/sunburst/text-to-image`), photoreal, the same high drone vantage as the new-world frame,
  the subject centred so every crop keeps it; then `bun media add … --tags "Day NN,role:frame"`.
- Otherwise one hero frame with the title over a firm shade, placed so it never crosses a face.

## Compact

- **A title: at most ~10 words.** A title card: readable in one second at phone size. A spoken film hook: within the
  first ~10 s, one breath.
- One sentence is usually the whole hook. The line after it disqualifies the obvious answer (*"Not the overworked.
  Not the underpaid."*) — the reader's first guess is the biggest threat to the second paragraph.
- Can a word come out? Take it out. Titles die of length.
- Banned: colon subtitles, listicles ("7 ways to…"), clickbait the body does not pay, a question the subtitle answers.

## Rehooks

Every heading, every act of a film, every new slide is a **rehook**: a statement that opens a new question, never a
label.
- label *"The dome factories"* → rehook *"Every dome cell owns at least one dome factory"*
- label *"Level 6"* → rehook *"The last level builds nothing"*
- label *"My decision"* → rehook *"You cannot invite a million people. You can invite one."*

## The process

1. Write **at least ten** hooks, from different angles: the question, the impossible scene, the number, the
   contradiction, the before/after — each written out with its anatomy (above) and turned up the extreme dial.
2. Score each: subject · action · end state · contrast pointable? As extreme as the truth allows? Pays off in the body?
   Understandable to someone who has never heard of maiaCITY (no inside words, no term not yet introduced)? Compact?
3. Keep one for the first line, one for the title, one for the title card — they may differ, and must not repeat
   each other word for word.
4. Write the hooks **last** in the draft, once the body is known, and rewrite them more than anything else.

## Copy work

When a hook lands anywhere — a video, a headline, a thread — copy it out, mark its six parts, then swap our subject,
action and end state into the same frame. Rhythm is learned by copying, not by theory. Keep the best in the
inspire-me cards (`inspire-me/`).
