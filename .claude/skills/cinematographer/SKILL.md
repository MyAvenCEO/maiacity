# Cinematographer

The cinematographer turns the director's shots into pictures: where the camera stands, what lens it wears, how it
moves, how bright the image is — the same craft for Samuel's iPhone and for the virtual camera of the worlds, so both
cut together as one film.

## The cinematographer's laws

1. **Set the visual rules in the opening, then keep them.** Bruce Block's seven components — space, line, shape, tone,
   colour, movement, rhythm — mean what the opening teaches them to mean; Storaro finds one governing idea in the
   script and annotates it scene by scene (`composition.md`).
2. **Visual intensity follows story intensity.** Contrast in a component raises intensity, affinity lowers it. Plan
   each component under the story's intensity curve; spend the biggest contrast on the climax and resolve with affinity
   (Block; `storyteller`, `retention.md`).
3. **A few lenses, and move the camera rather than reach for a longer one.** Deakins works mostly at 32–50 mm and
   calls a longer lens instead of moving closer lazy. Fix three or four per film, for the phone and the world alike
   (`lenses.md`).
4. **Choose a stance on faces:** close and wide, immersed with the place (Lubezki), or further back on a normal lens,
   observed (Deakins) — per scene, on purpose (`lenses.md`).
5. **Eye level by default;** every other angle is a statement — high diminishes, low empowers, a dutch tilt disturbs
   (Mascelli). Close-ups at the subject's eye height.
6. **Build depth from cues:** layers in front, middle and back, overlap, parallax as the camera moves, separation by
   light and tone (Block, Mascelli) (`composition.md`).
7. **Thirds as a habit, the centre as a statement** — Deakins hasn't consciously used the thirds since art college;
   Kubrick, Anderson and Fraser frame dead centre when the moment is about order or confrontation (`composition.md`).
8. **Motivate every move,** and keep it running through the cut (`movement.md`). Handheld means subjective, the tripod
   means observed — never alternated at random (Lubezki's *Birdman* logic).
9. **Protect the highlights, then expose as bright as they allow** (Lubezki, ETTR); keep the shadows' detail rather
   than lifting the blacks (Morrison). In the worlds: meter, don't grade (`exposure.md`).
10. **Simplify.** The subject and the frame matter more than the camera (Deakins); fewer things in frame, so the
    audience reads the story (Fraser).

## A visual bible for every film (one page)

- The story's intensity graph, with space (flat → deep), colour (`dip` → `bright`) and movement (still → moving)
  plotted under it.
- The lens set (three or four) and the sun hours of every scene.
- Six to ten reference frames.
- Where the strongest contrast of shot size sits (the climax), and where affinity resolves it.
