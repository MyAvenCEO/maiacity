# Structure: three acts, scaled to a short film

The masters' structures are the same few landmarks under different names. They are a reference for where the turns
fall, never a template to fill — and on our scale they compress into seconds.

## The landmarks

- **Syd Field's paradigm** (on a 120-page script): set-up pp. 1–30, confrontation 30–90, resolution 90–120 (1 : 2 : 1).
  The inciting incident within about 10 pages; Plot Point I at 20–30, the first pinch near 45, the midpoint near 60,
  the second pinch near 75, Plot Point II at 85–90. A pinch pulls the story back to its central conflict — what is at
  stake, and what opposes it. Field himself: the form matters, not the page numbers.
- **The sequence approach** (Frank Daniel, Paul Gulino): eight sequences across the three acts (2 + 4 + 2), each with
  its own tension and release, like a short film.
- **Save the Cat** (Blake Snyder): the beat sheet below, with its percentages.
- **McKee's chain** (*Story*): inciting incident → progressive complications → crisis (the hardest choice) → climax →
  resolution. The climax proves the controlling idea: a value plus its cause.
- **Dan Harmon's circle** (taught for five-minute pilots, so it scales to one episode): comfort, need, go, search, find,
  take, return, change.
- **Vogler's hero's journey** — for the season's arc, not the day's.
- **Vonnegut's shapes:** plot good fortune against time; a story needs at least one fall and one rise. Across 1,327
  books, the most read shapes were rise-then-fall, fall-rise-fall, and two "man in a hole" arcs back to back (Reagan et
  al., 2016).

## Save the Cat in seconds

| Beat | % | 2 min | 5 min | 10 min |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opening image and the promise | 0–1 | 0–1 s | 0–3 s | 0–6 s |
| Theme stated | 5 | 6 s | 15 s | 30 s |
| Set-up (the pain: `arc.md`) | 1–10 | to 12 s | to 30 s | to 60 s |
| Catalyst — the inciting incident | 10 | 12 s | 30 s | 60 s |
| Debate | 10–20 | 12–24 s | 30–60 s | 60–120 s |
| Break into Two (Plot Point I) | 20 | 24 s | 60 s | 120 s |
| B story (carries the theme) | 22 | 26 s | 66 s | 132 s |
| Fun and games (the pieces: `arc.md`) | 20–50 | 24–60 s | 60–150 s | 120–300 s |
| First pinch (Field) | ≈ 37 | 45 s | 112 s | 225 s |
| Midpoint: a false win or false loss, stakes up, often a clock | 50 | 60 s | 150 s | 300 s |
| Second pinch (Field) | ≈ 62 | 75 s | 188 s | 375 s |
| Bad guys close in | 50–75 | 60–90 s | 150–225 s | 300–450 s |
| All is lost | 75 | 90 s | 225 s | 450 s |
| Dark night of the soul (the breath beat) | 75–80 | 90–96 s | 225–240 s | 450–480 s |
| Break into Three (Plot Point II) | 80 | 96 s | 240 s | 480 s |
| Finale — the question answered | 80–99 | 96–119 s | 240–297 s | 480–594 s |
| Final image, mirroring the opening | 99–100 | last 1 s | last 3 s | last 6 s |

**YouTube compresses the front.** Its "Intro" metric is the share still watching at 30 s, and MrBeast's guide counts
losing a third of viewers in minute one as good. So in a 10-minute film the promise and the intention land in the first
30 s, and the catalyst at 60 s becomes a second, deeper inciting incident. At 2 and 5 minutes the percentages already
fit.

## Tension and release cycles

- **A feature:** a beat lasts seconds, a scene 2–3 minutes, a sequence 10–15, an act about 30.
- **Long-form YouTube:** MrBeast's guide gives minutes 0–1, 1–3, 3–6 and 6–end each their own job; LifeOfRiza keeps
  pushing through the first two minutes until the point of the video has landed.
- **A short (under 60 s):** one cycle — the question in the first line, the answer last, something changing every
  4–8 s (`retention.md`).
- **Our scaling** (one landmark every ~12.5 % of the runtime): a full cycle of ~25–30 s in a 2-minute film, ~60–75 s at
  5 minutes, ~2–2.5 minutes at 10. Each cycle closes a small question and opens a bigger one.
- **Rehooks at the landmarks** — Plot Point I, both pinches, the midpoint, all is lost, Plot Point II: about every
  15 s in a 2-minute film, every 35–40 s at 5 minutes, every ~75 s at 10 (derived, not sourced; it matches the common
  "every one to two minutes").

## For a daily documentary series

- **The story is already in the day — log it.** For every real moment: what he wanted, what happened, how that differs
  from what he expected, whether the value went up or down. Those gaps are the turns (McKee). Only what really happened
  becomes a beat.
- **The opposition must be real:** physics, time, money, one person against the size of the task, how cities are built
  today. If the day has no setback, the low is the honest distance between today and the vision — never an invented
  failure.
- **The 3D vision gives the promise and the dramatic irony.** Show the end state at 0–5 s: the audience knows the
  destination while Samuel, in the footage, stands at one step of the way — Hitchcock's informed audience. Use it again
  at the midpoint (what is at stake) and in the final image (now against then). Give vision shots one consistent look
  and sound, so a render is never mistaken for something already built.
- **One episode is one sequence:** it resolves its own question while the series' question — the city — stays open.
- **Write the ending first** (Pixar, Stanton): the voice-over is written once the day's ending is known.
- **Wrap the new in the familiar.** People need something familiar before they accept something new (LifeOfRiza): the
  unfamiliar vision travels in a familiar vlog grammar.
