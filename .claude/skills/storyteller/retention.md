# Retention: tension and release

A reader stays while there is something they still want. Retention is a rhythm of **tension and release**: open a
question, hold it with real material, release it — and open a bigger one the moment you do.

## The intensity curve

Plan every piece as a curve of beats on a 1–5 scale before drafting. Day 19's:

| hook | sunrise | dip | warmth | wonder | breath | pride | tender | silence | hope |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2 | 5 | 1 | 3 | 5 | 3 | **5 (highest)** | 2 | – | 4 |

- Alternate: a high only reads against a low, and a low only means something because a high follows it.
- **The biggest drop comes just before the warmest rise.** The false summit (`arc.md`) sits right before the peak.
- The peak comes late — never in the first half.
- End lower than the peak but warmer than the start.

## Contrast creates the feeling — in every sense

The strongest moments are the ones set hardest against their neighbours. Push the contrast on every axis available:

- **Story:** abundance against how we live now; the one against the many; the box against the dome; winter outside
  against a lemon tree inside.
- **Scale:** a macro detail against the whole valley (ECU of dew → EWS of the cell).
- **Writing:** a three-word line after a long paragraph; a three-line section next to a six-paragraph one; a scene
  against a number.
- **Pace:** calm holds against fast montages; a breath beat with no voice.
- **Light and colour:** day against night; one cold, desaturated grade for the low (`COLD`, the `dip` mood) against
  a warm, saturated high (`BRIGHT`). The world never looks grey otherwise.
- **Sound:** a cold open of birds only; the music dropping to a single cello drone for the low; a burst of rhythm for
  the turn back up; near silence before the last line.
- **Energy at a turn:** joy after a low is a lift in loudness, rhythm *and* brightness at once. A soft return reads
  as more of the same — the contrast doesn't land.

If a turn does not land, the fix is almost always more contrast, not more words.

## Curiosity and payoff

Curiosity keeps them going; a payoff lets them stop. Every piece is a tug-of-war between the two.

- **Never name the mechanism early.** The moment you write the word the reader already knows — "geodesic dome",
  "permaculture", "index fund" — the loop shuts. Describe what it does, what breaks without it, what everyone gets
  wrong. Name it only when the naming is itself the surprise.
- **Delay is not stalling.** Spend the wait on the obstacle: what most people do instead, the number that ruins the
  easy answer. A reader waits a long time if every line pays them something.
- **Pay off in pieces**, and open the next loop as you close one.
- **The retention test:** mark the first place a reader could leave satisfied. If it is not in the last section, the
  payoff landed too early — move it, or open something they still want after it.

## Pace (film)

- The visual hook within 3 s, the spoken hook within about 10 s.
- Change something every 4–8 s: the shot, the size, the movement, the sound.
- Shot length varies by act: calm 5–6 s, montage 1–2.5 s, awe holds 10–14 s.
- **Breath beats** carry no voice: the pause after the sunrise line, the 3.8 s before "Thirteen domes", the frogs
  before the ending.
- Captions always on, two lines at most, short phrases.
- **Loop ending:** the last frame lands where the replay flows on.

## Suspense and curiosity

- **Tell the audience the stakes; hold back only the answer.** If they know about the bomb under the table, they get
  fifteen minutes of suspense; if they don't, fifteen seconds of surprise (Hitchcock). Reveal the stakes and the clock;
  keep the outcome — and the mechanism — hidden.
- **Curiosity is a gap in what we know, and it pulls harder the closer the answer comes** (George Loewenstein): the last
  obstacle goes late, near the answer.
- **Drama is anticipation mixed with uncertainty** (Stanton); make a promise in the first moments that the time will pay
  off, and keep it.
- The "Zeigarnik effect" (that we remember unfinished tasks better) doesn't replicate reliably — don't lean on it. The
  urge to finish an interrupted thing (Ovsiankina) holds up better.

## The retention playbook, second by second

- **0–3 s:** the strongest true image — the one the title and the thumbnail promised. Nothing before the promise: no
  greeting, no logo.
- **3–30 s:** the spoken hook is the arching question; then the intention, the obstacle and the stakes; then the
  promise of the payoff; and a small payoff before 30 s (YouTube: move compelling moments earlier).
- **At every landmark** (about every 12.5 % of the runtime, `structure.md`): a value turn, a rehook line that opens a new
  question, a small payoff, and a pattern interrupt.
- **Pattern interrupts:** a change of camera set-up (handheld ↔ tripod, a whip between them); music before the scene; a
  drop to silence or sound alone; a cut to the 3D vision; a question on screen; a moment of surprise and delight, an
  Easter egg. A story that goes static dies (Stanton).
- **Before the end:** the low, then the choice, then the answer — fast. The final image echoes the first. A door to
  tomorrow, never a recap. On a short, the last frame flows into the first.
- **Short and long:** a short is one question and one turn — YouTube reports Shorts as "viewed vs swiped away", so the
  first frame earns the stay. A long film stacks sequences, each closed by a release that opens the next question.

## Mistakes the pros avoid

- Equal intensity everywhere; the peak in the first half (McKee).
- Keeping the stakes secret where suspense was possible (Hitchcock).
- A thumbnail promise the first minute doesn't deliver (MrBeast's guide).
- Long talk over a static picture (LifeOfRiza).
