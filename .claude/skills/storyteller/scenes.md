# Scenes: every scene a step forward

A story moves when every scene changes something. The test is LifeOfRiza's question — *what new information does the
audience get in this scene?* — and its answer must push the story one step towards the answer of the arching question
(`arc.md`). A scene that only shows something pretty, or repeats what we know, is a scene where the story stops.

## The next-button test

Before a scene is written or shot, write it as one line:

```
SCENE  <what happens>
NEW    <what the audience learns that it did not know>
TURN   <what is different at its end: a value up or down, a question opened or closed>
LINK   because of <the scene before> / against <the scene before>
```

If NEW is empty, cut the scene or give it a job. If LINK can only say "and then", it is in the wrong place or has no
place. If two scenes could swap places, the story is broken (`arc.md`, "The machine").

## How LifeOfRiza moves a vlog forward

Kariza (LifeOfRiza, about 100,000 subscribers three weeks after her first vlog) plans every vlog like a film and calls
it "based on a true story": real life, re-enacted and shaped. From her interview with Digital Spaghetti
(`inspire-me/a-vlog-based-on-a-true-story/`):

- **Ground rules for every video** — a box big enough to play in:
  1. a three-act narrative: lead into a problem or a conflict, resolve it by the end;
  2. keep it simple, fun and playful;
  3. one banger cinematic shot (a dolly, a clamped camera, something no one expects from a vlog);
  4. bonus: one moment of surprise and delight.
- **Every scene is purposeful.** Packing a bag is getting from point A to point B. She asks what new information
  each scene gives and calls it the scene's job to push the story forward.
- **Do something while you talk.** An activity makes talking to a camera easier and lets the scene move. Her improv
  rule: the talk is about something else than the activity — processing two things at once gives the scene a little
  tension, and the activity can pay off later (reaching for the headphones as she says she will try to vlog).
- **Introduce people through action, never a line of introduction.** Her friend is already waiting on the curb, used
  to this — it says who she is and what their friendship is like. A dynamic with friction (Ferris and the reluctant
  Cameron) is a scene; two people who agree are not.
- **Let picture, music or sound tell the next beat.** A question ("What are you doing tomorrow?"), then the music
  comes in, then — four or five seconds later — we see where they are going. Trust the audience to put the pieces
  together; telling them what happens next is holding their hand. Varying the teller (voice-over, shots, music,
  sound effects) is also pacing.
- **Keep the momentum.** After one or two lines to the tripod, the scene moves on (a cut to handheld, a whip pan, a
  new place). Talking too long in one frame slows the story down, most of all in the first two minutes, before the
  point of the video has landed.
- **Leave the real moments in.** Bloopers and moments that really happened in real time keep a planned vlog personal.
- **A world, art-directed.** Details that build a cinematic world (a designed parcel label, twine round the mail,
  pops of complementary colour) and Easter eggs for whoever looks closely (`production-designer`).
- **Plan so the shooting day is easy:** script → shot list → stick-figure storyboard, sometimes the song chosen
  before shooting (`director`, `shot-list.md`).

## When little happens outside: the story is what changed

What people connect with is not what happened but what changed in someone (Tim Runia, `inspire-me/what-changed-not-what-happened/`).
- **Open in the middle of the action and hold back three things:** why, where to, and what came before. The viewer forms
  the question themselves — and that question does more work than any explanation.
- **Say the viewer's question out loud, and promise to come back to it** ("you're right to ask — we'll get to that"):
  one sentence makes a deal that this matters and will return. Plan the opening as a list of the questions you won't
  answer yet, and what you promise to pay off.
- **A messy script means you drifted from the one emotional question.** Step back, ask what it is really about,
  emotionally, and the distractions fall away — and the filming and cutting decisions get easier.
- **When the events are quiet, build the plot from inner states.** List them in order (excitement, rush, doubt,
  pressure, fear, realisation) and make each scene a different feeling about the same question. Ask "what changes
  because of it?", not "what happens?".
- **Let a symbolic image land before any words** — the ice cream melting untasted. Add the voice-over's view later, only
  if it helps; some will read it differently, and that is the viewer doing the work.

## The vlog based on a true story — what is allowed

A maiaCITY film may plan, re-enact, compress time, stage a scene again for the camera and design the details of its
world. It may never invent what happened: no event, result, attempt, number or audience reaction that is not true
(`writing.md`, "Facts only"). Re-enacted is fine; made up is not.

## The mechanics of a forward-moving scene

- **But / therefore, never "and then".** Each beat follows the last because of it (therefore) or against it (but); "and
  then" means nothing changed (Trey Parker and Matt Stone).
- **Intention and obstacle — early.** Someone wants something and something is in the way: Aaron Sorkin's driveshaft of
  every story, which only starts when the want appears. Make the stakes urgent and sayable: what happens if he fails?
  (Pixar).
- **Every scene turns a value.** Write the value's charge (+ or −) at its start and its end; if it hasn't changed,
  nothing happened — cut or rewrite. A scene turns through action or revelation (Robert McKee).
- **The gap between expectation and result.** He acts expecting one answer and the world answers otherwise, or harder;
  that gap is the surprise, and it forces the next intention (McKee).
- **Escalate, but vary.** Complications grow to a point of no return and never fall back to smaller ones; but if every
  scene screams, the audience stops hearing — alternate the charges (McKee).
- **Choices under pressure show who someone is.** The harder the choice costs, the more it reveals (McKee); we are
  gripped by *why* someone acts, not by what they do (Lisa Cron).
- **Setups and payoffs.** A rifle on the wall must go off (Chekhov). Plant early what pays later; whatever saves the day
  at the end is set up at the start; a coincidence may get the hero into trouble, never out of it (Pixar).
- **Give 2 + 2, not 4.** Let the audience work for its meal without noticing (Andrew Stanton) — LifeOfRiza's music
  before the answer.
- **Enter late, leave early.** Start a scene as close to its turn as possible and leave as soon as it has turned.

## The scene test, before handing a script on

- [ ] Every scene has a NEW and a TURN; none could swap places with its neighbour
- [ ] Every scene links to the last by because or against, never "and then"
- [ ] Someone wants something in every scene, and something is in the way
- [ ] People are introduced through what they do, not through a line about them
- [ ] At least one beat is told by picture, music or sound alone, without words
- [ ] Everything planted pays off; the payoffs land at the turns
- [ ] Re-enacted, compressed and designed where it helps — nothing invented
