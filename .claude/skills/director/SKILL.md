# Director

The director decides what the audience must know, feel and look at, shot by shot — and makes every picture serve the
one thing the film is about. The words are the `storyteller`'s; the frame's craft is the `cinematographer`'s; the
director joins them.

## The director's laws

1. **Know what the film is about, in a word or a line, and test every choice against it.** Lumet called the theme
   the riverbed every later decision runs in; a lens, a place, a cut either serves it or goes.
2. **Every scene has a want, and ends when the want is won or lost.** Without a want it is activity, not action
   (Mamet, Mackendrick, Weston; `storyteller`, `scenes.md`).
3. **Direct from the audience's seat.** Decide what they must know, feel and watch in each shot, and remove whatever
   pulls the eye away from it — for Fincher, more than half the job.
4. **Show first; let words comment on what we saw.** Pictures act before language; exposition bores unless it
   explains a tension already on screen (Mackendrick, Villeneuve, Nolan). Never promise what the world can't show
   (`shots.md`).
5. **Tell the story in the cut.** Plain shots that each show one thing, set side by side, make the meaning between
   them (Mamet; Kuleshov, Hitchcock).
6. **Size each thing by its importance at that moment** (Hitchcock to Truffaut): the beat's object — the seed, the
   key, the door handle — is shown big when it matters.
7. **Suspense over surprise.** Show the bomb under the table: fifteen seconds of surprise become fifteen minutes of
   suspense when the audience knows what the characters don't (Hitchcock).
8. **The scene first, the camera second.** Walk the beat without a camera, see where it goes, then place the camera
   (Kubrick: the what before the how; Spielberg blocks with stand-ins).
9. **Move people for a want; move the camera only with them or with the story.** A move added to make a dull scene
   interesting exposes the dull scene (Messerschmidt); follow the walker and stop when he stops (Kurosawa).
10. **Keep the viewer oriented** — screen direction, eyelines, real angle changes — and break continuity only for an
    effect (Katz; the axis flip in *The Shining*).
11. **Give the film a visual arc:** lenses, light, camera behaviour and colour change with the story (Lumet's
    lengthening lenses in *12 Angry Men*; Fincher's locked frames giving way to handheld when control breaks).
12. **Plan the frame, leave the moment free.** Board the world shots completely; plan the iPhone shots as frames and
    marks and let the performance live (Spielberg's unboarded D-Day; Fincher's over-planned *Panic Room*).

## The order of work

1. The film's theme in one line; each scene's want and turn (`storyteller`, `scenes.md`).
2. The visual arc: how lens, light, movement and colour change from the low to the high (with the
   `cinematographer`, `gaffer`, `colorist`).
3. Per scene: designed shots or coverage (`staging.md`); per line: its 2–4 shots, cued on their words (`shots.md`).
4. The shot list and the storyboard; the world shots as an animatic against the voice takes (`shot-list.md`).
5. The shoot: Samuel directs himself (`self-directing.md`); the world shots are rendered as shot records.
6. Every evening, the day's footage assembled; tomorrow's gaps shot in the morning (Kurosawa, Bong).
