# Staging, coverage and continuity

How a scene is put in front of the camera: where people and things stand and move, how many set-ups it gets, and the
grammar that keeps the viewer oriented across the cuts.

## Coverage or design — choose on purpose

- **Coverage buys options in the edit;** design buys authorship and time. Bong Joon-ho boards every shot and shoots
  no coverage, a few lines per set-up, so the cut is decided on paper. Spielberg's oners fold the wide, the medium and
  the close-up into one move. Katz's triangle gives three set-ups on one side of the line that all intercut.
- **Ours:** world shots are designed completely (a shot record is a camera path — design is cheap there). Samuel's
  real scenes get light coverage: each action wide, medium and close (`cinematographer`, `solo.md`), plus two or three
  inserts per beat.

## Staging and coverage patterns

| Pattern | How it works | When | Effect |
|---|---|---|---|
| Master and coverage (Katz) | a wide of the whole scene, then mediums, over-shoulders, close-ups, inserts | real events whose cut you can't predict | the most options; generic without a point of view |
| The triangle (Katz) | three camera positions on one side of the line | two people talking | any shot cuts to any other |
| The oner (Spielberg) | blocking and camera move together, passing through wide, medium and close | scenes with movement, several people | immersive; the cut disappears |
| Boarded, no coverage (Bong) | only the boarded shots, a few lines each | when you already know the cut | authorship and speed; no rescue footage |
| Staging in depth (Bong, Kurosawa) | people at different depths in one frame | reactions, a shift of power | action and reaction at once |
| Planimetric (Anderson) | the lens square to a back wall, figures in a line; cuts at 90° or 180° | controlled, storybook worlds | formal distance, deadpan |
| One-point perspective (Kubrick) | the vanishing point centred, the subject on it | corridors, avenues, arrivals | inevitability, scale |
| Frame in a frame (Anderson, Bong) | through windows, doors, stairwells | isolation, longing, thresholds | looking into a closed world |
| Follow and stop (Kurosawa) | the camera moves only with the subject and stops with it | walking, working | the camera vanishes |
| Enter behind (Nolan) | the camera walks into a space behind the character | arrivals | the viewer enters as the character does |
| Look, see, react (Hitchcock) | a close-up, what they see, the reaction | discoveries, a face and a world | the meaning made in the cut |
| Weather as motion (Kurosawa) | wind, rain, smoke, drifting light move when people don't | still or empty frames | life without a camera move |
| Lens and height progression (Lumet) | lenses lengthen, the camera lowers across the film | rising pressure | the walls close in |
| Composure arc (Fincher) | locked frames while in control, handheld when control breaks | a change of state | the camera mirrors the mind |
| Axial cut (Kurosawa) | straight in along the lens axis to a much tighter size | emphasis, shock | a punch, not a jump |

## Depth and architecture

- One deep frame shows action and reaction together. Put the scene's layers at different depths (`cinematographer`,
  `composition.md`).
- Architecture frames relationships: doors, windows and stairs make power and isolation visible. In our worlds: the
  dome avenues for one-point perspective; the room's window and door as frames in the frame; height for status.

## Continuity: keeping the viewer oriented

- **The 180° line:** keep the camera on one side of the line between two people (or a person and what they look at);
  screen direction holds, the cut hides. Cross it only on purpose (to disorient) or on a move the viewer sees.
- **The 30° rule:** between two shots of the same subject, change the angle by at least 30° or the focal length by at
  least 20 mm — or the cut jumps. An axial cut to a much tighter size is the accepted exception.
- **Eyelines:** a look off-screen must land where the next shot puts what is seen, on the same side.
- **Match the action:** repeat the overlapping action in each angle so the editor can cut on it (`editor`).
- Murch ranks emotion and story above the space's continuity: give up continuity before you give up the feeling.

## Joining Samuel to the worlds

- **The Kuleshov triad:** Samuel looks (an iPhone close-up) — the dome city appears (a render on his eyeline) — Samuel
  reacts. The city becomes his vision.
- **Same side, same light:** the world shot sits on the same side of the line as the look, its light from the same
  side as the room's (`gaffer`).
- **Look at a fixed mark** where the rendered thing will be, on the side the render will match.
- **Shoot the world like a crew would:** eye height, a few fixed lenses, and the iPhone's equivalent focal length on
  any world shot that intercuts with footage (`cinematographer`, `lenses.md`).
- **The world as an animatic:** cut the renders against the voice takes, with placeholder frames for the iPhone
  shots, before filming (Anderson voiced a whole animatic for *The Grand Budapest Hotel*).

## Shooting for the edit

- Write the cut into the shot list: each shot's first and last image and what it hands on (Mamet; Bong's boards carry
  the edit points).
- Start on an empty frame and walk in; end by walking out, keeping screen direction.
- Handles: roll 2–3 s before the action and hold 2–3 s after (Bong once apologised to his editor for calling cut too
  early). Two or three inserts per beat (hands, objects, the window) to compress time and hide jump cuts.
- In the first 30 seconds, open on the image that poses the film's question and show what is at stake, so the rest
  plays as suspense (Hitchcock; YouTube measures the share still watching at 30 s).

## Numbers

- **Shots per beat:** designed — one; Bong films a few lines per set-up. A oner: one 1–3 minute shot for a scene.
  *Parasite*: about 960 cuts in 132 minutes, one every ~8 s.
- **Takes:** Fincher about 50 per scene on *Gone Girl* (press reports); Lumet found an actor's instinct wears out with
  repetition — change the action instead of repeating the take.
- **Rehearsal:** Lumet rehearsed two weeks and ended with a graph of where each level of emotion belonged — set the
  energy of every beat before the shoot.
- **Murch's Rule of Six:** emotion 51 %, story 23 %, rhythm 10 %, eye-trace 7 %, the 2D plane 5 %, 3D space 4 % —
  give up the lower ones first.
- **The show test:** a scene should be 60–80 % understandable with its dialogue in a foreign language (Mackendrick).

## Mistakes the pros avoid

- Moving the camera to rescue a dull scene (Messerschmidt).
- Directing a result ("be angrier") instead of a playable action ("plead") (Weston).
- Exposition before tension; saying the same thing twice (Mackendrick, Nolan).
- Anything in frame that pulls the eye off the point (Fincher).
- Zooming at a still subject; a move that keeps going after the subject stops (Kurosawa).
- Calling cut before the action ends (Bong).
- Surprise where suspense was possible (Hitchcock).
- Following the subject around instead of choosing shots (Mamet).
