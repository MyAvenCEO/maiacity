# Pacing, and its alternatives

Pace is not speed. Karen Pearlman (*Cutting Rhythms*) splits it into three things the editor controls:
- **Timing:** which frame, how long, where.
- **Pacing:** the rate of cutting, plus the rate of change inside the shots, plus the rate of events.
- **Trajectory phrasing:** how the movement energy of one shot links to, or collides with, the next.

They play over three rhythms at once: the physical (the movement of image and sound), the emotional (feeling crossing
faces) and the event rhythm (the structure). All of them drive cycles of tension and release (`storyteller`,
`retention.md`).

**Duration is relative.** A 10 s shot feels long among 1 s shots and short among 60 s ones; shorter footage doesn't
automatically feel faster (Pearlman, Joe Walker). A hold is earned by the compressed material before it.

## The pace menu

| Strategy | Typical shot length | Use it for |
|---|---|---|
| Steady pulse | 4–7 s | explaining, a calm stretch of story |
| Intensified dialogue | 2–4 s, cut on lines and reactions | an argument, energy |
| The long hold | 10–30 s | awe, dread, a performance — let it play |
| Staccato montage | 1–2.5 s | compression, lists, the pieces of an answer |
| Accelerando | shrinking shots with rising content | the run-up to a climax |
| Release | lengthening shots | the aftermath; letting meaning settle |
| The breath beat | picture without voice, on ambience or music | after a key line; between movements |
| The contrast cut | an abrupt change of tempo | resetting attention; making the next hold feel longer |
| Music-locked | cut to the score's bars and phrases, accents varied | montage, travel |
| The hidden-cut take | unbroken time, cuts hidden in moves or darkness | an immersive walk through a place |

**Evidence for the numbers.** Classical Hollywood averaged 8–11 s a shot, the 1980s 5–7 s, a typical film around 2000
3–6 s (David Bordwell); recent films about 4 s (James Cutting). The fastest features run 1.8–2.7 s; long-take directors
8–12 s. Tarantino and Sally Menke set slow-cut dialogue against fast-cut action. Walker slowed down so the Atreides
family's danger mattered; Hamilton made a goodbye wordless.

## Choosing the pace for a beat

1. **What must the viewer feel?** (Murch)
2. **What must they read** — eyes, a caption, a dense vista? Reading time is the floor of the shot's length.
3. **Where is this beat in the tension cycle?** Rising: accelerate. The peak: a hard cut, or a hold. After: release.
4. **What came before?** Hold after speed; cut after a hold.
5. **How much moves inside the shot?** Movement plays faster than the clock says; a static frame plays slower.
6. **Whose eyes are we in?** (Hamilton)

## Our numbers (from the films so far)

- The visual hook within 3 s, the spoken hook within about 10 s.
- **Something changes every 4–8 s** — and a change doesn't have to be a cut. A push-in, a reveal, a light shift, a sound
  event or a caption counts (Pearlman's rate of change inside the shot); that protects the long holds.
- Shot length by act: calm 5–6 s, montage 1–2.5 s, awe holds 10–14 s.
- Breath beats carry no voice: the pause after the sunrise line, the 3.8 s before "Thirteen domes", the frogs before the
  ending (`storyteller`, `retention.md`).

## YouTube: evidence and lore

- **YouTube's own guidance:** the first 30 s must pay off the title and thumbnail; a dip in the retention graph is a
  place viewers left — move strong material earlier.
- **MrBeast's production guide** gives each stretch of a video its own job (minute one, minutes one to three, three to
  six, six to the end), and counts losing about a third of viewers in minute one as good.
- **"Retention editing"** — loud effects, fast cuts, flashing lights, zero pauses — is already being abandoned by its
  own champions (MrBeast said he wanted to leave the "overstimulated" era).
- **Jump cuts** are native grammar for a talking head on YouTube.
- **No primary data exists** for "a change every N seconds", zoom or pattern-interrupt rules: treat them as lore and test
  them against our own retention graphs. Every upload is a test screening (`process.md`).

## Pace by feeling

- **Set shot length by what each section must feel like** — quicker shots and more movement in the highs; longer shots
  and more space between moments in reflection, so the insight can land. Ask "what does this part need to feel like?",
  not "how fast?" (Tim Runia, `inspire-me/what-changed-not-what-happened/`).
- **Make the turn felt, not announced.** At the peak the subject stops in frame and the music cuts out with a
  turntable's power-down: the form does what the story says — the momentum stops. No chapter card.
