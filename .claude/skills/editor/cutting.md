# The cut: where, and why there

## The Rule of Six

Every cut has six duties, weighted by Walter Murch (*In the Blink of an Eye*):

| Duty | Weight | The question |
|---|---|---|
| Emotion | 51 % | is this true to what the viewer should feel now? |
| Story | 23 % | does it move the story forward? |
| Rhythm | 10 % | is it at the right moment, rhythmically? |
| Eye-trace | 7 % | does the eye land where the last shot left it? |
| The 2D plane | 5 % | does screen direction hold? |
| 3D space | 4 % | is the geography of the place kept? |

Give up from the bottom when they conflict: emotion and story are almost never worth trading for continuity.

## Where the cut falls

- **On the word.** The named thing is on screen as it is said — the mango on "mango". In a world film the shot list
  cues it (`cue: [line, 'words']`, `director`, `shots.md`); in the studio, cut 0.1–0.15 s before the word.
- **On the thought.** A blink marks a completed thought; cut a frame or two before it and the cut lands where the
  viewer's mind pauses. Cut after it only for a double-take (Murch).
- **At the moment of inevitability.** Leave once the action is committed — the plane "when the wheels are up", a
  person just before the door. A mechanical overlap of the same movement stutters; a decisive frame sticks (Murch).
- **Enter late, leave early.** Start a scene mid-beat, leave it as soon as it turns; cut the shoe leather (routine
  travel) unless the tension lives in it. Let two or three lines play on one face rather than cutting on every line
  (Kirk Baxter).
- **Let it play.** Stay on a shot and leave the thinking to the audience; tension builds from what hasn't happened yet
  (Joe Walker on *Arrival*).

## Meaning between the shots

- **Cut to the reaction.** Kuleshov's neutral face read as hunger, grief or desire by the next shot; Hitchcock's squint
  turned kind or lecherous with one cutaway. Anne V. Coates cut for performance over action; Schoonmaker played
  *Goodfellas*' menace on the bystanders' faces.
- **Ours:** a neutral iPhone face cut against a rendered world reads as awe, dread or longing — by the world shot and by
  the sound. Film plenty of silent looking and listening shots: they are the most reusable reactions (`director`,
  `staging.md`, "the Kuleshov triad").

## Point of view and geography

- **Whose eyes are we in?** Hamilton's governing question for every sequence.
- **The shorter the shots, the stricter the geography:** eyelines and the 180° line must hold when shots are fast, or
  the action turns illegible (David Bordwell).

## Motion across the cut

- Moves run at an even speed and never start or stop on screen (`cinematographer`, `movement.md`): cut mid-move, once
  it is committed.
- **Hand the motion over:** the next shot moves in a related direction, or answers on purpose (a pull-back after a
  push-in, on a big turn). Keep the direction of travel unless you want a collision (Pearlman's trajectory phrasing).

## The real/virtual seam

- Put the rendered subject where the eye was at the end of the iPhone shot (eye-trace).
- Render handles before and after every world move, so the cut point can slide.
- Render two or three variants (lens, speed) of a world shot that matters, so you are still choosing, not just
  placing.
- Cut voice and music first, then render the world shots to fit them — the pictures follow the sound here.
- Check the 16:9 and the 9:16 cut separately: in 9:16 choose the framing where the eyes read; a vista needs a longer
  hold or a new framing.
- **Keep the bumps:** iPhone imperfection is authenticity; Schoonmaker keeps roughness for reality.
