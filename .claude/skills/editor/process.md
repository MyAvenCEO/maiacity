# From footage to a locked edit

## The steps

1. **Watch everything once, fresh,** without stopping. Log timestamped reactions before any analysis: the first
   viewing is the closest the editor gets to an audience (Murch). Mark why each shot exists; flag the good ones
   (Hamilton).
2. **Selects and string-outs.** Break the material into beats with all takes side by side (Baxter); sort by activity.
3. **The paper edit.** Cut from transcripts first, as documentaries do. Ours is the A1 voice: its takes and word timings
   are the paper edit, and the captions come from them.
4. **The assembly:** everything, in order, to the end, with no polish (Hamilton: lay shots down however rough until the
   sequence exists).
5. **The rough cut: fix the structure.** Reorder, remove, find the spine (`storyteller`, `scenes.md`). Walker rebuilt
   *Arrival*'s nightmare from only the pieces it needed.
6. **The fine cut:** frames, trajectories, reactions, sound bridges (`cutting.md`, `transitions.md`).
7. **Screen it, even unfinished.** *Star Wars* screened with war-film dogfights as placeholders; *Annie Hall*'s closing
   narration was recorded two hours before a test screening. Ours: the storyboard timeline in the studio, full screen,
   then the cut on a phone (9:16) and once on a TV (16:9).
8. **Lock, then grade and mix.** **🔒 Lock the edit** in the studio; unlocking is version n+1 (`story-producer`,
   `pipeline.md`). *Top Gun: Maverick*'s emotion fully arrived only in its seven-week mix — the mix is part of the cut's
   feeling (`sound-designer`).

## How much comes out

| Film | First version | What was cut |
|---|---|---|
| *Annie Hall* | a 140-minute first cut, 93 minutes final | about a third — it became a love story once the relationship was the spine |
| *The Talented Mr. Ripley* | a 4.5-hour assembly, about 2 hours final | about 45 % |
| *Top Gun: Maverick*, the first dogfight | 15 → 12 → 6 → 4:50 minutes | about two thirds |
| *The Conversation* | — | about a third of its scenes |
| *Star Wars* | — | the Anchorhead scenes, which front-loaded too many threads |

Plan for an assembly to lose a third to two thirds of itself (an inference from these films, not a published rule).

## Fresh eyes

- **Rebuild the ignorance you lost on set.** Samuel shot the footage himself: sleep before the first viewing, watch once
  without stopping, and show every cut to one person who wasn't there (Murch stays off set for this reason).
- **When stuck, walk away.** Answers come after sleep (Murch); by the end of a film the editor can't see it any more
  (Paul Hirsch).
- **Judge on the real screen.** Pick a shot by whether the eyes read; a small monitor pushes you closer (Murch). A chase
  that looked fine on a computer raced by when projected (Michael Bay had to "de-cut" it).
- **Every upload is a test screening:** do the first 30 s pay off the title, where does the retention graph dip? Carry
  it into tomorrow's cut.

## Mistakes the pros avoid

- Cutting for its own sake (Walker); polishing before the assembly exists (Hamilton).
- Mechanical action matches (Murch); continuity over performance (Schoonmaker, Murch).
- Front-loading threads and exposition (*Star Wars*, *Annie Hall*'s first twenty minutes).
- Shoe leather (Baxter); temp-music love (Walker); powering through when stuck (Murch).
- Illegible action geography (Bordwell, Hamilton).
- Devices as decoration: freeze frames, montages and dissolves became parodies when used without meaning.
- Overstimulation: loud effects and zero pauses (`pacing.md`).
